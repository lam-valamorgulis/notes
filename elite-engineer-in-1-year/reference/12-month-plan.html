Reference · The contract

The 12-month plan

Front-end taken down to the metal, then AI. Twelve milestones, one month each. Every month has a thing you build from a blank page, a thing you ship to real users, a gate you can fail, and a post you publish.

How to read this page: each month has six lines. Learn is the knowledge. Build is the thing you write from a blank page. Ship is software real people use. Prove is the interview-grade performance. Teach is the public post. Gate is pass or fail — and it names the way you fail, so you cannot lie to yourself.

Start date: Monday 10 August 2026. Finish date: Friday 30 July 2027. 8 hours a day, 6 days a week, about 2,300 hours. The dates are fixed. The scope inside a month flexes. You never move a deadline; you cut scope and say what you cut.

Why the plan is shaped like this

There are a hundred thousand engineers who can wire up a React component. There are very few who have written a layout engine, a reconciler, a bundler, and an autograd engine. This plan moves you from the first group into the second — because that is the only version of "front-end plus AI" that is hard to replace.

The eight rules

  1. No tutorial without a build. Watching is not learning. If you did not type it from a blank page, with the tutorial closed, you do not know it.
  2. Nothing counts until it is evidence. A benchmark, a test, a trace, a public URL, a recording. "I understood it" is not a unit of progress.
  3. Delete before you add. Every month you delete something: code, a dependency, a step in a user flow, a feature nobody used. The best engineers subtract.
  4. Three minutes, plain words, or you do not know it. If you cannot explain it to a non-engineer in three minutes, you learned the words and not the thing.
  5. Go one level down. For every claim, ask how you know it is true. Keep going down until you reach something you measured yourself. That is first principles, and it is the whole job.
  6. Field numbers beat lab numbers. A Lighthouse score is a hint. Real users on real phones are the truth. Every performance claim this year needs field data.
  7. One rest day, every week. Not a reward — a requirement. A tired engineer ships bugs and calls it bad luck.
  8. The gate is judged, not self-assessed. In Months 4, 6, 9, and 12 an outside senior engineer decides whether you passed. You do not grade your own exam.

The shape of every month

WeekWhat you doWhat exists at the end of the week
Week 1
Acquire		 Read the one primary source for the month. Notes in your own words only. Build the smallest toy version to feel the shape of the thing. Notes you wrote, a toy that runs, and a written list of the five things you still do not understand.
Week 2
Build		 Close every tab. Build the month's thing from a blank page. When stuck, write the question down first, then look it up, then write the answer in your own words. The build, with tests, a benchmark, and a README explaining how it works.
Week 3
Ship & break		 Put something in front of real users. Then attack it: throttle the CPU, kill the network, use it with a keyboard only, use it on a cheap Android phone. A public URL someone else uses, plus a list of the failures you caused and fixed.
Week 4
Prove & teach		 Timed drills, spoken explanations, the interview gate. Then the public post and the learning record. Then the retro: what to keep, what to cut. A passed (or failed) gate, a published post, and a learning record in learning-records/.

Stage 1 · Months 1–3 · The machine under the browser

Goal: stop guessing about the platform. By November you can explain — and partly rebuild — the machine your React code runs on.

Month 1 · 10 Aug – 6 Sep 2026

What a program is, and what a browser tab really is

Learn Process, thread, virtual memory, page fault, stack versus heap, syscall, file descriptor, scheduler, cache lines. Then Chrome's real shape: the browser process, one renderer process per site, the GPU process, site isolation — and inside a renderer, the main thread, the compositor thread, and raster threads. What "the main thread is blocked" physically means.

Build (a) A small Unix shell in Python with fork, execvp and dup2 — real pipes and redirection, so processes stop being an abstraction. (b) A page-cost CLI in TypeScript: drive Chrome over the DevTools Protocol and report a page's real cost — process count, main-thread blocking time, long tasks, JS heap size — as a table.

Ship That CLI, published to npm with a README and a version number, used by at least three people who are not you.

Prove Whiteboard the path from pressing Enter on a URL to pixels on screen, naming which process and which thread does each part. Explain a page fault, a context switch, and why one long task destroys responsiveness.

Teach Post: "A browser tab is four processes and one very busy thread".

Sources cpu.land · Chromium multi-process architecture · Inside look at modern web browsers · DevTools Performance docs · man 2 fork

Gate — Month 1 Your shell runs cat file | grep x | wc -l with real pipes and you explain every fork and dup2 from memory. Your CLI's main-thread blocking number lands within 10% of the DevTools number on three real sites, and you can explain both numbers.
You fail if you cannot say which process runs your JavaScript, or which thread scrolls the page.

Month 2 · 7 Sep – 4 Oct 2026

The JavaScript engine and the event loop

Learn How V8 runs your code: parse, bytecode, the optimising compiler, hidden classes, inline caches, deoptimisation. Garbage collection: young and old generation, minor and major collections, what keeps an object alive, what a leak looks like in a heap snapshot. Then the loop: task queue versus microtask queue, queueMicrotask, requestAnimationFrame, requestIdleCallback, rendering opportunities, starvation.

Build (a) A working programming language in TypeScript — scanner, parser, resolver, and a tree-walking interpreter with closures and classes. This is what makes closures stop being magic. (b) An event-loop simulator: your own task and microtask queues plus a frame callback, which reproduces the classic ordering puzzles — and you check its output against the real browser, case by case.

Ship A small library on npm that real users run: a long-task tracker, or a leak detector that samples the heap and reports growth.

Prove Explain a stale closure bug at the level of environments and heap references, and say what the garbage collector does with the captured variable. Predict the output of six hard ordering puzzles cold. Read a heap snapshot and name the leak. Explain one deoptimisation from a real trace.

Teach Post: "A closure is a pointer to an environment — proved with my own interpreter".

Sources Crafting Interpreters · V8 blog and V8 docs · ECMAScript spec (lexical environments, jobs) · Tasks, microtasks, queues and schedules · MDN: JavaScript execution model

Gate — Month 2 Your interpreter runs recursive Fibonacci and a closure counter correctly, your simulator matches the real browser on every puzzle, and you find a real leak in a real heap snapshot.
You fail if you cannot say where a captured variable lives, or if you skipped the resolver because "it worked without it".

Month 3 · 5 Oct – 1 Nov 2026

The rendering pipeline — write a layout engine

Learn HTML parsing into a DOM, CSS parsing into rules, the cascade and style resolution, layout (block, inline, text wrapping, flex), paint, raster, composite. Which stages a given change re-runs. Why transform and opacity can skip layout entirely. Layers, will-change, contain, content-visibility, forced synchronous layout, and what the compositor can animate without the main thread.

Build A mini browser engine in TypeScript: HTML parser → style tree with a real cascade → block layout with text wrapping → one flex row → paint to a canvas. It must render a small target page correctly, compared against a screenshot.

Ship A real page or component where you removed layout thrashing or a forced reflow, with a measured before and after in the field.

Prove Given any CSS change, say which pipeline stages re-run and why. Explain why animating top is expensive and transform is not. Read a performance trace and name the cause of the long task, not just its label.

Teach Post: "I wrote a layout engine and stopped guessing about reflow".

Sources Web Browser Engineering (the book to follow) · Matt Brubeck's toy layout engine series and robinson · RenderingNG · MDN: how browsers work · HTML parsing spec · CSS Flexbox spec

Gate — Month 3 Your engine renders the target page correctly, including text that wraps, and you trace one style change through every pipeline stage by hand on paper.
You fail if your layout only handles absolute positioning, or you skipped text wrapping because it was hard. Text wrapping is the hard part.

Stage 2 · Months 4–6 · The framework layer

Goal: rebuild the tools you use every day. After this stage no framework is a black box to you — and no framework question can corner you.

Month 4 · 2 Nov – 29 Nov 2026

Write a mini React: reconciler, hooks, scheduler

Learn UI = f(state). The element tree, the fiber tree, reconciliation, keys and why index keys lose state, the render phase versus the commit phase, hooks as a linked list on the fiber, why hook order is a rule and not a style, batching, effect timing, refs, context. Then concurrent rendering: time slicing, interruption, priorities, and what it genuinely cannot fix.

Build Your own React in TypeScript, from a blank page: createElement, a fiber tree, reconciliation with keys, a commit phase, then useState, useEffect, useMemo, useRef and useContext — then a scheduler with time slicing and two priority levels. Test it by rendering the same ten components as real React and comparing the result.

Ship A real product's re-render problem fixed, measured with the React Profiler: fewer renders, lower interaction latency, in the field.

Prove Explain the hook order rule from the data structure, not from the documentation. Explain a stale closure inside useEffect and three ways to fix it, with the trade-off of each. Live-code useState in 45 minutes.

Teach Post: "Hooks are a linked list — here is my 300-line React".

Sources React fiber architecture · Build your own React · react.dev · the React source itself

Gate — Month 4 · plus the first outside judge Your React updates a keyed list without losing component state, your hooks work in a real component, and a long render can be interrupted. You can draw the fiber tree for a five-component app from memory. Then: your first mock interview with an outside senior engineer.
You fail if you diff by index, or if state jumps to the wrong row when the list reorders.

Month 5 · 30 Nov – 27 Dec 2026

The build layer: modules, bundling, transforms

Learn ES modules versus CommonJS, the module graph, resolution rules, abstract syntax trees and transforms, tree shaking and why side effects defeat it, code splitting, source maps, content hashing and long-term caching, a dev server and how hot module replacement actually swaps a module, TypeScript compiling versus type checking, bundle budgets.

Build A mini bundler in TypeScript: entry file → parse → module graph → resolve → transform → emit one file with a small runtime. Then add code splitting, then a real source map, then a dev server with hot module replacement over a WebSocket.

Ship A real repository's bundle reduced, with before and after kilobytes and a field metric — plus the budget wired into CI so it cannot regress again.

Prove Explain what tree shaking cannot remove, and why. Explain a source map's mappings field well enough to decode one by hand. Diagnose a duplicated dependency from a bundle report and say what to do about it.

Teach Post: "A bundler is a graph walk and a string join — my 400-line version".

Sources minipack (the smallest honest example) · Rollup docs (tree shaking, side effects) · esbuild docs · Vite: why (dev server and HMR) · Source map specification

Gate — Month 5 Your bundler builds a three-module app, its source map maps a real error back to the right line, hot module replacement updates without a full reload, and the CI budget blocks a deliberate 50 kB regression.
You fail if an existing bundler appears anywhere in your pipeline.

Month 6 · 28 Dec 2026 – 24 Jan 2027

The network, from the socket to first paint

Learn DNS, TCP, TLS, HTTP/1.1 framing, HTTP/2 multiplexing, HTTP/3, head-of-line blocking, request priorities. Caching properly: Cache-Control, ETag, stale-while-revalidate, CDN layers. preload, preconnect, prefetch. Compression. Then rendering strategies: client rendering, server rendering, streaming HTML, React Server Components, and the real cost of hydration.

Build An HTTP/1.1 server on a raw TCP socket with Node net: request parsing, keep-alive, chunked responses, correct 400s, timeouts, graceful shutdown. Then stream HTML out of it progressively and measure first paint against the buffered version. Then a WebSocket handshake and frame parser, by hand.

Ship A real page moved to streaming or server rendering, or its caching fixed, with field numbers for real users.

Prove Draw the full path from URL to pixels with the timeout and the failure mode at every hop. Explain why hydration is expensive and what reduces it. Explain the three caches a response can come from, and which one lied to you.

Teach Post: "I streamed HTML from a raw socket and watched first paint drop".

Sources High Performance Browser Networking · RFC 9112 and RFC 9110 · Beej's Guide · Node net docs · React Server Components

Gate — Month 6 · plus the second outside judge One thousand concurrent keep-alive connections with no leaked file descriptors, and your streamed page beats the buffered page on first paint by a measured margin you can explain. Then: an outside senior engineer runs a front-end system design interview on you.
You fail if a framework appears in your request path, or if your streaming "improvement" has no number attached.

Stage 3 · Months 7–9 · The product

Goal: turn that depth into things users feel. Depth with no taste produces clever software nobody wants.

Month 7 · 25 Jan – 21 Feb 2027

Performance as an engineering discipline

Learn Lab versus field. LCP, INP and CLS, and what each one actually measures. INP broken into input delay, processing, and presentation. The 16.7 millisecond frame budget. Long tasks and yielding. Images and fonts. Virtualised lists. content-visibility. Web workers. Real-user monitoring. Budgets in CI. And a method for finding cost, instead of a list of tips.

Build A real campaign on a real site: five rounds of measure → predict → change → verify, each written down, including the rounds that made it worse. Plus a virtualised list from scratch, and one heavy computation moved into a worker. Plus a CI gate that fails a pull request on a budget breach.

Ship A measurable field improvement on at least two vitals for real users — not a lab score.

Prove Given a trace you have never seen, find the top three costs and rank the fixes by effort against impact. Defend the ranking when I push back.

Teach Post: "Five rounds of performance work with the numbers — including the two that failed".

Sources Core Web Vitals · INP and optimising INP · DevTools Performance · Lighthouse CI · Systems Performance (for the method)

Gate — Month 7 Two vitals improved in the field for real users, and a CI gate that blocks a deliberate regression you introduce to test it.
You fail if your evidence is a Lighthouse score, or the sentence "it feels faster".

Month 8 · 22 Feb – 21 Mar 2027

Taste, accessibility, and deletion

Learn Perceived speed versus measured speed. Every state designed: empty, loading, partial, error, offline. Motion with a purpose. One decision per screen. Design tokens and a real design system. WCAG 2.2 and the ARIA authoring patterns. Keyboard and screen-reader correctness as correctness, not kindness. Forms and error messages. And Ousterhout's rules for the code behind it: deep modules, shallow interfaces.

Build Rebuild one surface you own to a fanatical standard: delete a third of the code and one step of the user's flow. Then build two accessible primitives from scratch to the ARIA pattern — a combobox and a modal dialog — with keyboard tests and a real screen-reader pass.

Ship To real users, with task success measured before and after.

Prove A design defence: I attack every pixel and every abstraction; you justify it or change it. Then a code review of someone else's pull request, written to Google's standard, with severities.

Teach Post: "I deleted 40% of this feature and the numbers went up".

Sources ARIA Authoring Practices · WCAG 2.2 quick reference · A Philosophy of Software Design · Google code review standard · Fowler on architecture

Gate — Month 8 One user-facing metric measurably improved, zero accessibility failures on that flow (automated check, keyboard-only pass, and a screen reader), and fewer lines of code than you started with.
You fail if it looks nicer and nothing was measured. Pretty with no number is decoration.

Month 9 · 22 Mar – 18 Apr 2027

State, offline, real-time — and browser security

Learn Server state versus client state. Cache layers, stale-while-revalidate, optimistic updates and rollback. IndexedDB, Service Worker strategies, background sync, queued writes, conflict resolution and its failure cases. Real-time over WebSocket or server-sent events, with reconnection and replay. Then security in the browser: XSS and why escaping alone is not enough, Content Security Policy and Trusted Types, CSRF and SameSite cookies, sessions versus tokens, OAuth in a browser, clickjacking, the npm supply chain, and what must never reach the client.

Build (a) An offline-first feature: local store, write queue, sync, conflict handling — proved under a flaky-network simulator you wrote yourself. (b) Attack your own application: write a working XSS, a CSRF, a token leak, and a replayed request. Fix each one, add the regression test, and add a Content Security Policy that holds.

Ship The offline feature to real users, plus the hardened authentication or payment path.

Prove A security review of a real codebase with CRITICAL / HIGH / MEDIUM / LOW severities and evidence for each. Explain what a Content Security Policy cannot stop. Explain your conflict rule and name the case where it loses data.

Teach Post: "I broke my own app four ways in one afternoon".

Sources OWASP XSS prevention · OWASP Top 10 · MDN: CSP · SameSite cookies · Service Worker API · IndexedDB API · TanStack Query (cache semantics done well)

Gate — Month 9 · plus the third outside judge Your offline feature loses no writes under the flaky-network simulator, and every exploit you wrote has a test that failed before the fix and passes after. Then: an outside senior engineer runs a deep dive and a code review on you.
You fail if you found no vulnerabilities. Finding nothing means you did not attack it.

Stage 4 · Months 10–12 · AI

Goal: be the engineer who builds AI products, not the person who writes prompts. Same standard of proof as the browser months.

Month 10 · 19 Apr – 16 May 2027

Neural nets, built by hand

Learn The chain rule as code. Backpropagation. Tensors and broadcasting. Loss functions and optimisers. Overfitting and underfitting. Tokenization, embeddings, attention, the transformer block, the training loop, and how behaviour changes with scale. Enough Python to be genuinely fluent, not merely able to read it.

Build An autograd engine from scratch, yourself, about 150 lines. Then a character-level language model. Then a small GPT in PyTorch trained on text you chose. Write it, break it, fix it — do not run someone's notebook.

Ship Something real people use that a model powers, however small: a classifier, a search reranker, a writing tool.

Prove Derive backpropagation on paper for a two-layer network. Explain attention in three minutes with a hand-drawn diagram. Explain why your loss stopped improving, with evidence.

Teach Post: "Backpropagation is one rule applied twelve times — here is my 150-line engine".

Sources Neural Networks: Zero to Hero · micrograd · nanoGPT · The Annotated Transformer · Attention Is All You Need · PyTorch tutorials · Fluent Python

Gate — Month 10 Your GPT trains from code you wrote and produces text clearly better than random, and you can name every tensor shape in the forward pass from memory.
You fail if you ran someone else's notebook and watched the loss go down.

Month 11 · 17 May – 13 Jun 2027

LLM systems and the interfaces on top of them

Learn The raw API loop. Tool calling from first principles. When a fixed workflow beats an agent. Context engineering. Retrieval done properly: chunking, hybrid search, reranking. Evaluation: offline sets, model-as-judge and how it fools you, regression gates in CI. Cost and latency budgets, caching, guardrails, partial failure. Then the interface half, which is your edge: streaming over server-sent events, token-by-token rendering, cancellation mid-stream, interruptible and optimistic UI, showing uncertainty honestly, and on-device inference in the browser — including when it beats a server call.

Build (a) An agent from raw HTTP calls, no framework: tools, retries, cancellation, streaming, and a cost meter. (b) A streaming chat interface you wrote yourself over server-sent events: token rendering, abort, resume, error recovery, and no layout shift. (c) An evaluation suite that runs in CI and blocks a deploy on regression. (d) An MCP server exposing your own tools.

Ship An AI feature to real users, with evaluations gating deploys, a cost dashboard, and a fallback for when the model is wrong or down.

Prove Design an LLM system under time pressure with the cost and latency arithmetic on the board. Defend why part of your design is deliberately not an agent. Name your eval set's blind spots before I find them.

Teach Post: "My agent worked in the demo and failed in production — the eval suite that fixed it".

Sources Building effective agents · Anthropic Cookbook · MCP docs · OpenAI Evals · DSPy · AI Engineering — Chip Huyen · MDN: server-sent events · AI SDK docs (streaming UI patterns) · Chrome built-in AI

Gate — Month 11 Your eval suite catches a regression you plant on purpose. Your streaming interface cancels mid-stream cleanly, with no leaked request and no layout shift. You can state cost per request and 95th-percentile latency from memory.
You fail if you shipped without evaluations. Vibes are not a test suite.

Month 12 · 14 Jun – 30 Jul 2027

Zero to one: an AI product where the interface is the hard part

Learn Nothing new. This month you deliver.

Build Find a real problem a real person has, where the difficulty is the interface and not the model call. Four weeks. Design it, build it, ship it, operate it, support it. Fixed date, flexible scope. Say no to everything that is not the core. Every layer is yours now — the engine, the pipeline, the framework, the network, the model. Use them.

Ship Real users, a public URL, monitoring, evaluations in CI, on-call (you), and a price if it deserves one.

Prove Interview week: two system designs (one front-end heavy, one AI heavy), two timed coding sessions, one deep dive on your own work, one code review, and one "tell me about a failure you owned". Judged by an outside senior engineer.

Teach Publish the year: twelve posts collected in one place, with the numbers, including what failed.

Sources Your own twelve months of notes. If you need a new book in Month 12, the first eleven months did not work.

Gate — Month 12 · the final judge It is live, someone who is not you uses it, and you can explain every layer of it — from the React render down to the syscall — under questioning.
You fail if it is a demo instead of a product, or if the only person who has ever used it is you.

What this plan deliberately leaves out

You cannot have everything in twelve months. These are cut on purpose. Each one is a read about it, do not build it item:

CutWhy, and what you do instead
A storage engine and a database-internals month You still learn to read a query plan and choose an index when it comes up. You do not write a write-ahead log this year. If you later go backend-heavy, this is the first thing to add.
Distributed consensus (Raft, replication) Replaced by the parts a front-end engineer actually meets: retries, idempotency, offline sync, and conflict resolution in Month 9. Read the Raft paper; do not implement it.
Server operations and running a fleet Replaced by real-user monitoring, CI budgets, and error tracking in Months 7 and 12. You monitor what you ship; you do not run infrastructure.
Training or fine-tuning large models You build a small one by hand in Month 10 to understand it. Production AI work here is systems and interface work, not training runs.
A third language TypeScript and Python only, all year. The C you read in Months 1 and 3 is for understanding, not for writing.

When you fail a month

You will fail one. Probably Month 3 or Month 5. The procedure is not negotiable:

  1. Say it out loud, in writing, the same day. Which part of the gate failed, and what evidence is missing. Hiding a failure is the only unrecoverable mistake here.
  2. Re-run the gate in Week 1 of the next month. Not the whole month — only the gate. You keep moving, and the debt gets paid immediately.
  3. Two failures in a row means the plan is wrong, not you. Then we recut it: fewer hours, longer months, or a different order. Effort is not the variable to increase.
  4. A learning record for every failure. What you believed, what was actually true, and what it predicts you will get wrong next.
The trap that ends most twelve-month plans Months 1 and 2 feel great, because reading feels like progress. Month 3 is the wall: text wrapping in a layout engine is genuinely hard, nothing looks right for days, and no post gets published. That is not a sign the plan is wrong. That is the first month the plan is working. Get through Month 3 and Month 5 and the rest of the year is downhill.
Remember this
  • Four stages: under the browser (1–3), the framework layer (4–6), the product (7–9), AI (10–12).
  • You rebuild your own tools: an interpreter, a layout engine, a React, a bundler, an HTTP server, an autograd engine, an agent.
  • Every month has the same four proofs: build from a blank page, ship to real users, pass a spoken gate, publish a post.
  • Field numbers beat lab numbers. A Lighthouse score is never the evidence.
  • Outside judges in Months 4, 6, 9 and 12. You never grade your own exam.
  • Fixed dates, flexible scope. Cut scope out loud; never move the date.
  • Failing a month is normal — re-run only the gate, in Week 1 of the next month.

If you remember one thing: a month only counts when someone who is not you can see the evidence.

Stuck, disagreeing, or think a month is in the wrong order? Say so. I would rather argue with you in Week 1 than watch you fail a gate in Week 4 politely.
