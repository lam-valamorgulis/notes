DNS, TCP and TLS — what a request costs in round trips

Chapter 3 starts where Chapter 2's first bar ended. Before a single byte of your CSS can arrive, the browser has a conversation — and on a phone that conversation is measured in hundreds of milliseconds.

core Domain expertise: moves ability #1 (explain from first principles) and ability #3 (name the trade) — this is the lesson that makes "just add a CDN" or "just add another app" into a number.

The win: after ~35 minutes you can count the round trips a new origin costs, explain why a faster connection does not fix a slow site, and price the fourteenth third-party script in a theme without measuring anything.

1. The unit of cost is a round trip, not a kilobyte

A request is a conversation across physical distance. The smallest possible exchange — say something, hear an answer — is one round-trip time, one RTT. Nothing can be faster than that, because the signal has a speed limit. Typical numbers: 10–30 ms on good wifi to a nearby server, 100–200 ms on mobile, more on a poor connection or a distant origin.

Now count what must happen before your CSS file's first byte, for a domain the browser has never spoken to:

StepWhat it asksCost
DNS"what is the IP address for this name?"~1 RTT (often 0, if cached by the OS or the resolver)
TCP handshakeSYN → SYN-ACK → ACK: "can we talk?" "yes" "good"1 RTT before data may be sent
TLS handshakeagree on keys and verify the certificate1 RTT on TLS 1.3, 2 RTTs on TLS 1.2
The request itselfGET the file, wait for the server to answer1 RTT, plus the server's own thinking time

So a brand-new origin costs roughly 3 RTTs before your request is even sent, and about 4 before the first byte arrives. At 150 ms RTT that is around 600 ms of doing nothing at all — no bytes, no rendering, no progress. It is the same 600 ms whether the file is 2 KB or 200 KB.

2. Two consequences that surprise people

Bandwidth does not fix latency

Doubling a customer's connection speed does nothing to the four blocks above; they are governed by distance and the speed of light, not by capacity. This is the central argument of High Performance Browser Networking: for typical web pages, latency is the bottleneck, not bandwidth. It is also why testing on office fibre hides almost every network problem your customers have — throttle, or you are not testing.

TCP starts slowly, on purpose

A new connection does not send at full speed. TCP slow start begins with a small congestion window — roughly ten packets, about 14 KB — and doubles it each round trip until it sees loss. So a 100 KB stylesheet on a fresh connection is not one transfer; it is several round trips of increasing size (HPBN — Building blocks of TCP).

That is where the old "keep critical CSS under 14 KB" rule came from, and it is the honest reason inlining the first screen's CSS works so well: those bytes ride along in the HTML's very first window, with no extra round trip at all.

Trap — every third-party domain is a fresh bill A reviews app, a chat widget, a font host, an analytics endpoint and a pixel are five separate origins, so five DNS lookups, five TCP handshakes and five TLS handshakes. HTTP/2 multiplexing does not help across origins — it reuses one connection per origin. A theme with fourteen third-party origins has bought fourteen handshakes on a 150 ms network, and no amount of minifying your own JavaScript touches that.

4. Explain it back Feynman check

Part 1 · Plain words — no jargon at all

Getting a file from a server is like phoning someone far away. Before you can ask your question you have to look up the number, wait for them to pick up, and agree you are both who you say you are. Each of those steps is one there and back, and each there-and-back takes real time — on a phone network, maybe a sixth of a second. Do that with five different companies and you have spent most of a second before anyone has answered a single question. Calling the same person again is much faster, because the line is already open.

Part 2 · One analogy, and where the analogy breaks

The analogy. A phone call. Look up the number (DNS), the line connects (TCP), you confirm identities (TLS), then you ask (the request). A wider phone line does not make the ringing shorter.

Where it breaks. On a phone, once connected you can talk as fast as you like. A new network connection starts deliberately quiet and gets louder over several exchanges, because it does not yet know how much the route can carry. So even after the greeting, the first pages of your answer arrive in small batches with waits in between — which is why a small first payload arrives disproportionately sooner than a large one.

Part 3 · The sticking point

People measure size and ignore count. Two 50 KB files on one origin are much cheaper than two 5 KB files on two new origins, and that ordering feels wrong until you count round trips. The second sticking point: believing HTTP/2 made origins free. HTTP/2 multiplexes requests over one connection per origin — it removed the need to shard assets across domains, and it did nothing about the setup cost of a domain you had no reason to talk to. The third: testing on fast wifi and concluding the site is fine, when the whole of this lesson is invisible above about 30 ms RTT.

Part 4 · Teach it back to me

Two or three sentences to a merchant who wants to install a fifth app that adds a script from a new domain. Explain what it costs before it does anything useful, in everyday words, and give them the decision rather than a refusal. Then send it to me — the skill is quantifying it honestly ("about half a second on a phone, before the app does any work") without exaggerating.

5. Point at it: the Timing tab is this lesson gate #3

  1. See the blocks. DevTools → Network, throttle to Slow 4G, reload with the cache disabled. Click any request from a third-party domain → Timing tab. You will see the exact rows from section 1: DNS Lookup, Initial connection, SSL, Request sent, Waiting (TTFB), Content Download. Read the millisecond values and add up the first three.
  2. Compare a reused connection. Now click a later request to the same domain. DNS, connection and SSL are gone — the connection was reused. Those two screenshots side by side are gate #3 for this core.
  3. Count the origins on a real storefront. In the Console: 
    const origins = new Set(performance.getEntriesByType('resource').map(r => new URL(r.name).origin));
console.log(origins.size, [...origins]);
    Multiply origins.size - 1 by three round trips, times a realistic mobile RTT. That single number is often the most persuasive thing in a performance report.
  4. Check the protocol too. Right-click the Network table header → enable the Protocol column. h2 or h3 means requests to that origin share one connection; http/1.1 means the browser is limited to about six parallel connections per origin, each with its own handshake.
Work drill — do this on a real theme

A storefront on Slow 4G shows a 2.9 s LCP. The Network panel lists 41 requests across 12 distinct origins: the shop's own CDN, a font host, two review apps, a chat widget, an analytics endpoint, a pixel, a currency converter, and four more. Your own theme's JavaScript and CSS total 90 KB, all from the shop's origin. Total transferred: 1.4 MB.

Before flipping the recall cards: estimate the round-trip cost of the eleven extra origins at 150 ms RTT, and say whether minifying the 90 KB of theme code is a good use of a day. Then the interviewer's follow-up: the merchant will not remove any app. Name three things you can still do, and be honest about which of them actually removes round trips and which only hides them.

6. Live coding session

Type it, predict it, break it

No files needed — this one runs in the Console of any real storefront. Type it by hand:

const rows = performance.getEntriesByType('resource').map(r => ({
  origin: new URL(r.name).origin,
  dns:  Math.round(r.domainLookupEnd - r.domainLookupStart),
  tcp:  Math.round(r.connectEnd - r.connectStart),
  ttfb: Math.round(r.responseStart - r.requestStart),
  kb:   Math.round((r.transferSize || 0) / 1024)
}));
console.table(rows.filter(r => r.dns + r.tcp > 0));   // only new connections show setup cost

Predict before running: how many rows will have a non-zero dns or tcp value — will it be all 41 requests, or roughly one per origin? Run it and see. One per origin, because every later request reused the connection. That table is section 1, measured on the client's own site.

Now measure the total you are paying:

const setup = rows.reduce((n, r) => n + r.dns + r.tcp, 0);
console.log('handshake time spent:', setup, 'ms across',
  new Set(rows.map(r => r.origin)).size, 'origins');

Run it once on fast wifi and once with Slow 4G throttling, and compare. The bytes did not change; the number did. Predict the ratio before you look.

Third round — feel slow start. Make two files locally, one 12 KB and one 300 KB, serve them with python3 -m http.server, throttle to Slow 4G, and time each with the cache disabled. Predict first: the big file is 25× the bytes — will it take 25× the time, more, or less? Then look at the Content Download row in the Timing tab for each. The small file rides the first congestion window; the large one needs several round trips, so the ratio is worse than the byte ratio suggests. That is the mechanism behind "inline the first screen".

The gate — only now touch the tracker Open the tree tracker and mark DNS → TCP → TLS handshake cost in RTTs done only if all three held, from memory: (1) you explained the cost of a new app's domain to a merchant — the teach-back in section 4; (2) you predicted that only one request per origin shows setup cost, before running console.table; (3) you compared the Timing tab of a first and a later request to the same origin. Two out of three is zero out of three.

Remember this
  • The unit of network cost is the round trip (RTT): 10–30 ms on good wifi, 100–200 ms on mobile.
  • A new origin costs DNS + TCP + TLS ≈ 3 RTTs before the request is sent — about 600 ms at 150 ms RTT.
  • That cost is identical for a 2 KB and a 200 KB file. Count requests and origins, not just bytes.
  • A reused connection skips all three: one round trip instead of four.
  • Latency is the bottleneck, not bandwidth. A faster connection does not shorten a handshake.
  • TCP slow start begins at roughly 14 KB and doubles per round trip — which is why a small first payload arrives disproportionately sooner.
  • HTTP/2 reuses one connection per origin. It never makes an extra origin free.
  • Every third-party app domain is a fresh 3-RTT bill, before the app does anything useful.

If you remember one thing: a new domain costs about half a second on a phone before it is any use at all.
