LCP, CLS, INP — definition and measurement

The last core of Chapter 2, and the only one a client will ever quote at you. Three numbers that turn "the site feels slow" into three separate, fixable engineering problems.

core Domain expertise: moves ability #5 (judge work and say what is wrong) and ability #3 (name the trade) — these metrics are how a merchant's complaint becomes a ticket you can price.

The win: after ~40 minutes you can define each metric precisely, name the four parts LCP breaks into, calculate a CLS score by hand, and say which of Chapter 2's lessons fixes each — plus explain why your green Lighthouse score and the client's red Search Console report are both correct.

1. Why exactly three

A customer's experience of a page has three separate failure modes, and they need three separate numbers because they have three separate causes:

  • Did the main thing appear quickly? → LCP, Largest Contentful Paint. A loading problem.
  • Did it stay still while I read it? → CLS, Cumulative Layout Shift. A stability problem.
  • Did it respond when I touched it? → INP, Interaction to Next Paint. A responsiveness problem (lesson 0007's mechanism, given a score).

Collapsing them into one score would hide the cause. A page can load instantly and jump under your thumb; it can be rock-steady and take 600 ms to react. Per web.dev — Core Web Vitals, each is judged at the 75th percentile of real users — so "good" means three in four visits were good, not the average.

2. LCP splits into four parts — and that is the whole diagnosis

LCP is the render time of the largest contentful element in the viewport: usually a hero image, a video poster, a background image, or a block of text. Chasing "make LCP faster" is hopeless; splitting it is easy (web.dev — LCP):

|----- TTFB -----|-- load delay --|---- load time ----|-- render delay --| request sent HTML arrives image starts image finished LCP (server time) downloading downloading painted fix: hosting, fix: make it fix: fewer bytes, fix: unblock the Liquid, CDN DISCOVERABLE right format, main thread and (no lazy, no right size, the CSS above it JS-built src, priority hint preload if late)

Two of those four are the ones that bite a Shopify theme:

  • Load delay — the browser found the hero image late. Causes: loading="lazy" on the hero (the most common single mistake in this whole lesson), a src built by JavaScript so the preload scanner from lesson 0011 never sees it, or the image hidden inside a slider that initialises after hydration. The hero image should be eagerly loaded and carry fetchpriority="high".
  • Render delay — the bytes arrived and the browser still could not paint, because render-blocking CSS or a long task was in the way. That is lessons 0010 and 0007, showing up in a metric.

3. CLS is a score you can compute by hand

For each unexpected shift: impact fraction × distance fraction. Impact fraction is the share of the viewport covered by the moving content, before and after, combined. Distance fraction is how far it moved, as a share of the viewport's largest dimension. CLS is the largest burst of shifts within a 5-second window (web.dev — CLS).

Worked example, the one every merchant has: an app injects a 100 px announcement bar at the top of an 800 px-tall viewport, pushing everything down.

content occupying the viewport, then pushed down 100px impact fraction = affected area / viewport ≈ 1.0 (the whole screen moved) distance fraction = 100px / 800px = 0.125 layout shift score = 1.0 x 0.125 = 0.125 → already "needs work"

One banner. One shift. Past the good threshold. That is why "we will just inject it at the top" is never a free decision — and the fix is not to remove the banner, it is to reserve its space before it arrives.

Cause of shiftFix
Images with no dimensionswidth and height attributes, or aspect-ratio in CSS
Injected banners, apps, ads, embedsreserve a fixed-height container, or overlay instead of push
Web font swap changing text heightfont-display: optional/swap plus size-adjust, and match the fallback metrics
Animating height/topanimate transform instead — lesson 0013, again
Content inserted above what the user is readinginsert below, or only in response to their own interaction

One relief: a shift within 500 ms of a user interaction is expected and excluded. Opening an accordion is not penalised. Injecting a banner on its own is.

4. Field versus lab — why both reports are right

You run Lighthouse: green. The client sends a Search Console screenshot: red. Nobody is lying.

Lab (Lighthouse, DevTools)Field (CrUX, Search Console, RUM)
Whoyou, once, on your devicereal visitors, 28-day rolling window, 75th percentile
INPnot measurable — no real interactions. Lighthouse reports TBT as a proxymeasured from actual taps
CLSonly shifts during the short synthetic loadthe whole visit, including shifts you never scrolled to
Good fordebugging a change you just madedeciding what to work on at all

The professional habit: field data chooses the work, lab data verifies the fix. Getting that backwards is how teams spend a sprint on a metric no customer was suffering.

5. Explain it back Feynman check

Part 1 · Plain words — no jargon at all

Three separate questions about a page, each with its own score. Did the big thing on the screen show up quickly? Did the page stay still while I was reading it, or did it jump and make me tap the wrong thing? When I tapped, how long before anything visibly happened? Each one is measured on real visitors' phones, and the score has to be good for three visits out of four — so one fast test on a developer's laptop proves nothing.

Part 2 · One analogy, and where the analogy breaks

The analogy. A restaurant review with three separate marks: how long the food took, whether the table wobbled, and how long the waiter took to notice you. Averaging them into one star rating would hide which one to fix.

Where it breaks. A restaurant is judged by its average night. These are judged at the 75th percentile — close to the worst quarter of visits, which means the slow phone on a bad connection counts far more than your test does. And the measurement window keeps moving: a fix takes weeks to show up in the field, because the 28-day window has to refill. There is no "we deployed it, refresh the report".

Part 3 · The sticking point

Three, and each one produces wasted work. First: treating LCP as one thing. It is four — server time, discovery delay, download time, render delay — and until you know which part dominates, any fix is a guess. Second: thinking a green Lighthouse score means the site is fine. Lighthouse cannot measure INP at all, and it only sees shifts during a short synthetic load. Third: believing CLS is about "how much moved". It is about how much of the screen moved, times how far — which is why one small banner at the top can score worse than a large change in a corner.

Part 4 · Teach it back to me

Two or three sentences to a merchant who writes: "Google says our Core Web Vitals fail on mobile but your test says the site is fast — which is it?" Explain both being true, say which one you will act on and why, and set an honest expectation about when the report will change. Then send it to me — the words I am looking for are "real visitors" and "75 out of 100", not "percentile".

6. Point at it: measure all three yourself gate #3

  1. Which element is the LCP? DevTools → Performance → record a reload. On the Timings track, click the LCP marker: the summary names the exact element and links it in the Elements panel. Half the time it is not the element anyone assumed.
  2. See shifts happen. Rendering panel → tick Layout Shift Regions. Reload and watch: every shifting area flashes blue. Then the Experience section of a performance recording lists each shift with its score and the nodes involved.
  3. All three, in the Console, on any page. Paste this and interact for a few seconds: 
    new PerformanceObserver(l => l.getEntries().forEach(e =>
  console.log('LCP', Math.round(e.startTime), e.element)
)).observe({ type: 'largest-contentful-paint', buffered: true });

new PerformanceObserver(l => l.getEntries().forEach(e => {
  if (!e.hadRecentInput) console.log('shift', e.value.toFixed(4), e.sources?.[0]?.node);
})).observe({ type: 'layout-shift', buffered: true });

new PerformanceObserver(l => l.getEntries().forEach(e =>
  console.log('interaction', e.name, Math.round(e.duration) + 'ms')
)).observe({ type: 'event', durationThreshold: 40, buffered: true });
    Three observers, three metrics, no libraries. This is gate #3 for this core — and it is also how you demonstrate a problem to a client in a screen share.
  4. Then check the field. Search Console → Core Web Vitals, or the CrUX data shown in PageSpeed Insights. Compare it with what you just measured and note the gap; the gap is the device and network you are not testing on.
Work drill — do this on a real theme

A client's Search Console reports mobile LCP 4.8 s (poor), CLS 0.19 (needs work), INP 260 ms (needs work). You inspect the theme: the hero image is inside the slider section and carries loading="lazy"; an app injects a 90 px announcement bar after page load on an 800 px viewport; product images have no width/height; and a reviews script runs a 280 ms task during load. Your own Lighthouse run scores 96.

Before flipping the recall cards: assign each observed problem to the metric it damages and to the LCP sub-part where relevant, then order the whole list by value-for-effort. Then the interviewer's follow-up: you ship every fix on Monday and the client emails on Wednesday saying Search Console is unchanged — what do you tell them, and what do you show them instead?

7. Live coding session

Type it, predict it, break it

One file. Type it by hand — you are going to cause a bad CLS on purpose and then measure it:

<!doctype html>
<style>body{margin:0;font:16px system-ui} .row{padding:24px;border-bottom:1px solid #ddd}</style>
<div id="app"></div>
<script>
  app.innerHTML = '<div class="row">product row</div>'.repeat(20);

  new PerformanceObserver(l => l.getEntries().forEach(e => {
    if (!e.hadRecentInput) console.log('shift score', e.value.toFixed(4));
  })).observe({ type: 'layout-shift', buffered: true });

  // the app banner, arriving 1.5s late, pushing everything down
  setTimeout(() => {
    const bar = document.createElement('div');
    bar.style.cssText = 'height:90px;background:#C86A08';
    document.body.prepend(bar);
  }, 1500);
</script>

Predict before running: calculate the shift score by hand first — impact fraction times distance fraction, with your own window height. Then run it and compare your arithmetic with the number the observer prints. Being within a few hundredths means you understand the formula rather than the slogan.

Now fix it two ways and predict the score for each before testing:

  // fix A — reserve the space up front
  document.body.style.paddingTop = '90px';   // then the bar is position:absolute at the top

  // fix B — overlay instead of push
  bar.style.cssText += ';position:fixed;top:0;left:0;right:0';

Both should print nothing at all. Zero shifts, same banner, same pixels of information — the difference is entirely whether existing content moved.

Third round — break LCP on purpose. Add a large hero image and test three versions, predicting the LCP time for each:

  <img src="hero.jpg" width="1200" height="600" loading="lazy">                  // 1
  <img src="hero.jpg" width="1200" height="600">                                 // 2
  <img src="hero.jpg" width="1200" height="600" fetchpriority="high">            // 3

Throttle to Slow 4G and record each. Version 1 is dramatically worse, and the LCP marker will name the image as the element — the single most common real-world LCP bug, reproduced by you in one attribute. Keep the width and height on all three: that is the CLS half, and it costs nothing.

8. Prove it

The gate — only now touch the tracker Open the tree tracker and mark LCP, CLS, INP — definition and measurement done only if all three held, from memory: (1) you explained the lab-versus-field answer to a merchant — the teach-back in section 5; (2) you calculated a shift score by hand before the observer printed it, and predicted the three LCP versions' order; (3) you found the LCP element in a real profile and watched Layout Shift Regions flash. Two out of three is zero out of three. This completes Chapter 2's core — do the weekly review before Chapter 3.

Anything unclear — how the 75th percentile is chosen, why interaction-caused shifts are excluded, which sub-part of LCP your theme is losing? Ask me. I am your teacher; that is what this session is for.

Remember this
  • Three failure modes, three numbers: LCP loading, CLS stability, INP response.
  • Thresholds: LCP ≤ 2.5 s, CLS ≤ 0.1, INP ≤ 200 ms — all at the 75th percentile of real visits.
  • LCP splits into four: TTFB · load delay · load time · render delay. Diagnose the part, never the whole.
  • loading="lazy" on the hero image is the classic LCP bug. Load it eagerly, consider fetchpriority="high".
  • Shift score = impact fraction × distance fraction. One 90 px banner on an 800 px viewport can score 0.11.
  • Shifts within 500 ms of a user interaction are excluded — opening an accordion is fine, injecting a banner is not.
  • Field data chooses the work; lab data verifies the fix. Lighthouse cannot measure INP at all.
  • Field reports lag: the window is 28 days rolling, so a Monday fix is not visible on Wednesday.

If you remember one thing: never optimise a Core Web Vital before you know which sub-part of it is losing.
