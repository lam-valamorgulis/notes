Day 7 · Lesson 21 · The last ten minutes

The questions you ask, and closing

"Do you have any questions for us?" is not politeness. It is the last scored section, and it is the one candidates prepare least.

Your win today: you have six questions written down that only a senior engineer would ask, an answer ready for salary, and a closing sentence you have said out loud.

Why this section is graded

Your questions reveal what you pay attention to. "Is there free coffee?" and "How do you decide what gets built?" describe two different engineers, and the interviewer hears the difference instantly. They are also the last thing said, which is what people remember.

Trap — "No, I think you covered everything" It is the most common answer and it is read as low interest, every time. Even in a 20-minute screening call, have two. If they genuinely covered a question, say so and ask a better one: "You covered the stack, so instead — what does a code review usually look like here?"

Six questions worth asking

AskWhat it signals
"What does the front end look like today — is it one app, or several? Any parts everyone is afraid to touch?"You expect real systems with real history, not a greenfield fantasy.
"How does work reach me? Does it arrive as a designed ticket, or am I in the conversation earlier?"You care about how decisions are made, not just about being assigned tasks.
"What's your testing story on the front end, and are you happy with it?"Almost nobody asks this. Everyone has an opinion about it, and the honest answer tells you a lot about the team.
"With a distributed team, how much overlap do people have, and what's expected to be synchronous?"Directly on the job description. It says you have worked remotely before and know where it breaks.
"What would a successful first three months look like for this person?"The single most useful question in any interview. It gets you the real job description.
"What's the hardest technical problem the front-end team has hit this year?"It turns the interview into a technical conversation between peers — the best way to end.
Then actually listen If the answer names a problem you have solved, say so briefly. "That's interesting — we hit something similar with device changes during a call. What we ended up doing was…" Twenty seconds. Ending the interview with a moment where you were useful is worth more than any answer you gave earlier.

Salary — answer it without flinching

It will come up, usually early, often from a recruiter. Two rules: know your number before the call, and give a range with a reason.

If they ask first

"For a senior front-end role at this scope I'm looking at [X to Y]. That's based on the market for React roles at this level. Where does the band for this role sit?"

If you would rather not go first

"I'd rather understand the scope a bit more before I put a number on it. Do you have a band for the role?" Say it once. If they push, give your range — a second deflection reads as evasive.

Never give a single number: it becomes the ceiling. Never give a range you would be unhappy with at the bottom, because that is where you will land.

The question that tests your composure

"Do you have any concerns about my profile?" is a strong question to ask, and you must be ready for the answer. If they name a gap, do not defend — acknowledge it, then give evidence:

The shape

"That's fair — [X] hasn't been the centre of my day-to-day. What I'd point at is [the closest concrete thing], and it's the area I've deliberately worked on most recently. If it's important for this role, I'd want to know that now rather than later."

That last sentence is the one that lands. It says you want the right outcome, not just an offer.

The last sixty seconds

Most candidates end with "thank you for your time" and leave a flat ending. Close on purpose instead. Three sentences:

  1. Interest, specific. "This is the kind of front end I want to work on — a real product with real complexity, not a landing page."
  2. One reason you fit. "The part I'd bring straight away is experience with React against systems React doesn't control — sockets, media, imperative engines."
  3. The next step. "What are the next steps, and when should I expect to hear?"

Afterwards

  • Write down every question they asked, within an hour, while you remember. This is the most valuable artefact of the whole day — it is next time's study list, whatever the outcome.
  • A short follow-up message, same day: thank you, one specific thing from the conversation, one line of continued interest. Four sentences. A long one reads as anxious.
  • If an answer was wrong, you may correct it in that message — briefly, once. "I said INP measures X; it actually measures Y." It shows you keep thinking. Do not do this more than once.
  • If it does not work out, ask for feedback once, politely, and accept whatever comes. The list of questions you wrote down is worth more than the feedback anyway.

Remember this
  • "Any questions?" is scored, and it is the last thing they remember.
  • Never say "no, you covered everything". Have two, even for a screening call.
  • The most useful question there is: "What would a successful first three months look like?"
  • Hold benefits, hours and promotion questions until later rounds.
  • Salary: a range with a reason, never a single number, never a bottom you would refuse.
  • If they name a concern, acknowledge and give evidence — do not defend.
  • Close with three sentences: interest, fit, next steps.
  • Write down every question they asked, within the hour.

If you remember one thing: end the interview as a conversation between two engineers, not as an exam you survived.
