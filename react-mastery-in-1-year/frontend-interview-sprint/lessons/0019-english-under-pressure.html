Day 6 · Lesson 19 · Working with people

English under pressure

They are not grading your grammar. They are working out whether they could pair with you on a hard problem. Those are very different tests, and the second one you can prepare for.

Your win today: you have twelve rehearsed sentences that keep a conversation moving when your English deserts you — and you have stopped apologising for your English.

What is actually being graded

They do not care aboutThey care a lot about
Article mistakes ("the state" vs "state")Whether you understood the question
Verb tensesWhether you say when you do not understand
Your accentWhether you can be interrupted and recover
Perfect vocabularyWhether the technical words are right

One rule follows from that table: get the technical nouns exactly right and let the grammar be imperfect. "The effect is not clean up when component unmount" is understood perfectly. "The thing that runs after removes the listener" is not.

The twelve sentences

Learn these until they arrive without thinking. Each one buys you time or rescues a moment.

When you did not understand

  1. "Sorry, could you say that again a bit slower?"
  2. "Just to check I understood — you're asking about X, is that right?"
  3. "I'm not familiar with that term. Could you describe what it does?"
Number 2 is the most valuable sentence in this lesson "Just to check I understood — you're asking about X, is that right?" does three things at once: it buys you five seconds, it proves you listen, and it stops you answering the wrong question for two minutes. Senior engineers do this in their native language. Use it two or three times in an interview and it reads as care, not as weakness.

When you need time to think

  1. "Let me think about that for a second."
  2. "There are two ways to look at this. Let me start with the simpler one."
  3. "Can I draw this? It's easier to show than to say."

When you do not know

  1. "I haven't used that. What I do know that's close is…"
  2. "I don't know. My guess would be X, and I'd check it by Y."
  3. "I'd have to look that up rather than guess — I don't want to give you a confident wrong answer."
Why number 9 works so well

Saying "I don't want to give you a confident wrong answer" tells an interviewer exactly what they most want to know: that you will not bluff in a code review or a production incident. Bluffing is the failure they are screening for. Admitting a gap cleanly, then showing how you would close it, scores better than a shaky guess.

When you lose the thread mid-sentence

  1. "Sorry, let me start that again."
  2. "The short version is…" (use this when you feel a sentence collapsing — it forces you to land the point)
  3. "Does that answer your question, or would you like me to go deeper?"

Number 12 is a closing move. It ends your answer cleanly instead of letting it fade out, and it hands control back — which sounds confident, not uncertain.

Slow down — it is the highest-value change

Under stress everyone speeds up, and speed is where a second language breaks: grammar slips, words vanish, sentences run into each other. Speaking more slowly than feels natural fixes several problems at once — you have time to find words, your accent becomes clearer, and pauses read as thoughtfulness rather than as hesitation.

The one habit to practise today

Pause for one full second before you start answering. Every time. It feels enormous and it sounds calm. It also stops the most common interview mistake, which is answering the question you expected instead of the one that was asked.

Sounds worth ten minutes of practice

Vietnamese does not use most final consonant clusters, and final consonants are usually unreleased. English technical vocabulary is full of both. These are the words you will say most often this week, so drill the endings — not the accent, just the endings:

SoundWords you will actually say
final -ps / -ks / -tsprops, hooks, effects, components, requests, events
final -st / -sk / -cttest, list, task, context, object, select
th — a sound Vietnamese does not havethread, think, third, throttle, with
plural -s that changes meaning"the effect runs" vs "the effects run"

Ten minutes reading your own answers out loud, deliberately finishing every ending, is enough to make a noticeable difference in a week. This is not about sounding native. It is that "conteck" and "context" cost the listener a beat, and a few of those in a row is when someone stops following you.

Never apologise for your English

Trap — "Sorry, my English is not very good" Three things go wrong. You have told them to expect problems, so they start noticing them. You have spent your opening seconds on a weakness. And it is usually not true — you are working in English right now. If you genuinely need something, ask for the thing instead of apologising for yourself: "Could you speak a little slower? I want to make sure I answer the right question." That is a request, not an apology, and it makes you sound careful rather than nervous.

Practise the right way

  1. Record yourself answering three questions. Listen back once. It is uncomfortable and it is the fastest correction available.
  2. Shadow: play a five-minute React conference talk, then repeat one sentence at a time, copying the rhythm. Ten minutes a day for a week is enough to change your pacing.
  3. Rehearse the openings only. The first fifteen seconds of an answer is where anxiety lives. Once you are talking, it gets easier — so make the start automatic.
  4. Practise being interrupted. Have someone cut you off mid-sentence with a follow-up. Recovering smoothly is a skill, and interviewers do it constantly.
Drill · 20 minutes, today Record yourself answering these three, out loud, once each, no notes:
  • "Tell me about yourself." (60 seconds)
  • "Explain the difference between state and props." (45 seconds)
  • "Tell me about the hardest thing you've built." (90 seconds)
Listen back and mark three things only: Where did I speed up? Which technical word came out unclear? Did I apologise for my English? Then record the same three again tomorrow morning.

Remember this
  • They grade understanding, not grammar. Get the technical nouns exactly right.
  • "Just to check I understood — you're asking about X?" is the most useful sentence you own.
  • "I don't know, my guess is X and I'd check it by Y" beats a confident wrong answer, always.
  • Pause one full second before every answer. It sounds calm and stops you answering the wrong question.
  • Speak slower than feels natural. Speed is where a second language breaks.
  • Drill final consonants: props, hooks, tests, context, effects.
  • Never apologise for your English. Ask for what you need instead.
  • Close answers with "Does that answer it, or should I go deeper?"

If you remember one thing: a slow, clear, imperfect sentence beats a fast, fluent, confusing one.

Ask: "interview me in English and correct only the things that lost you." Not every mistake — only the ones that broke understanding.
