Day 6 · Lesson 18 · Working with people

Agile & the distributed team

"Experience working with global or distributed teams is highly valued." That sentence is in the job description because remote work fails on communication, not on code.

Your win today: you can answer the three delivery questions — how you work with a backend engineer, what you do when a task grows, and how you handle a nine-hour time difference — with specific habits rather than good intentions.

Agile, minus the vocabulary

Do not recite the ceremonies. Say what each one is for, which is what tells them you have actually lived it.

CeremonyWhat it is forHow it fails
StandupSurfacing blockers early enough to fix them todayIt becomes a status report to a manager. Nobody says they are stuck.

The standup that works when nobody is in a room

Say this — the format, and why

"I put blockers first, not last. In a distributed team a blocker mentioned at the end of a written standup is read eight hours later, and that is a lost day. So: what's blocked, what I finished, what I'm on today. Sixty seconds."

Estimation — the question everyone answers badly

"How long will this take?" is not really a question about time. It is a question about uncertainty, and the strong answer separates the two.

Say this

"I give a range and I say what would move it. 'Two to four days — two if the API returns what we discussed, four if I have to handle pagination and the error states aren't specified.' A single number hides the risk, and the risk is the thing my product manager actually needs to plan around."

SituationWhat to say
You are asked to estimate something vague"I can give you a number after a two-hour spike, or I can give you a rough range now with a wide band. Which is more useful?"
You are already over the estimateSay it the day you know, not on the due date. "This is going to be Thursday, not Tuesday — the API returns nested data and the table needs restructuring. Options are A or B."
Asked to commit to a date you do not believe"I can commit to that scope, or to that date. Which one matters more?" Never agree quietly and then miss it.
Trap — the silent slip The single most damaging habit in a remote team is knowing on Monday that Friday is impossible and saying nothing until Friday. Your teammates cannot see your screen, so silence reads as "on track". Interviewers ask about this specifically because they have been burned by it. Bad news early is a senior behaviour; bad news late is the thing that gets people managed out.

Working with backend engineers

This is the collaboration the job description names first. There is one habit that answers it:

Say this, word for word

"I agree the contract first — the endpoint, the exact response shape, the error shape, the paging. Then I build against a mock of it and they build the real one. That way neither of us is blocked, and if the shape changes we find out in a conversation rather than in integration."

Then add the detail that shows it is real practice, not theory:

  • Write the type first. A TypeScript interface for the response is a contract both sides can read, and it makes disagreements visible before any code exists.
  • Ask about the error shape. Most integrations agree the happy path and discover in QA that errors come back in three different formats.
  • Ask about empty and large. What does zero results look like? What happens at 10,000 rows? These two questions catch more bugs than any amount of testing later.
  • Never block waiting. If the endpoint is two days away, build against the mock and integrate on day three.

Distributed work — the four habits

The question shape that gets fast answers "I'm trying to X. I expected Y, I'm getting Z. I've checked A and B. Unless I hear otherwise I'll go with option 1 and can change it later." That last clause is the important one — it means the other person can reply "yes" in five seconds, and you are never blocked waiting for permission.

When the ticket is unclear

  1. Write down what you think it means, in one sentence, and post it. "Reading this as: filter applies to the current page only, not the whole result set. Correct?"
  2. Start on the part that is certain, whatever the answer.
  3. State your assumption and keep going if the answer does not arrive. Never sit still for a day.

That sequence — restate, start on the certain part, assume and continue — is the whole answer to "how do you handle ambiguity", and it is much better than "I ask the product owner".

The English factor, said directly

The job description asks for English and Vietnamese. On a distributed team, written English carries most of the load, and written English is the format where you have time to think. That is an advantage — use it. Short sentences, one idea each, a bullet list instead of a paragraph. A clear three-line message from a non-native speaker beats a rambling paragraph from a native one, every time.

Drill · out loud, 3 minutes

"You're in Vietnam, half the team is in Europe, and you're blocked on a backend question at 4pm your time. What do you do?"

A weak answer waits. A good answer: post the question in the shape above with your intended assumption, keep building against a mock, and pick up the reply the next morning. Then the senior addition — "and if this keeps happening, I'd raise it in the retro, because a repeated blocker is a process problem, not a personal one."

Remember this
  • Say what each ceremony is for, not what it is called.
  • In a written standup, blockers go first. Last means eight hours lost.
  • Estimate with a range plus the thing that would move it.
  • Bad news early. Silence reads as "on track" when nobody can see your screen.
  • With backend: agree the contract, then build against a mock. Ask about errors, empty, and large.
  • Four habits: write it down, ask fully, show progress, protect the overlap hours.
  • Ambiguity: restate, start on the certain part, assume and continue.
  • Written English is your advantage — short sentences, lists over paragraphs.

If you remember one thing: on a distributed team, being easy to work with is a technical skill.
