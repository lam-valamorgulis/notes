Day 4 · Lesson 12 · Working with people

Telling the story

Your experience is real. Whether they hear it depends entirely on how you shape the first ninety seconds.

Your win today: you have three rehearsed stories — the hard technical thing, the disagreement, and the failure — each 90 seconds, each ending in a place where you want the follow-up question.

Why 90 seconds

Under 45 seconds sounds like you have nothing to say. Over two minutes and the interviewer stops listening and starts waiting. Ninety seconds is long enough for a real technical arc and short enough that they stay engaged.

Non-native speakers usually make the opposite mistake to the one they fear. The fear is "my English is not good enough". The real problem is length: a long, unstructured answer is where grammar falls apart and the point gets lost. A short, rehearsed structure fixes both at once.

The four-part shape

PartSecondsWhat goes here
Context15What the product was and your role. One sentence. No company history.
Constraint20The one thing that made it hard. This is the hook — make it technical and specific.
Contribution40What you decided and did. Named technologies. "I", not "we".
Consequence15The result, with a number if you have one, and what you learned.

Story 1 — the hard technical thing

You built this yesterday. The shape:

Skeleton — fill it with your own words, then say it out loud five times Context: "A virtual-office collaboration product — video calls, screen share, chat and presence. I worked on the front end in React and TypeScript."

Constraint: "The hard part was that React had to render state it did not own. A call is a long-lived machine with sockets, transports and hardware, and it changes for reasons React knows nothing about — the other person hangs up, the network drops, someone unplugs a headset."

Contribution: "I worked to a boundary: the engine owns the call, React renders a snapshot of it. Commands go down, state comes back up as events. Concretely that meant streams reaching the screen through a ref and an effect, cleanup that stops the tracks so the camera light goes off, and no component keeping its own copy of the mute state — because the moment it does, the button starts lying to the user."

Consequence: "It held up as the app grew. And the rule I took from it — never keep a second copy of state that something else owns — is the first thing I check in code review now."
End on a hook, on purpose That last line invites "what does that look like in review?" The best answers end somewhere you can go deeper. Never end on "…and then we shipped it", which closes the door.

Story 2 — the disagreement

They will ask some version of "tell me about a disagreement with a colleague". This question has one right shape and many wrong ones.

WrongWhy it fails
"I've never had a disagreement."Reads as either dishonest or disengaged.
A story where you were right and they were wrong.They are listening for how you handle being wrong, or being overruled.
A story about a person, not a decision.Now it is gossip, and they are imagining you talking about them.

The right shape: a technical disagreement, resolved with evidence, where you say what you did after the decision went the other way.

The line that ends this story well

"We disagreed, so I built the smallest version that would answer the question, and we looked at the numbers together. It went their way. I wrote up what we learned so the next person would not have to run it again."

Story 3 — the failure

"Tell me about a time you broke something." Use a real one. Rules:

  • The bug must be yours. A story about someone else's mistake fails the question.
  • Spend most of the time on the fix and the prevention, not the drama.
  • End with the guard you added — a test, a lint rule, a checklist item — so it cannot happen again.
  • Never blame the deadline, the spec or QA.

The camera-light bug is a good one if it is true for you: a stream not cleaned up, the device left open after a call, the user's camera indicator still on. It is visceral, it is specific, and the fix is one line plus a code-review rule.

"I" and "we"

"We" hides you. In a 90-second story, say "I" for every decision you made and "we" only for the team's outcome. This feels boastful in some cultures and it is not — the interviewer literally cannot hire "we".

Instead ofSay
"We decided to use an SFU.""I looked at the topologies and argued for an SFU, because mesh dies at about five people."
"We fixed the device bug.""I traced it to a burst of devicechange events and serialised the handler with a lock."
"The team improved performance.""I profiled it, found the context value rebuilding every render, and split the context."

The honest answer about experience

Job descriptions say "3 years of ReactJS". Nobody's experience is a clean line, and everyone knows it. What loses trust is padding; what builds it is precision.

If part of your React is recent or self-directed, say it like this

"My production React is [X]. Where I've deliberately caught up in the last year is [Redux Toolkit / server state / testing] — here's what I built to do it." Then give the concrete thing. Precision reads as confidence. Vagueness reads as hiding.

Never claim a technology you cannot answer a second question about. One follow-up exposes it, and after that they discount everything else you said.

"Why are you leaving?"

One rule: talk about what you are moving toward, never what you are running from. Two sentences, then stop. No criticism of a current employer, however fair — the interviewer hears how you will talk about them one day.

Drill · record yourself Say all three stories out loud with a timer. Record them on your phone and listen back once. You are checking three things: Did I go over 2 minutes? Did I say "we" where I meant "I"? Did the technical hook land in the first 30 seconds? Listening back is uncomfortable and it is the single most effective 20 minutes of this week.

Remember this
  • Ninety seconds. Under 45 sounds empty; over 120 loses them.
  • Four parts: context, constraint, contribution, consequence — and 40 of the 90 seconds are yours.
  • Have three ready: the hard thing, the disagreement, the failure.
  • The disagreement story must include what you did after losing it.
  • The failure must be your own, and must end in a guard that prevents a repeat.
  • Say "I" for decisions. They cannot hire "we".
  • Be precise about experience. Vague claims collapse on the first follow-up.
  • End every story on a hook you want to be asked about.

If you remember one thing: rehearsed is not dishonest — it is respect for their time.
