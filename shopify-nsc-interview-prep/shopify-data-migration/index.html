Shopify Data Migration

"Here is their old data, get it into Shopify." Nine lessons on the job every Shopify engineer is eventually handed — from the first scoping question, through the one decision that makes the whole thing re-runnable, to the report that proves it worked.

Why this sub-course exists: a migration is high risk and highly visible. Wrong prices go live, order history vanishes, search traffic drops the day the domain switches. And almost every failure is silent — the import succeeds and the data is wrong. This sub-course is about seeing those failures before they happen, and being able to prove they did not.

The big picture

Start with the sentence that sorts everything. What a data migration actually is asks eight words — is the old system being switched off? — and the answer decides the whole design. "Yes" means a migration: it ends, so you may freeze the source and take one clean snapshot. "No" means you were handed a sync wearing a migration's name, and the design belongs to main-course Lesson 11 instead. That lesson also names the thing juniors get wrong about the deliverable: nobody can read 40,000 products, so the client is not buying your importer — they are buying your evidence that it worked. And it fixes the one order you do not get to choose: products, then customers, then orders, because an order is mostly pointers.

Then the first honest conversation. What moves, what breaks, what never moves sorts every record type into three buckets, and the third one is a relationship risk rather than an engineering one. Customer passwords never move — the old system only holds one-way hashes, so nobody can move a password — although passwordless sign-in with a one-time code has made that a much smaller problem than it used to be. Historical orders have no CSV import at all. And the limit that quietly reshapes catalogues is three options per product, because a fourth axis is dropped in silence.

Next, the decision the rest of the job hangs on. The matching key & idempotency starts from a certainty: the import will run more than once. So the real question is never "how do I write this record?" but "how do I find the record this already became?" Shopify has a documented answer most candidates have never heard of — a custom ID, your legacy primary key in an id-type metafield, a type the platform makes unique for you — which productSet can look up and upsert against in one call. The same lesson carries the most destructive detail in the whole subject: productSet deletes list entries you leave out, so a batched variant list does not add, it replaces.

Then the pipes. Three ways to push the data chooses between a CSV import, one mutation per record, and a bulk operation using four published numbers — and shows why the professional default is the slow one, because a migration's hardest requirement is accounting, not speed. It also collects the CSV traps Shopify states plainly and nobody reads: an import that cannot be cancelled, a spreadsheet sort that loses your images, and an innocent option rename that replaces every variant ID on the store.

The huge catalogue load is where a competent plan still fails, and not because of your code. There is a documented daily throttle on creating new variants for non-Plus stores — and the two Shopify pages that describe it do not agree on the threshold. At the low end, a 300,000-variant catalogue is three hundred days of imports. That is why "which plan is the store on?" is a technical question asked before the estimate, and why the biggest lever in the job is refusing to import a reference catalogue that the merchant does not actually sell.

Then the two hard cases. Historical orders & money walks the five defaults in orderCreate that each produce an order which imports perfectly and is wrong — the missing processedAt that puts four years of trading on one day, and the missing line price that quietly charges 2019 orders at 2026 rates. It is also the reason the reconciliation report has a money row and not just counts. Shopify → Shopify takes the case that feels easiest and has the least official guidance, and finds one structural fact underneath it: every ID changes, so the job is not migrating data but re-establishing identity — in two passes, with the integration re-mapping as the real cost.

Then you land it. The cutover & the proof puts the steps in the only order that works, protects the client's search traffic with a bulk redirect import submitted before the domain moves, writes inventory last and absolutely, and gives the honest answer about rollback: it is DNS, not data. Finally the migration interview turns all of it into a performance — three question shapes, an opener that stays true about what you have and have not run, a five-stage script, the six answers that sink candidates, and a twenty-question drill that mixes every lesson together on purpose.

Three reference cards carry the compressed version: the glossary for exact vocabulary, the what-moves matrix to take into a scoping call, and the cutover runbook for the day it goes live.

How this fits the main course

This sub-course is the one-time move. Its siblings cover the neighbouring problems and it links across rather than repeating them:

  • Lesson 11 · Shopify ↔ ERP sync — the ongoing version. If the old system is not being switched off, you are there, not here.
  • Lesson 18 · Bulk operations — the mechanics of bulkOperationRunMutation. Lesson 4 here chooses when to use it; Lesson 18 shows how.
  • POS Lesson 6 · Migrating off a legacy till — the programme shape: ownership matrix, six phases, parallel run. Lesson 8 here covers only the data-specific parts of a cutover.
  • POS Lesson 3 · Multi-location inventory — the eight inventory states, and why absolute writes beat deltas. Read it before writing inventory at cutover.
  • Game Locker sub-course — a live job where onboarding a merchant means exactly this work, followed by a sync forever.

What this sub-course deliberately does not do

  • It does not teach ongoing sync. Different problem, different lesson. Lesson 1 draws the line and stays on the migration side of it.
  • It does not teach one source platform. Magento or WooCommerce export quirks are lookup work. The skill is the shape that repeats.
  • It does not cover theme or app rebuild. A replatform needs both; Lesson 7 names them as dependencies and stops.
  • It does not state a fact it could not verify. Every number was read from shopify.dev or help.shopify.com on 2026-08-03, with the link on the page. Where the docs disagree with each other, the lessons say so instead of picking one.
