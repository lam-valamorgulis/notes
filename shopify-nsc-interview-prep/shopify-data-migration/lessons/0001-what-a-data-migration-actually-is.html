Lesson 1 · Shopify Data Migration · First principles

What a Data Migration Actually Is

A migration is a one-time move with an end date. A sync never ends. Confusing the two is the most expensive mistake in this whole subject — and the interview question that exposes it is only eight words long.

Your win today: say the one difference between a migration and a sync, name the deliverable a migration is actually judged on, and recite the forced import order — plus the reason Shopify gives for it.

Ties to your mission: "here is their old data, get it into Shopify" is the task you will be handed. This lesson sets the frame every other lesson sits inside.

1. Two jobs that look identical and are not

Both jobs move records from another system into Shopify. Both use the same API. They are still different jobs, because one of them stops.

MIGRATION vs SYNC — THE ONLY DIFFERENCE THAT MATTERS MIGRATION SYNC ───────── ──── ┌──────────────┐ old system ──────► Shopify │ ▼ │ │ old ⇄ Shopify │ one pass │ │ ▼ └──────────────┘ old system is both systems stay alive, SWITCHED OFF forever, and disagree has an END DATE has NO end date correctness = matched once correctness = converges always you can freeze the source you can never freeze anything a re-run is a REPAIR a re-run is NORMAL OPERATION ⇒ the migration question is "did it land?" the sync question is "is it still landing?"

That gives you very different freedoms. In a migration you may stop the world: close the old shop, block edits, take one clean snapshot. A sync never gets that. So a migration can be simpler and stricter — simpler because nothing is changing under you, stricter because you only get one honest shot at the numbers.

The eight-word question "Is the old system being switched off?" Ask it first, every time. "Yes" means migration — plan a freeze, a cutover and a proof. "No" means you were handed a sync project wearing a migration's name, and the design is Lesson 11's, not this sub-course's.
Both, in real life Most real projects are a migration followed by a sync. You move the history once, then keep the two systems talking about new records. Say that out loud when you spot it — it shows you can see a project's shape, not just its ticket title. The Game Locker job is exactly this: onboard a store's back catalogue once, then sync forever.

2. What you are actually judged on

Junior thinking: the deliverable is the import script. Senior thinking: the deliverable is the proof.

Nobody can read 40,000 products. So the client cannot check your work directly — they can only check your evidence. That means the artefact that ends the project is a table:

RecordIn the old systemIn ShopifyDifferenceExplained?
Products12,43112,4310—
Variants38,90238,514−388Yes — 388 had a 4th option, merged (see note)
Customers52,11051,998−112Yes — 112 had no email address
Orders (last 3 yrs)88,20488,2040—
Order total value$4,113,882.10$4,113,882.10$0.00—

This report shape is engineering practice, not a documented Shopify process. Shopify tells you how to write data in; it says nothing about how to prove it landed. That gap is yours to fill — which is exactly why filling it reads as senior.

Look at the third row. A difference of zero is not the goal. An explained difference is the goal. A migration with 388 unexplained missing variants has failed. A migration with 388 variants missing for a stated reason the client accepted has succeeded.

Say it this way

"The deliverable is not the importer, it is the reconciliation report — counts and money totals on both sides, with every difference explained. A difference of zero is not what I aim for; an explained difference is. That is the only thing the client can actually check."

3. The order is not your choice

You cannot import in whatever order is convenient. Shopify is explicit:

help.shopify.com · Migrate to Shopify "The order in which you import certain data matters. Import your products first, then your customers, and then any historical orders so that products and customers can be properly connected to the orders."

The reason is plain once you see it: an order is mostly references. It points at a customer and at variants. If the thing it points at does not exist yet, the reference cannot be made — and you get an order with a name typed into it instead of a link to a real customer record.

WHY THE ORDER IS FORCED — AN ORDER IS MOSTLY POINTERS 1. PRODUCTS 2. CUSTOMERS 3. ORDERS ┌───────────────┐ ┌──────────────┐ ┌─────────────────┐ │ product │ │ customer │ │ order #1042 │ │ └─ variant ──┼──┐ ┌──┤ email │ │ processedAt ▸ │ │ (SKU) │ │ │ └──────────────┘ │ customer ──────┼─┐ └───────────────┘ │ │ │ lineItems: │ │ │ │ │ variantId ────┼┐│ │ └────────────────────────────┼─────────────────┘││ └─────────────────────────────────┼──────────────────┘│ └───────────────────┘ import 3 before 1 and 2, and the pointers have nothing to point at → the line becomes loose text, the customer becomes a stranger, and lifetime-value reporting is quietly wrong forever
Cover the screen Recite the three-step import order, then say why in one sentence. If your "why" is "because the docs say so", try again — the real answer is about pointers.

4. The three questions that scope the job

You will be asked to estimate a migration long before you have seen the data. Three questions get you most of the way:

#QuestionWhat it decides
1What moves, and how far back?Scope and price. "All orders since 2009" and "orders since 2023" are different projects. Shopify's own advice: at minimum cover your return-policy window, and 2–5 years is the usual recommendation.
2What is the matching key?Whether the job is re-runnable. This is the single most important technical decision and it gets its own lesson — Lesson 3.
3How will we prove it worked?The definition of done. Agree the reconciliation table before writing code, or "done" becomes an argument.
The failure mode this prevents Migrations rarely fail at the write. The API call works; that part is easy. They fail on mapping (their data does not fit Shopify's shape) and on proof (nobody agreed what "worked" means, so the project never ends). A candidate who talks only about mutations and rate limits has answered the easy third of the question.

5. Where the difficulty actually sits

Rough split, from the shape of the work rather than any published figure:

WHERE A MIGRATION'S EFFORT REALLY GOES understanding their data ████████████████████ the long pole mapping it to Shopify ██████████████ writing the importer █████ proving it landed ████████████ the cutover itself ███ the API is the SMALL part. "which field in their export is the SKU?" costs more days than every mutation in this sub-course put together.

This is why the next lesson is not about code. It is about which records can move at all — because the first real conversation with a client is a list of what they are about to lose.

Remember this
  • A migration ends; a sync does not. Ask "is the old system being switched off?" first.
  • A migration may freeze the source. That is a freedom a sync never has — use it.
  • The deliverable is the reconciliation report, not the importer. Counts and money on both sides.
  • An explained difference is the goal, not a difference of zero.
  • The import order is forced: products → customers → orders — "so that products and customers can be properly connected to the orders."
  • An order is mostly pointers, which is why it must go last.
  • Migrations fail at mapping and proof, not at the write. The API is the small part.
