Lesson 9 · Shopify Data Migration · Capstone

The Migration Interview

Eight lessons of knowledge, turned into a performance. Three question shapes, one honest opener, a five-stage script for the design question, the six answers that sink candidates, and a rapid-fire drill that mixes every lesson together on purpose.

Your win today: answer "walk me through a data migration" for four minutes without hedging — using a script you can run under pressure, and an opener that is true.

Ties to your mission: the mission ends at "answer for it in an interview." This is that page. Do it last, out loud, and preferably twice.

1. Three shapes, and only three

ShapeSounds likeWhat they are testingYour move
The design question "They have 40,000 products and 4 years of orders on Magento. How would you move them?" Whether you have a shape in your head or are improvising. Run the five-stage script in section 3. Do not start with mutations.
The trap question "What could go wrong?" / "What would you check first?" Whether you have been burned, or only read. Give a silent failure, not a loud one. Loud failures are easy; silent ones prove experience.
The experience question "Tell me about a migration you have run." Honesty, and whether your story survives follow-ups. Section 2. Never inflate — the follow-up questions will find it.
The single best habit for all three Name the silent failure. Anyone can say "the import might fail". Only someone who has thought properly says "the import will succeed and be wrong — every 2019 order priced at 2026 prices, because omitting a line-item price makes the API use the current variant price." Silent failures are the currency of this subject.

2. The honest opener — 45 seconds

The rest of this course is consistent about one thing: never claim work you have not done. Migration is the same. Fill this in with what is actually true for you, and say it once, early, without apology.

The template

"To be straight with you about scope: I have ______________. What I have not done is ______________. But I know the shape of the job well, and I would rather show you that than overstate it — so let me walk you through how I would run one, including the parts I would expect to get wrong first."

If your real experience is…Fill the first blank with…
Theme and app integration work"…moved catalogue and customer data at volume through the Admin API as part of integration work, and dealt with the reconciliation side when the numbers disagreed"
Bulk corrections and data fixes"…run large bulk updates against live stores, which is the same idempotency problem with a smaller blast radius"
Only supporting a migration someone else led"…worked on a migration led by someone else, where I owned this part"
Genuinely none"…not run a full platform migration end to end"
Why this works better than bluffing A migration interview is unusually easy to probe. Three follow-ups get to bedrock: what was the matching key? how did you prove it worked? what was the rollback? A borrowed story collapses on the second one. A true small story plus a strong design answer does not — and interviewers consistently rate honesty about scope as a senior signal, because it is what they need from you on a real project.

3. The five-stage script for the design question

Say the stage names out loud as you go. It tells the interviewer you have a method and it stops you rambling.

THE SCRIPT — 4 MINUTES ① SCOPE "First I'd ask three things: is the old 30 s system being switched off, how far back do orders go, and which Shopify plan are they on." ② WHAT MOVES "Then I'd sort every record into moves cleanly, 45 s moves with loss, never moves — and send that list in writing before starting." ③ IDENTITY "The key technical decision is the matching key. 60 s I'd put their primary key in an id-type metafield ★ the core and upsert with productSet identifier.customId, so the import is re-runnable by anyone." ④ ROUTE + ORDER "Products, then customers, then orders. One 45 s mutation per record for accounting, bulk for volume. Inventory last, absolutely." ⑤ PROOF + CUTOVER "The deliverable is the reconciliation report — 60 s counts AND money. Static data loads weeks early; the cutover moves only the delta, inventory, redirects and DNS. Rollback is DNS." then STOP and ask: "which part would you like me to go deeper on?" — that hands them the follow-up and shows you know there IS more depth.
The most common way strong candidates lose this question They start at stage ④. Mutations, rate limits, bulk operations — all correct, all the easy third of the job. Starting there tells the interviewer you have implemented a migration but never owned one. Stages ①–③ are what a lead does, and they cost you 2 minutes and 15 seconds.

4. Six answers that sink candidates

The red flagWhy it lands badlySay this instead
"I'd export a CSV and import it." Fine for a small shop; on a paid job it says you have not met a real dataset. And there is no order CSV import. "CSV works for a small clean catalogue. On a paid migration I need a per-record result for every skipped record, so I'd go through the API."
"I'd use bulk operations because it's faster." Optimises for the wrong thing. Speed is rarely the constraint; accounting is. "Bulk for the volume-heavy passes. But the hardest requirement is accounting, not speed, so I want a per-record response where explanations matter."
"I'd make sure the import doesn't fail." Names the loud failure. Migrations mostly fail by succeeding wrongly. "Failure isn't my worry — silent success is. Wrong dates, today's prices on old orders, an unfulfilled backlog. All of those import cleanly."
"I'd roll back the import if there's a problem." Not a real capability. Interviewers know it. "The rollback is DNS, not data. I keep the old system alive and paid for an agreed window and point the domain back if needed."
"SKUs are the natural key." Trusts the client's data. Every catalogue has blank and duplicated SKUs. "I'd check for duplicate and blank SKUs first. I'd rather use their primary key in an id-type metafield, which Shopify makes unique automatically."
"I'd import everything they have." Misses the biggest lever in the job, and can create an undeliverable timeline. "I'd ask what they actually sell versus what's reference data, and how far back orders need to go. Both are scope levers worth real money."
Cover the screen Recite the five stage names in order. Then give one silent failure and one loud one, and say which is the better interview answer.

5. Rapid-fire — interleaved on purpose

These jump between lessons deliberately. Mixing topics is harder than reviewing one at a time, and that difficulty is what builds durable memory rather than the feeling of knowing.

6. The three set-pieces

Say each one aloud, timed, before revealing the model answer. Four minutes for the first, two for the others.

8. Read this next

Then close the loop Re-read the runbook and the what-moves matrix — those two pages are the whole sub-course compressed, and they are the right things to skim in the hour before an interview. The glossary keeps your vocabulary exact under pressure.
Remember this
  • Three question shapes: design, trap, experience. Nothing else comes up.
  • Name silent failures, not loud ones. "It imports cleanly and is wrong" beats "it might fail."
  • Open honestly about scope, in one sentence, early. Three follow-ups always reach bedrock.
  • Five stages: scope → what moves → identity → route and order → proof and cutover.
  • Do not start at stage 4. Leading with mutations says you have implemented one, never owned one.
  • The matching key is the technical core. If you say one thing well, say that.
  • Reconcile money, not just counts — and say why counts alone would pass.
  • Rollback is DNS. Keep the old platform paid for an agreed window.
  • Finish in four minutes and hand back a follow-up.
If you remember one thing: the interviewer is not checking whether you can call the API — they are checking whether you can be trusted with something that is expensive to get wrong and easy to get wrong quietly.
