Lesson 8 · Shopify Data Migration · Landing it

The Cutover & the Proof

Everything so far was writing records. This is the hour it becomes real: the freeze, the sequence, the redirects that protect the client's search traffic, and the report that ends the project. Plus the honest answer about rollback.

Your win today: write the cutover sequence in the right order, name the step that protects the client's search traffic, and say plainly what a rollback actually is on a migration — because it is not "undo the import".

Ties to your mission: this is the lesson that turns knowledge into a delivery. It complements POS Lesson 6, which covers the programme shape — ownership, phases, parallel run. This one covers only the parts specific to data.

1. Use the freeze — it is the only advantage you have

From Lesson 1: a migration may stop the world, and a sync never can. The freeze is how you cash that in.

THE TWO-PHASE LOAD WEEKS BEFORE │ FREEZE │ CUTOVER HOUR ───────────── └────────┘ ──────────── load everything that does load only what NOT change: CHANGED since: · products, variants · edited products · customers · new customers · historical orders · orders since snapshot · media, metafields · INVENTORY (all of it) · collections · redirects live · DNS switch slow, unhurried, verifiable fast, rehearsed, small ⇒ the cutover hour should move MINUTES of data, not months of it. a team that loads everything on cutover night has turned a calm project into a gamble on one evening.
The delta pass is free if you did Lesson 3 Because every record carries a custom ID and every write is an upsert, the delta pass is the same importer run again with a narrower query. No second codebase, no special "update mode". That is the payoff of the matching key decision — and it is worth saying out loud in an interview, because it shows one decision paying for itself twice.

2. The sequence

#StepWhy it sits here

6Publish products to sales channelsImported products may be sitting hidden. Section 4.
7Write inventoryLast. The only number that must be true at this exact moment.
8Submit redirectsLive before the domain resolves to Shopify, not after.
9Switch DNS / connect the domainThe actual go-live. Section 5.
10Run the reconciliation reportThen and there, not the next morning. Section 6.
11Turn notifications back onEasy to forget, and the client stops hearing about real orders.
Step 11 is the one that gets forgotten Notifications go off in step 1 and the project ends at step 10. Two weeks later somebody asks why nobody was told about a large order. Put step 11 in the runbook as a checkbox with a name against it, not as something you will remember.

3. Redirects: the highest-value hour in the project

The old URLs do not move. Shopify says so directly: its "link structure for individual pages is likely different from your previous service." So every link the world holds — Google's index, other people's blog posts, saved bookmarks, email campaigns — points at a page that will not exist.

Fixing it is a bulk import of its own:

StepCallDetail
1stagedUploadsCreateGet an upload target for your redirect CSV. Shopify publishes a sample redirect CSV to copy the format from.
2urlRedirectImportCreateTakes the staged upload url and creates a UrlRedirectImport. Needs the write_online_store_navigation scope.
3urlRedirectImportSubmitActually runs the import. Two steps on purpose — create, then submit.

Source: shopify.dev · urlRedirectImportCreate, read 2026-08-03. Single redirects use urlRedirectCreate; the admin route is Content → Menus → URL Redirects, which also accepts a CSV.

WHERE THE REDIRECT LIST COMES FROM 1. the client's OWN sitemap.xml ← every page they published 2. Google Search Console ← every page that gets TRAFFIC (top pages by impressions) ★ prioritise these 3. the old platform's URL patterns ← /product/ → /products/ 4. server logs, if you can get them ← the long tail nobody remembers then: old path → new path, one row each. ⇒ source 2 is the one that matters. 500 redirects for the pages that actually earn traffic beats 50,000 generated mechanically for pages nobody visits.
Say it this way

"Redirects are the highest-value hour in a migration, because the client's search traffic is an asset they paid years to build and it can be lost in a day. I generate the list from Search Console rather than from the sitemap — I want the pages that actually earn traffic, ranked — and I submit the redirects before the domain resolves to Shopify, so no visitor ever sees a 404."

Cover the screen Name the three mutations in the bulk-redirect sequence, in order. Then say which source you would build the redirect list from, and why it beats the sitemap.

4. Two launch-day traps in the data itself

Trap 1 — the store looks empty Documented: "If the products that you import are marked as hidden, then they aren't published until you make them available to your sales channels."

This is normal and correct behaviour during a migration — you want products hidden while you load them. The failure is forgetting to publish. The symptom is a live store with a complete catalogue in the admin and an empty storefront, discovered by the client rather than by you. Make publishing an explicit numbered step, and verify by counting published products on the storefront, not in the admin.
Trap 2 — the SSL error nobody expects Documented: "You might need to disconnect any existing domains you want to transfer from your previous platform's settings. If you don't, then you might encounter errors with SSL certificates."

So the domain must be released on the old side before Shopify can secure it. The failure mode is the worst kind: the store is live, correct, and shows a browser security warning. Sequence the disconnect explicitly with whoever controls the old platform, and confirm it happened rather than assuming.

5. Inventory goes last, and absolutely

Every other record is history and can be written a week early. Inventory is the one number that must be true now, because it decides whether the store oversells in its first hour.

6. The report that ends the project

Run it during the cutover, not the next morning. It is the artefact from Lesson 1, now filled in.

THE RECONCILIATION REPORT ROW TYPE SOURCE SHOPIFY Δ EXPLAINED ──────── ────── ─────── ── ───────── products 12,431 12,431 0 — variants 38,902 38,514 −388 4th option merged customers 52,110 51,998 −112 no email address orders (3 yrs) 88,204 88,204 0 — ★ order value $4,113,882 $4,113,882 $0.00 — ★ inventory units 214,880 214,880 0 — published products — 12,431 — storefront count redirects live — 4,812 — top pages covered ★ the two money/quantity rows are the ones that catch the silent bugs. counts alone would have passed even with every 2019 order priced at 2026 prices. sign-off = every Δ has a sentence next to it.

This report shape is engineering practice, not a documented Shopify process — Shopify documents how to write data in and says nothing about proving it landed. Which is exactly why producing it reads as senior.

7. Rollback — the honest answer

Interviewers ask this, and the weak answer is "I would roll back the import."

WHAT IS ACTUALLY REVERSIBLE reversible in minutes NOT reversible ───────────────────── ────────────── · DNS / domain · 88,000 imported orders → point back at the old (deletable one by one, not site. THIS is the practically undoable) real rollback. · emails already sent to · publishing (unpublish customers or staff products again) · a CSV import already · redirects (they are running — "can't be just data) canceled once they begin" · inventory (absolute writes, set them back) ⇒ the rollback plan is: KEEP THE OLD SYSTEM ALIVE and revert DNS. Not "undo the data".
Say it this way

"The rollback is DNS, not data. I keep the old system running and un-frozen for an agreed window — usually a week or two — so if something serious surfaces we point the domain back and the client keeps trading while we fix it. Un-importing tens of thousands of orders is not a practical rollback, and I would rather say that plainly than pretend there is an undo button. What I can reverse quickly is publishing, redirects and inventory, because those are small and absolute."

Which means one contract clause matters "The old platform stays live and paid for N weeks after cutover." Clients want to cancel it on day one to save money. That subscription is your rollback plan, and it is also what keeps image URLs alive if any media is still being fetched (Lesson 5). Get the date in writing.

Remember this
  • Two-phase load: everything static weeks early, only the delta plus inventory at cutover. The cutover hour should move minutes of data.
  • The delta pass is the same importer re-run — the payoff of the matching key.
  • Notifications off before importing orders, and back on at the end. Step 11 is the forgotten one.
  • Redirects: stagedUploadsCreate → urlRedirectImportCreate → urlRedirectImportSubmit, and they must be live before the domain moves.
  • Build the redirect list from Search Console, ranked by traffic — not mechanically from the sitemap.
  • Imported products can sit hidden — publishing to sales channels is an explicit step, verified on the storefront.
  • Disconnect the domain on the old platform first, or you get SSL certificate errors on a live store.
  • Inventory last, absolute, with compareQuantity and a referenceDocumentUri.
  • Reconcile counts and money at cutover. Counts alone pass even when every price is wrong.
  • Rollback is DNS, not data. Keep the old system alive and paid for an agreed window.
If you remember one thing: the cutover hour should be boring — everything that could have been done earlier was, and the only things left are the delta, inventory, redirects and DNS.
