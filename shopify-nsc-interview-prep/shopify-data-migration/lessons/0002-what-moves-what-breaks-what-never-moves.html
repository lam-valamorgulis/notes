Lesson 2 · Shopify Data Migration · The records

What Moves, What Breaks, What Never Moves

The first honest conversation in a migration is a list of what the client is about to lose. Three buckets, one table, and the two sentences in Shopify's own docs that quietly admit the limits.

Your win today: sort any record type into moves cleanly, moves with loss, or never moves — and explain why customer passwords are in the third bucket, plus the 2026 reason that matters much less than it used to.

Ties to your mission: Lesson 1 said migrations fail at mapping, not at the write. This is the mapping conversation — held before the estimate, not after.

1. Three buckets

Every record type in a migration lands in exactly one of these. Sorting them is the first hour of the project.

2. The matrix

Shopify's own migration guide lists what can be moved: products, customers, historical orders, gift cards, certificates and store credits, blogs, and pages. Here is that list with the catch attached to each one.

CollectionsMoves cleanlyCSV, collectionCreate Plan them before the product import so products can be assigned during it.

3. Passwords: why they cannot move, and why that stopped mattering

Shopify's guide says it in one line: customer passwords cannot be migrated. The reason is not a missing feature — it is how passwords are stored anywhere competent.

WHY A PASSWORD CANNOT BE MOVED old system stores: bcrypt$2b$12$Kx9f... ← a ONE-WAY hash (not the password) to move it you would need Shopify to: · accept a foreign hash format, AND · run the old system's exact hashing algorithm forever Shopify does neither. So the hash is portable bytes that nothing on the other side can verify. ⇒ "we cannot migrate passwords" really means "nobody knows your customers' passwords — including your old system"
The 2026 answer that makes this a small problem Shopify's current customer accounts are passwordless: "They enter their email address and receive a one-time 6-digit verification code. A password isn't required to sign in." So on a store using them, there is no password to lose. The customer types their email, gets a code, and is in — with the order history you imported already attached. Knowing this turns an apology into a design answer.
Say it this way

"Passwords cannot move, and that is correct behaviour rather than a gap — the old system only holds one-way hashes, so nobody can move a password. On current customer accounts it barely matters, because sign-in is passwordless: the customer enters their email and gets a one-time six-digit code. What I do plan for is the communication — customers need to be told once that their history is intact and how to get back in."

4. The two limits that reshape a catalogue

These are the ones that turn "moves cleanly" into "moves with loss", and they are both about product shape rather than volume.

LimitDocumented asWhat it does to a migration
3 options per product "A product can have a maximum of 3 options — products with more than 3 options won't have their options imported." A source catalogue with size × colour × material × length must lose one axis. You either fold two into one option ("Oak / 2m") or split into separate products. This is a client decision, not yours.
2048 variants per product "By default, stores have a limit of 2048 product variants for each product." Rarely a wall since it was raised from 100 in October 2025 — but it decides the model for huge catalogues. See Lesson 5.
Prices per location "Prices by location aren't supported — prices are automatically set to the product's highest price." A limit of Shopify's own Store Migration app, not of Shopify. If the source prices per shop, do not use that tool.
The fourth-option trap A source catalogue with four axes does not fail loudly. Shopify's tooling imports the product and silently drops the extra options. You get products that look fine and are unbuyable in the combination the customer wants. Count the distinct option names in the source export before you estimate — one GROUP BY saves a week.

5. What "never moves" actually costs

Bucket 3 is small, and every item in it is a conversation rather than a task:

  • Passwords → a customer email on launch day, and passwordless accounts to land on.
  • Reviews → budget for a review app, and accept its own import path. Star ratings are social proof and the client will notice their absence within hours.
  • Analytics history → the old dashboards die with the old platform. Export the reports the client cares about as files, before the contract ends.
  • Old URLs → the pages do not move, so you generate redirects instead. Shopify says the link structure "is likely different from your previous service." This is the highest-value hour in the whole project.
Put it in writing, early Bucket 3 is not an engineering risk — it is a relationship risk. Every item on that list, sent as a short written note before the work starts, converts from "you lost our reviews" into "we agreed reviews would be re-collected." Same facts, completely different conversation.

Remember this
  • Three buckets: moves cleanly, moves with loss, never moves. Bucket 3 is a written conversation, not a task.
  • Historical orders have no CSV import — migration apps, the Order API and the Transaction API are the routes.
  • Passwords never move, because the old system holds one-way hashes. Nobody can move a password.
  • Current customer accounts are passwordless — email plus a one-time 6-digit code — so the loss barely bites.
  • 3 options per product is the limit that reshapes catalogues, and extra options are dropped silently.
  • 2048 variants per product by default, raised from 100 in October 2025.
  • Saved payment methods barely move: Stripe credit cards, Braintree credit cards and Apple Pay. Raw card numbers need Plus or Enterprise plus Shopify Professional Services.
  • Reviews never move between platforms — budget a review app.
If you remember one thing: bucket 3 does not ruin projects because the data is lost — it ruins them because nobody said so until after go-live.
