The Pricing Engine

A card shop does not have a price. It has three, and only one of them is stored. Understand how the other two are produced and you understand the most interesting subsystem Game Locker has.

Your win today: explain why buy price is derived rather than stored, draw the rule model from memory — filter plus two percentages, three rule types, global rounding — and narrate the one real test where the price did not reproduce, and what that failure tells you about their architecture.

Every fact here is a labelled row in verified-facts §6. Labels: verified read from a live tenant · inference reasoned, sample size given · gap never state as fact.

1. Three prices, and only one is a number

Start by refusing the word "price". On a trading-card platform it means at least three different things, and mixing them up is the fastest way to build the wrong system.

NameWhat it isWho sets itStored?
Market price What this card is worth in the world right now, from a global price feed Nobody. It is observed, not decided Yes. It is data that arrives
Sell price What the shop charges you for it The shop, as a rule over market price No — computed, then published
Buy price What the shop pays you for it. Two of them: cash and store credit The shop, as a rule over market price No. Computed on demand

Only market price is a stored fact. The other two are opinions the shop holds, expressed as rules, evaluated when someone asks.

Say it like this

"Their buy price isn't a column. It's the output of a rule engine over a market feed. That's why a card can have no buy price at all — it isn't missing data, it's the shop saying it doesn't want that card."

2. Why derived beats stored, and where it stops being true

Imagine the alternative. You store buy_cash and buy_credit on every priced row. There are around 1.12 million catalogue items, five conditions each, so several million rows. Now the shop owner decides to pay 12% instead of 10% for foils over five dollars.

StoredDerived
Change one ruleRewrite millions of rows. Minutes to hours, and it can half-failUpdate one row. Instant
Market price movesRewrite every affected row againNothing to do
"Why is this card $3.03?"You cannot tell. The number lost its reasonName the rule that produced it
Reading one cardOne index lookupEvaluate rules per read
Reading sixty cards for a search pageOne querySixty evaluations, unless you are careful

Derived wins on every row except the last two, and the last two are exactly where a naive implementation dies. So the real design is not "derived or stored". It is:

The shape that actually works Derived is the source of truth. Materialised is a cache with a publish time. The API evaluates rules live, so a rule change is visible immediately. A nightly job materialises the same numbers into a snapshot table and publishes them outward to Shopify, because a sync cannot re-evaluate a rule engine a million times. When they disagree, the live evaluation is right and the snapshot is stale — and you can say by exactly how long, because the snapshot carries its publish time.

You can see both halves in the real system. The API returns a live buy price on /inventory/search. The catalogue change log says "Changes will be reflected on Inventory tomorrow at 6.00 am" — that is the materialised half, on a daily cadence.

3. The rule model — a filter plus two percentages

This is the whole model, and it is smaller than you would guess.

A RULE ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐ │ type sell │ buy │ stop-buy │ │ │ │ FILTER — which cards does this rule touch? │ │ game Magic: The Gathering │ │ market price >= $5.00 │ │ condition LP │ │ rarity All │ │ print variant Foil │ │ stock Any │ │ │ │ OUTPUT — what does it pay? │ │ cash 10 % │ │ credit 15 % │ └─────────────────────────────────────────────┘ matched 746,555 items

verified Those are the real filter axes and the real numbers from one active rule on a live tenant. A second active rule matched 2,830 items. Five automations existed in total.

Two things are worth noticing immediately.

Credit pays more than cash. 15% versus 10%. That is not generosity — store credit costs the shop less, because it comes back as a sale at retail margin. The ratio is a lever the owner tunes, and on the observed tenant it was exactly 2:3.

The percentages are of market price, not of sell price. That sounds obvious and it is the thing everyone gets backwards. A card that sells for $34 might be bought for $3. The shop's margin on a trade-in is enormous compared with its margin on a wholesale purchase, and that is the entire economics of a card shop.

4. Three rule types, not one

The most commonly missed fact about this engine.

TypeWhat it doesWhy it exists
Sell Sets the retail price: market price × a percentage Ordinary repricing. Keeps the shelf in line with the market without anyone typing
Buy Sets what the shop pays, in cash and in credit The trade-in offer
Stop buy Refuses to buy the matched cards, whatever any Buy rule says "We already have eleven of these." A shop's shelf space and cash are finite
Stop-buy is the one that proves it is a real product A pricing engine written by someone who has not run a shop has two rule types. A third type that exists purely to say no — and which must therefore override a rule that says yes — only gets built after a shop owner has been stuck with sixty copies of a card nobody wants. When you see a negative rule type in a pricing system, someone with a warehouse asked for it.

Above all three sits a global Price rounding control. It applies last, to whatever the rules produced. That matters more than it sounds — see §7.

5. No rule means no offer, and that is a feature

On a live tenant, a card with no matching rule returned activeBuyRule: __UNSET__ and a blank buy price — rendered as —/—.

The instinct is to call that a data-coverage bug. It is not. A card with no buy rule is the shop saying it does not want that card. Blank is the answer, and the correct front-end behaviour is to say "we're not currently buying this card" rather than to show an empty field or fall back to a guess.

The number you must not quote An early version of this discovery measured buy-rule coverage at 10.8% — 18,077 of 167,311 rows — and concluded the feature would launch mostly blank. The author retracted it three weeks later: it was a dev-tenant figure produced by a single test rule, and never a production measurement. It is a perfect example of a number that is precise, memorable, quotable, and wrong. If you repeat it in a room with someone who works there, you are telling them something false about their own product.

6. The inversion — why mint cards get refused

Here is a consequence of the model that nobody designed and everybody has to live with.

The observed active rule filtered on Condition = LP — Lightly Played. Only LP. So:

A customer brings in two copies of the same card Near Mint copy ── no rule matches ──▶ no offer "we're not buying this" Lightly Played ── rule matches ──▶ $3.03 cash / $4.54 credit The BETTER card is the one the shop refuses.

Nothing is broken. The rule engine did exactly what it was told. But the customer experience is incoherent, and the person who wrote the rule almost certainly did not picture this moment — they were thinking "let me test a rule on LP foils" and it became the live configuration.

This is a great thing to notice out loud It is a design consequence, not a bug, and saying so is the difference between a bug report and an engineering opinion. The fix is not code. It is either a rule-authoring UI that warns when a rule covers a worse condition but not a better one, or a validation that a Buy rule set must be monotonic across the condition ladder. Both are product decisions. Naming which one you would argue for is the answer they are listening for.

7. The test that failed — and why it is the best fact in the course

Someone had a clean theory: buy price is a percentage of market price, both of which the API returns, so we can compute it ourselves and stop depending on the vendor.

They tested it against one real card.

CashCredit
Rule says 10% / 15% of marketPrice A$34.02A$3.40A$5.10
Actual, from the APIA$3.03A$4.54
Ratio between the two outputs2 : 3 — exact, in both the prediction and the actual
Base price implied by the actual~A$30.28, not the A$34.02 the API exposes

verified, n=1. Now read what that pattern actually says.

The ratio is exact. 3.03 : 4.54 is 2:3, and 10% : 15% is 2:3. So the rule engine is applying the rule precisely as documented. Nothing is fuzzy or rounded away at the percentage step.

The base is wrong. It is off by about 11%. That is far too large to be rounding and far too consistent to be noise.

The conclusion

"They price off a market figure they don't expose. The marketPrice in the API is a display number — probably a trend or a high — while the engine uses something more conservative underneath. So the fallback of computing it yourself is dead, not because the rule is secret, but because the input is."

Why this matters more than a correct guess would have A failed prediction, reported honestly, with the failure analysed, is a stronger signal than any right answer. It shows someone formed a hypothesis, tested it against reality, and let reality win. In an interview, "we tested our own assumption and it failed, and here is what the failure told us" is worth three confident claims. Most candidates never test their assumptions at all.

8. The nightly run

The catalogue change log on a live tenant, verbatim:

Last updated today 9.22 pm Changes will be reflected on Inventory tomorrow at 6.00 am Magic: The Gathering 38,715 updates 3 new sets added 403 items corrected 389 out of 38,309 prices moved >= 30%

Four things fall out of that box, and together they kill a very expensive assumption.

ReadingNumberSo
CadenceDaily, published at 06:00Not hourly, not streaming. A batch with a known publish time
Share of the catalogue that moves38,309 of ~869,531 MTG cards = ~4.4%A given card is usually unchanged day to day
Share that moves hard389 = ~0.04%Big swings are rare enough to alert on individually
Non-price changes403 corrections, 3 new sets, plus image and duplicate workThe feed carries data quality, not only prices
The assumption this destroys A project plan for integrating with this platform was written around "hourly price updates for 100,000+ SKUs" and treated it as the primary performance risk. The real cadence is a daily batch in which 96% of rows do not change. An entire performance workstream was scoped against a number nobody had checked. Measure the feed before you architect for it.

It also softens a design worry. If only one card in twenty-three moves on a given day, a quote you gave a customer yesterday is very likely still right. A 24 to 48 hour quote validity is a reasonable business choice rather than a dangerous one — and note that this is a catalogue movement figure. Buy price moves when the market moves or when staff edit a rule, and the rule-edit rate was never measured. gap

9. Build it — schema and evaluation order

NestJS and Postgres, as decided in the mission. Start with the tables, because the tables are where the "derived" decision either holds or leaks.

The atom

The priced thing is not a card. It is a printing in a condition — that is what the real system gives its own id to, which you can see in the SKU: each of the five conditions carries a different card id.

card          id, game, name, oracle_text, rarity, image_url
printing      id, card_id, set_code, collector_no, finish        -- finish: normal | foil
sku           id, printing_id, condition                          -- NM|LP|MP|HP|DMG
                                                                  -- THIS is the priced atom
market_price  sku_id, source, amount, as_of                       -- the only stored price
inventory     sku_id, location_id, qty

Model finish on printing, not on sku. That mirrors what they did — a foil card is a separate product in Shopify, not a variant — and it is correct, because a foil is a physically different printing, not a different state of the same object.

The rules

price_rule
  id            uuid
  tenant_id     uuid
  type          'sell' | 'buy' | 'stop_buy'
  priority      int          -- lower wins. See the gap below
  active        bool
  filter        jsonb        -- game, market_min, market_max, conditions[],
                             -- rarities[], finishes[], stock_min, stock_max
  cash_pct      numeric      -- buy only
  credit_pct    numeric      -- buy only
  sell_pct      numeric      -- sell only

tenant_pricing_setting
  tenant_id     uuid
  rounding_mode 'none' | 'nearest' | 'up' | 'down' | 'charm'
  rounding_step numeric      -- e.g. 0.05, 0.25, 0.50
A real gap you have to decide yourself What happens when two Buy rules match the same card? The live tenant had five automations and two active Buy rules, but their precedence was never observed. gap So this is a design decision you own, and it is worth stating which you would pick: most specific filter wins (elegant, but "specific" is hard to define across six axes), or an explicit priority integer (ugly, obvious, debuggable, and what a shop owner can actually reason about). Take the integer. Pricing bugs cost money, and money-path code should be boring.

The evaluation, in order

quoteBuy(sku, location) -> { cash, credit } | null 1 market = marketPriceOf(sku) if market is null -> return null (no feed, no offer) 2 stop = matchRules('stop_buy', sku, market, location) if stop is not empty -> return null (refused on purpose) 3 buy = matchRules('buy', sku, market, location) if buy is empty -> return null ("not buying this card") 4 rule = lowestPriority(buy) 5 base = pricingBaseFor(sku) <-- NOT market cash = base * rule.cash_pct credit = base * rule.credit_pct 6 cash = round(cash, setting) credit = round(credit, setting) 7 return { cash, credit, ruleId: rule.id }

Three things about that pipeline are worth defending out loud.

Step 2 comes before step 3. Stop-buy is a veto, not a competitor. If you evaluate it alongside Buy rules and pick by priority, a badly-priced rule can outrank the shop's own refusal to buy. Veto first, always.

Steps 3 and 5 return the same thing for different reasons. Both produce "no offer", but one means the shop declines and the other means we have no data. Return them as distinct reasons even though the customer sees the same message, because in six weeks someone will ask "how many cards are we refusing versus how many are we missing prices for" and you will want to answer without a migration.

Step 5 is deliberately not market. That is the finding from §7 encoded as a function boundary. pricingBaseFor() starts as return market and carries a comment saying it is known to be wrong by about 11% on the one card tested. When someone finally runs the two-hundred-row experiment, exactly one function changes.

The two jobs

JobWhenWhat it does
ingest-market-feedNightly, before the publish windowUpsert market_price. Record how many rows changed and by how much — that count is the number in §8, and it is your best health signal
publish-prices06:00Re-evaluate sell and buy for every sku with stock or a recent market move, write price_snapshot, then hand the changed set to the sync

Note the filter on the second job: with stock or a recent market move. Re-evaluating 1.12 million items nightly when 96% of them did not move is the difference between a job that finishes and a job that does not.

The invariant worth a test A snapshot row must always name the rule that produced it. price_snapshot.rule_id is not optional. Six weeks from now the question is never "what is the price" — it is "why is it that", asked by an angry shop owner. A price with no traceable reason is an unanswerable support ticket, and you will get one per week forever.
