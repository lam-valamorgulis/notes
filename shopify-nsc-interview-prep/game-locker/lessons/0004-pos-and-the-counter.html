The Counter They Do Not Own

"Who owns the transaction?" is the organising question for any retail system. At Game Locker the answer is the interesting one: none of the three systems in the room. A merchant's own till owns it — so Game Locker is not even the first to know that a card was sold.

Your win today: answer "who owns the transaction?" correctly and say why that is unusual, count the two hops between the counter and the website, explain what the kiosk actually is and why it needs committed — and know that pickup in store is POS Pro only, billed per location.

Ties to your 24-hour plan: block 6. Builds on Lesson 3, POS L01 and POS L04. Rewritten 2026-08-06 after a fact check — see the note at the end of §1.

1. Who owns the transaction?

From POS L01: every retail system sorts into "nothing to sync" or "two systems of truth", and the question that sorts it is who creates the order and takes the money.

Run the three candidates. The answer is the reason this role exists.

WHO OWNS THE TRANSACTION AT A GAME LOCKER STORE? Shopify POS? NO. Their merchants are not using it at the counter. Game Locker? NO. It is not a point of sale at all. Its site lists inventory, pricing, orders, customers, analytics and a self-service kiosk — no till, no cash drawer, no payment. A THIRD-PARTY ✅ YES. Binder POS, Storepass, or TILL similar. Game Locker publishes CSV IMPORT TEMPLATES for exactly those, which is the tell. SO: the system that knows FIRST belongs to NEITHER of the two companies in the room.
Why this is the most important sentence in the lesson Most integration jobs have two systems and you own the pipe between them. This one has three, and the first hop is not yours. So when a merchant asks "how fast does the website know?", the honest answer has two numbers in it — and a candidate who gives one number has not understood the shape of the problem.
Honesty note — this lesson was rewritten on 2026-08-06 An earlier version said Game Locker sold a POS kiosk that owned the counter. That was wrong, and it was wrong about their own product. Re-read from gamelocker.co: the kiosk is customer-facing self-service (§6), and Game Locker is not a POS. Never say "your kiosk owns the transaction" in the interview. The corrected picture is a stronger answer, and the whole lesson is now built on it. The extended version is the Shopify Data Sync sub-course.

2. Why a card shop needs a card-specific till

Be able to argue their merchants' side. It is a real argument, and it shows you understand the domain rather than just the platform.

Card shop workflowDoes Shopify POS do it?
Buylist — buying cards from customers over the counter, at a price derived from the day's market rateNo. Shopify POS sells; it does not run a purchasing desk with per-card offer prices.
Grading and condition assessment at intake, with the price adjusted per conditionNo — this is domain logic that lives in the card system.
Scan a card and identify the printing out of a million-plus possibilitiesNo. This is Game Locker's actual product.
Trade-ins — cards in, store credit out, in one transactionAwkward at best.
Singles pricing that moves daily from market feedsNo — Shopify holds a price; it does not source one.
Tournament entry, event ticketing, player registrationNo.

So the separate till is not an accident. A card store's counter does things a general retail POS was never built to do.

And notice what Game Locker chose not to build Game Locker does inventory, pricing, orders, customers and analytics — and deliberately not the till. Instead of competing with Binder POS it publishes import templates for it. That is a product decision worth respecting out loud: they picked the layer where their 145-million-card catalogue is the advantage, and left the cash drawer to somebody else. Saying that shows you looked at their strategy, not just their API.

3. The awkward question, asked properly

You will be tempted to ask "why not just use Shopify POS? It would delete the whole sync problem." That is technically true and socially wrong — it tells a merchant their choice of till is a mistake, before you know anything about their shop.

Ask it from the other end instead:

The way to raise it

"Since your merchants keep their own till rather than moving to Shopify POS, I assume that is deliberate — the buylist and grading workflows probably need it. What I'd want to understand is what that costs you on the Shopify side, because a third party owning the counter is what creates the sync problem in the first place."

That does three things at once: it credits the arrangement, it shows you know the trade-off, and it lands the conversation exactly where your strength is.

Memorise this. It is the line that separates you from other candidates.

"The widest gap in this chain is the one I do not own. So I would build my half properly and measure the hop in front of it and report that number too — even though it does not flatter me — because it is the number the merchant actually feels. Then the conversation about automating that import is a business conversation with evidence behind it, instead of a complaint."

The promise you must not make "A counter sale disappears from the website in a few seconds" is impossible here, and it is exactly what a keen candidate says. There is no counter-sale event — you are downstream of a file. Promising seconds means promising something that depends on whether somebody remembered to run an export. Give two numbers instead, and say which one is yours.

Game Locker's kiosk is real and worth understanding properly, because it is the part most people will get wrong — including, until recently, this lesson.

Why this is a strong thing to raise unprompted It shows you know the inventory states are not trivia — they encode the difference between physical reality and commercial permission. And it lands on their own product: the kiosk is Game Locker's feature, so getting committed right is squarely their problem, not the till vendor's. That makes it a contribution rather than a criticism.

7. The counter-orders decision

A genuine fork in the road, and a great thing to have an opinion about.

When a card sells at the counter, should an order appear in Shopify?

Option A — stock onlyOption B — write the order too
What you pushJust the new stock levelA full order, via orderCreate
Shopify reportingOnline sales onlyAll sales, one place
Customer historyOnline purchases onlyComplete — good for marketing
RiskReporting lives in Game Locker, and Shopify's numbers are partialMoney is now recorded twice. Financial reports, tax and payouts can double-count. Order edits and refunds must be mirrored forever
EffortLowHigh, and permanently high

My recommendation, and the reasoning matters more than the choice: start with A, and only move to B for a specific named business need — most often "marketing wants one customer view for email segmentation".

The reason is that option B moves a money-shaped problem into a system that is not the book of record for that money. Financial double-counting is a much worse class of bug than incomplete reporting, and it is discovered by an accountant rather than by you.

And the third system makes option B worse than it looks You would not be minting those orders from a live till feed. You would be minting them from an imported CSV — so every synthetic Shopify order inherits whatever the import got wrong, arrives hours late with a timestamp you have to set by hand, and has no reliable link back to the till's own record for reconciliation. Writing money into a third system from a file is a worse idea than writing it from an event. That is a specific reason to prefer A here, beyond the general one.
This is a "trade-offs to non-technical stakeholders" answer The non-technical framing: "we can give marketing one complete customer view, but the cost is that every sale is recorded in two systems and finance has to trust that they always agree. I'd rather solve the marketing need directly than take on a money-duplication risk to get it."

8. Click and collect — and the cost fact

The JD names click and collect explicitly. On Shopify, "collect in store" is local pickup, and there is a commercial fact attached that matters more than the technical one.

FactDetail
Multi-location inventoryAvailable on POS Lite — you do not need Pro to track stock per shop.
Pickup in storePOS Pro only, billed per location. A merchant with 4 shops pays for 4.
How a pickup order is shapedA fulfillment order with a PICK_UP delivery method. Stock becomes committed, not available.
An over-the-counter saleRETAIL — one of the delivery method types. A sale with no fulfilment leg at all.
Why the pricing fact is the useful one Their customers are small game stores. "Click and collect requires POS Pro, and Pro is billed per location" is a real budget conversation, and it is likely a support question they hear often. Knowing it means you can answer a merchant question without escalating — directly relevant to "work with our marketing team and customers". Most candidates know the API shape and not the invoice.
The awkward follow-up, and the honest answer If a merchant's counter is Binder POS, who hands the card over at pickup? Shopify created the pickup order and holds the stock as committed, but the staff member at the counter is working in a different system. So a pickup either needs the staff to work in Shopify for that one action, or it needs the pickup order pushed into Game Locker so it appears in the tools they actually use. That is a workflow question, not an API question, and asking it shows you are thinking about the person behind the counter rather than the diagram.

9. Bursts, not streams

The last point, and it is the one that marks retail experience.

Shop wifi drops. A card shop on a Saturday tournament day has forty people on the guest network — so a till must be able to take money offline, and every card-shop POS can. Then it catches up in a burst.

Put that together with hop 1 and you get the same shape twice:

TWO SEPARATE REASONS THE DATA ARRIVES IN BURSTS 1. the till was OFFLINE and queued sales locally 2. the import is a BATCH by design — a file, hourly or daily, not an event per sale EITHER WAY, WHAT REACHES YOU IS: a pile of changes, all at once, ALREADY OLD. CONSEQUENCES YOU MUST DESIGN FOR: · the oversell window is not "my sync interval". It is "how long since the last import" — and on a bad day, "how long the wifi was down". · replaying a stale burst of DELTAS is catastrophic: each decrement applies again to a moved number. → ABSOLUTE writes are not elegance, they are the only thing that survives this. · compareQuantity will REJECT the stale ones. That is correct. The worker must RE-READ and resolve, not treat a rejection as an error to alert on. · a burst of 400 messages for 90 cards should COLLAPSE to 90 writes, newest per card.

This connects straight back to Lesson 3. Absolute writes with compareQuantity are the right call not for theoretical reasons but because you are permanently replaying old truth against a moved target — and here that is guaranteed by the architecture, not just by bad luck.

One honest note about the POS UI Extensions bonus point The JD lists Shopify POS UI Extensions under Bonus Points, and it is worth thinking about when they would even run. A POS UI Extension runs inside Shopify POS — so it only helps the merchants who use Shopify POS at the counter. If most of theirs are on Binder POS or Storepass, that bonus applies to a subset of stores, or to a direction they are heading rather than where they are. Asking which it is is a better move than either claiming the skill or dismissing the line. Your honest position on having authored one is in Lesson 8 and POS L08.
