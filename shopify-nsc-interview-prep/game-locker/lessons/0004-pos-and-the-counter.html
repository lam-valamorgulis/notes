POS and the Counter

A Game Locker store's counter runs on a third-party till, not on Game Locker and not on Shopify POS. That is the tension, and how you talk about it is the most senior thing you can do in this interview.

Your win today: explain what a merchant gives up by keeping their own till, give an honest recommendation that does not insult the product they sell, and know that pickup in store is POS Pro only and billed per location — a cost fact their customers ask about constantly.

Ties to your 24-hour plan: block 6. Builds on Lesson 3 and on POS L04.

Corrected 2026-08-06 — this lesson's premise changed, and the conclusion survives An earlier version said Game Locker sells a POS kiosk, so their kiosk owns the counter. That was wrong. Their site describes the kiosk as customer-facing self-service — collectors browse stock and submit orders and buylists, no money moves — and Game Locker is not a point of sale at all. It publishes CSV import templates for tills like Binder POS and Storepass, which is what actually runs a shop's counter.

Everything this lesson concludes still holds, and gets stronger: a third-party system owns the transaction, Shopify is downstream, and the oversell window exists. It is simply wider than described here, because the till's data reaches Game Locker by import rather than instantly — so there are two hops, not one, and the first one belongs to nobody in the room.

Never say "your kiosk owns the transaction" in the interview. Say "a merchant's own till owns the transaction, so you are two hops from Shopify." The full corrected treatment is the Shopify Data Sync sub-course, especially its Lesson 2.

1. The awkward question, asked properly

A Game Locker store's counter runs on a third-party till such as Binder POS or Storepass — Game Locker publishes CSV import templates for exactly those. Shopify also has a POS. A candidate who has thought about the role will notice this and be tempted to say "why not just use Shopify POS?"

Do not say that. Their merchants chose those tills for real reasons, and Game Locker's whole value sits in that same card-specific domain. Asking why the arrangement exists sounds like telling them their market is wrong, before you know anything about their customers.

Ask it the other way round instead:

The way to raise it

"Since a merchant's counter runs on their own till rather than on Shopify POS, I assume that is deliberate — the card-specific workflows probably need it. What I'd want to understand is what that costs you on the Shopify side, because owning the counter is what creates the sync problem in the first place."

That sentence does three things at once: it credits their design, it shows you know the trade-off, and it puts the conversation exactly where your strength is.

2. Why a card shop genuinely needs its own till

Worth being able to argue their side, because it is a real argument and it shows you understand the domain rather than just the platform.

Card shop workflowDoes Shopify POS do it?
Buylist — buying cards from customers over the counter, at a price derived from the day's market rateNo. Shopify POS sells; it does not run a purchasing desk with per-card offer prices.
Grading and condition assessment at intake, with the price adjusted per conditionNo — this is domain logic that lives in the card system.
Scan a card and identify the printing out of ~145M possibilitiesNo. This is Game Locker's actual product.
Trade-ins — cards in, store credit out, in one transactionAwkward at best.
Singles pricing that moves daily from market feedsNo — Shopify holds a price; it does not source one.
Tournament entry, event ticketing, player registrationNo.

So the separate till is not an accident. A card store's counter does things a general retail POS was never built to do, and Game Locker's whole value is that domain logic. Being able to say that means you are arguing for their design better than they expected you to.

3. What owning the counter costs them

Now the other half, which is your value. Every one of these is free with Shopify POS and must be built and maintained when a third-party till owns the transaction.

The reframe that makes you valuable rather than critical Do not present this list as "your architecture has problems". Present it as "this list is the job description". Every cost on the right side is a thing they are paying an engineer to handle — you. That turns a criticism into an accurate statement of what you would own.

4. The counter-orders decision

One item on that list deserves its own section, because it is a genuine fork in the road and a great thing to have an opinion about.

When a card sells at the counter, should an order appear in Shopify?

Option A — stock onlyOption B — write the order too
What you pushJust the new stock levelA full order, via orderCreate
Shopify reportingOnline sales onlyAll sales, one place
Customer historyOnline purchases onlyComplete — good for marketing
RiskReporting lives in Game Locker, and Shopify's numbers are partialMoney is now recorded twice. Financial reports, tax and payouts can double-count. Order edits and refunds must be mirrored forever
EffortLowHigh, and permanently high

My recommendation, and the reasoning matters more than the choice: start with A, and only move to B for a specific named business need — most often "marketing wants one customer view for email segmentation".

The reason is that option B moves a money-shaped problem into a system that is not the book of record for that money. Financial double-counting is a much worse class of bug than incomplete reporting, and it is discovered by an accountant rather than by you.

This is a "trade-offs to non-technical stakeholders" answer The JD asks for someone who can explain technical trade-offs to non-technical people. This is that question in its natural habitat. The non-technical framing is: "we can give marketing one complete customer view, but the cost is that every sale is recorded in two systems and finance has to trust that they always agree. I'd rather solve the marketing need directly than take on a money-duplication risk to get it."

5. Click and collect — and the cost fact

The JD names click and collect explicitly. On Shopify, "collect in store" is local pickup, and there is a commercial fact attached that matters more than the technical one.

FactDetail
Multi-location inventoryAvailable on POS Lite — you do not need Pro to track stock per shop.
Pickup in storePOS Pro only, billed per location. A merchant with 4 shops pays for 4.
How a pickup order is shapedA fulfillment order with a PICK_UP delivery method. Stock becomes committed, not available.
An over-the-counter saleRETAIL — one of the delivery method types. A sale with no fulfilment leg at all.
Why the pricing fact is the useful one Their customers are small game stores. "Click and collect requires POS Pro, and Pro is billed per location" is a real budget conversation, and it is likely a support question they hear often. Knowing it means you can answer a merchant question without escalating — which is directly relevant to "work with our marketing team and customers". Most candidates know the API shape and not the invoice.

The committed-stock subtlety

When a pickup order is placed, the unit moves from available to committed. It is still physically in the shop but is no longer sellable — which is exactly right, and exactly the thing a third-party kiosk will get wrong.

A COLLECTOR RESERVES A CARD FOR PICKUP Shopify: available 1 → 0, committed 0 → 1 the card is spoken for. Correct. Game Locker: still shows 1 on hand… and it IS on hand. Physically present. Not sellable. ⚠ IF THE KIOSK ONLY LOOKS AT "how many are here?", staff will sell a card that is already paid for by someone driving over to collect it. The kiosk needs the difference between ON HAND (it is in the building) and AVAILABLE (you may sell it) — which is exactly the distinction the 8 inventory states exist to express.

This is a strong thing to raise unprompted. It shows you know the inventory states are not trivia — they encode the difference between physical reality and commercial permission.

6. POS UI Extensions — the bonus point, answered honestly

The JD lists "Experience with Shopify POS UI Extensions (React based)" under Bonus Points. Two things to get right here: the facts, and the honesty.

The facts

  • They are built with Preact and TSX plus a TOML config, and rendered to native POS components through remote-dom — not a web view.
  • Extension targets decide where your UI appears: a tile on the smart grid, an action, or a block.
  • The Cart API can write — add line items, apply discounts, set attributes. But an individual Cart Line Item is read-only.
  • There are Connectivity and Storage APIs, and they exist for a specific reason: shop wifi drops. An extension that assumes a live connection will fail in a real shop.
  • Direct API Access lets an extension call fetch("shopify:admin/api/graphql.json") and talk to the Admin API without your own backend in the middle.

The honesty

Your position, stated cleanly and without apology:

The honesty line — say it exactly like this

"I haven't authored a POS UI Extension myself. My agency shipped one and I've read it closely — an inventory-transfer app — so I know the architecture, and I found real bugs in it. What I don't have is the experience of getting one through review and onto a shop floor."

Then follow it with the specific thing you learned, because that is what converts a gap into evidence of judgment:

What you found in itWhat it teaches
Direct API Access used 33 times, the app's own backend 0 times — the server existed only for OAuthExtensions can be almost backend-free. That is a real architectural choice with real consequences for auth and rate limits.
A 6-mutation send path with no rollback, padded with fixed sleep() callsMulti-step writes with no compensating action leave half-finished state. Sleeps are not synchronisation.
A success banner set inside a finally block, on a function returning null on both paths, reading userErrors at the wrong response pathA totally failed receive still showed "Transfer received". The worst kind of bug: it tells the user the opposite of the truth.
Scanner de-dupe via useRef rather than useStateBarcode scanners fire fast and repeatedly. State updates are async; a ref is not.
Why this beats claiming the experience "I read one closely and here are three defects I found, with the reasons they are defects" is a stronger signal than "yes I've built them" — because it is checkable, it is specific, and it demonstrates code review, which the JD asks for by name. Claiming authorship you do not have is also the one thing that can end an interview badly if they probe.

7. Offline is a constraint, not a feature

Last point, and it is the one that marks retail experience. Shop wifi drops. A card shop on a Saturday tournament day has forty people on the guest network.

So the counter cannot depend on the network being up to take money. That has a consequence people miss:

OFFLINE CHANGES THE SHAPE OF THE PROBLEM The kiosk MUST be able to sell while offline. Therefore stock decrements QUEUE UP locally. Therefore when it reconnects, it pushes a BURST of changes that are already MINUTES OR HOURS OLD. Consequences you must design for: · the oversell window is not "the sync interval" — it is "however long the wifi was down" · a delta-based sync replaying a stale burst is catastrophic → absolute writes matter even more · compareQuantity will REJECT stale writes, which is correct behaviour, so the worker must re-read and resolve rather than treat rejection as failure

This connects directly back to Lesson 3. The reason absolute writes with compareQuantity are the right call is not theoretical elegance — it is that a shop's network will go down and you will be replaying old truth against a moved target.
