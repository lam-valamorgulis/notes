Lesson 7 · Game Locker · Capstone

The Game Locker Interview

Every bullet in their job description mapped to something you can say. The three question shapes, the six answers that lose the job, the five questions to ask them, and a rubric to mark yourself against.

Your win today: for every line of the job description, have one sentence ready that is true. Then run a full mock, note what made you stall, and put only that on the one-pager.

Ties to your 24-hour plan: block 23. Do this after the speaking drills, not before — the drills give you the shape, this gives you the content.

1. The three shapes every question takes

ShapeSounds likeWhat they are testingYour move
Platform recall "What's the difference between the Admin and Storefront API?" Whether you actually use Shopify or read about it Be precise. Real numbers, real names. Then add one thing they did not ask — the why behind it.
Design / judgment "How would you keep inventory in sync?" Whether you have shipped something that had to survive Ask a question first. Then ownership, then failure modes, then trade-offs. Draw it.
Behaviour / ownership "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a decision." Whether you are safe to give a whole area to One real story. What you decided, not what the team decided.
The tell that separates the shapes If the question has one right answer, it is recall — be fast and exact. If it starts with "how would you", it is judgment — slow down and ask a clarifying question first, because answering a design question immediately is the most common way senior candidates sound junior.

If the TCG question worries you You do not need to be a player. What they actually need is someone who understands that a card's identity is set-plus-printing, that condition and language change the price, and that a graded slab is a one-of-one object. That is domain modelling, and it is more useful to them than knowing the current Standard metagame. If asked directly whether you play: say the truth, then pivot to the modelling — "I'm not a player, but the data problem is unusually interesting: nine attributes and only three option slots."

3. Six answers that lose the job

Do not sayWhy it costs youSay instead
"I'd sync in real time both ways so they're always consistent." Real-time two-way consistency across two systems is not achievable. It signals you have never run one. Name a direction of ownership, an accepted lag, and a reconciliation process.
"Just use Shopify POS instead of your kiosk." Their kiosk is the product they sell, and a card counter needs buylist and grading that Shopify POS has no concept of. "I assume the kiosk is deliberate for the card workflows — what does owning the counter cost you on the Shopify side?"
"I'd put all 145 million cards in Shopify with bulk operations." Confuses the catalogue with the stock. Millions of dead pages, no sales. "Shopify holds what the store actually has. The catalogue stays with you for search, matching and pricing."
"Marketplace Kit would handle the multi-vendor part." It is deprecated. Naming a withdrawn tool as your plan is worse than saying you do not know. "There is no native multi-vendor path — Marketplace Kit was deprecated. So it is an app or a custom build, and here are the three shapes."
"I've worked on POS integrations." (if you did not build them) Probing will find the edge, and then every other claim gets re-examined. "I've maintained themes that consume a third-party stock system, and I've read our POS extension closely. I haven't authored one."
"I'd add a 30-minute sync job and that's fine." On stock levels of 1, thirty minutes is many oversells a week. Push on the event, keep the job as a safety net, and re-verify at checkout.

4. The five questions to ask them

Asking nothing reads as not caring. These five are chosen because each one demonstrates something while genuinely getting you information you want.

QuestionWhat it shows
"Is the marketplace meant to run on Shopify, or is Shopify one channel feeding it?" You spotted the biggest ambiguity in the whole JD. It genuinely cannot be told from the text.
"For the marketplace — does a buyer need one cart across several sellers?" You know that single answer eliminates an entire architecture.
"When a store wants something the shared theme doesn't do, where do you draw the line between a new theme setting and custom work for that store?" You understand the multi-tenant theme problem is the real operational tension.
"Today, when the kiosk and Shopify disagree on stock, how do you find out — and who notices first, you or the merchant?" The best question on the list. It goes straight at observability and reconciliation, and their answer tells you how mature the integration really is.
"What does the first version of the marketplace have to prove?" You think in scope and launch risk, not just features.
Ask question 4 even if you only ask one "Who notices first, you or the merchant?" is the question a person who has operated a sync asks. If they answer "the merchant tells us", you have just found the most valuable thing you could do in your first month — and you can say so, which turns your question into a mini pitch.

5. The closing 30 seconds

Interviews end with "do you have anything to add?" Have something. Not a summary of your CV — one sentence about the problem, which is what they will remember.

A close to adapt

"The thing that makes this interesting to me is that you have two systems that both legitimately write the same number, and real money on both sides of it. That is the kind of problem I would rather work on than a bigger, tidier one. If I joined, the first thing I'd want to know is how you find out today when the two disagree."
