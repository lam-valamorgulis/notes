The Sync Engine

The job description names four things that must stay in sync — inventory, orders, products, customers. Give each one a single owner and a single direction, and the hard problem shrinks to one: the minute after a card sells at the counter.

Your win today: draw the ownership matrix from memory, explain why two-way sync of the same field is a bug rather than a feature, and narrate the oversell minute by minute — then name the four defences and which one you would ship first.

Ties to your 24-hour plan: block 5, the centre of the whole day. Read POS L01 and POS L03 first — this lesson assumes the three writes and the 8 inventory states.

If you have more than one block for this This page is the compressed version, built for a 24-hour deadline. The Shopify Data Sync sub-course is the same subject over eleven lessons, followed forward through one card shop — and it covers seven things this page does not: the gap you do not own (a merchant's till reaches the platform by CSV import, so the widest delay is upstream of this whole diagram), deletes and tombstones, clock disagreement, field-level ownership, what to do when the queue is backed up, telling lag from drift, and four hundred shops sharing one worker pool. Use this page to revise; use that one to go deeper.

1. Start by refusing to say "sync"

"Keep the two systems in sync" is not a specification. It hides the only question that matters: when they disagree, who is right?

Until that is answered per field, every design discussion goes in circles, and every bug becomes unfixable — because both systems are behaving correctly according to their own rules.

Say this early in any sync conversation

"Before I design anything I want to name, for each piece of data, which system owns it. Sync is cheap when there is one owner and one direction. It is close to impossible when both sides can write the same field."

Read down the "Direction" column. Five of the six are one-way. That is not luck — it is the design goal. The one row that is genuinely two-way is inventory, and that single row is why this job exists.

Corrected 2026-08-17 — the direction of the inventory row An earlier version of this table gave inventory to Game Locker with Shopify as a secondary writer. A live integration says the emphasis is the other way round. Their own team, in writing: "you would also never need to read/update inventory from our side, as we are synced with your Shopify products, so you just need to manage stock updates from that end and it syncs with us." And separately: "the Shopify integration is two-way updating. So if we update inventory on Shopify, it updates inventory in GameLocker."

So Shopify is where stock is authored, and the platform follows. Two consequences worth carrying:

One — it removes a credential problem. Accepting a trade-in means incrementing stock in Shopify. No write access to the platform is needed for the stock half at all. That is genuinely convenient and it is why one design in B04 survives with zero vendor dependency.

Two — it does not make the oversell go away. Everything in §5 still holds. The counter is still two hops upstream through a third-party till, and that hop is the widest delay in the whole picture. Being told "manage stock in Shopify" does not help when the sale happened somewhere Shopify has not heard about yet.
And one thing now proven rather than assumed The integration app is real and identified. Shopify's own Product.events log carries appTitle "Game Locker" with attributeToApp true and messages like "Game Locker created a new product: …". App attribution is written by Shopify, so it is not inferable from naming conventions — it is the platform telling you who did it. There is no public App Store listing, so it is a custom or unlisted app. See verified-facts §7.
Customers deserve a real answer, not a shrug Both systems create customers — Shopify at checkout, Game Locker at the counter. Matching on email is the obvious move and it is imperfect: counter customers often give no email, and one household shares an address. My answer would be: Shopify owns the online customer record, Game Locker owns the in-store one, matched on email where it exists and deliberately left separate where it does not. Forcing a merge on a fuzzy match creates wrong loyalty balances, and a wrong loyalty balance makes a customer angrier than a duplicate row ever does.

Where the storefront fits — and where it does not

One boundary is easy to get wrong, and getting it wrong makes the whole design sound confused. The theme never talks to Game Locker. Liquid reads Shopify's own products, prices and inventory, and nothing else. There are two conversations happening, and they never meet:

COLLECTOR's browser │ loads the product page ▼ SHOPIFY ← the theme (Liquid) reads Shopify's OWN data. ▲ │ It has no idea Game Locker exists. │ │ │ └──► order placed → webhook orders/create ──► GAME LOCKER │ └──── GL pushes products, prices, stock IN (Admin API) Browser ↔ Shopify every page view, public GL ↔ Shopify Admin API server to server, no browser

So on an online sale, almost nothing happens until the very end:

StepWhat Shopify doesDoes anything reach Game Locker?
Product page loadsServes its own last-known dataNo. No call to GL, ever
Add to cartNothing. No stock check, no reservationNo. Nothing at all
Checkout / paymentDecrements its own inventoryNot yet
Order createdFires orders/createYes — this is the first signal
Add to cart promises nothing A cart is a note in the browser. It does not reserve the card, does not reduce any number, and does not tell Game Locker anything — update.js does not even check stock (see §5). Ten collectors can hold the same single card in their carts at once. That is exactly why defence #2 below is re-verify at checkout, not at add-to-cart: the cart never made a promise, so the check has to happen at the moment money moves.

Which means the online direction has precisely one entry point — the order webhook — and Shopify has already decremented its own inventory before you hear about it. Hold that thought, because it is the whole reason the next section exists.

3. The echo loop — the bug that eats a weekend

Inventory being two-way creates a failure mode that is not obvious until it happens, and it is a genuinely strong thing to raise unprompted in an interview.

THE NAIVE DESIGN Shopify sale ──► webhook ──► GL decrements ──► GL pushes ▲ stock to │ Shopify └──────────────────────────────────────────────┘ the push looks like a CHANGE, so something reacts to it again… THE SYMPTOM stock oscillates: 4 → 3 → 4 → 3 or double-decrements: one sale, stock drops by 2 and it only happens under load, which is the worst kind THE FIX — mark the origin of every write every inventory write carries WHERE IT CAME FROM ├── "this came from Game Locker" → do not echo back └── "this came from a Shopify sale" → GL applies it once, keyed by order id

Two mechanisms make this concrete:

  • Idempotency keys. The Shopify order id is a natural one. "Order 1234 already decremented this item" is a row in a table, checked before applying. Webhooks are at-least-once, so you will receive duplicates — this is not optional.
  • Reference documents. Shopify's inventory mutations accept a referenceDocumentUri. Stamping your own origin on every write means an adjustment history you can actually read six weeks later when someone asks why stock went to −3.
Why this is worth raising unprompted Almost nobody mentions echo loops in an interview, because you only learn about them by having one in production. Naming it before you are asked is a strong signal — and if the interviewer has lived through one, you have just described their worst week to them.

4. Absolute or delta? The most practical question in the lesson

Shopify gives you two ways to write inventory, and choosing wrong is the difference between a sync that self-heals and one that drifts forever.

inventorySetQuantitiesinventoryAdjustQuantities
SemanticsAbsolute. "This is now 7."Delta. "Add −1 to whatever is there."
Lost message meansNothing — the next push corrects itPermanent drift. The −1 is gone forever
Duplicate message meansNothing — same value written twiceDouble-decrement. An oversell you caused yourself
Use forThe sync from Game Locker. GL knows the true countA single known event, applied exactly once

So: the recurring sync uses absolute writes. That is the whole point. An absolute write is naturally idempotent — replaying it is harmless — which means a dropped webhook or a retried job is self-correcting rather than corrupting.

The detail that wins the answer: compareQuantity

inventorySetQuantities takes a compareQuantity alongside the new value. Shopify applies the write only if the currently stored quantity still matches what you thought it was. If it has changed underneath you, the mutation fails with a userErrors entry rather than silently overwriting.

COMPARE-AND-SET, IN THE ONE CASE THAT MATTERS 10:00:00 sync reads Shopify: available = 1 10:00:01 a shopper buys it online → Shopify: available = 0 10:00:02 sync writes { quantity: 1, compareQuantity: 1 } WITHOUT compareQuantity → write succeeds. Stock is now 1. IT DOES NOT EXIST. You have resurrected a sold card. WITH compareQuantity → REJECTED. compare was 1, actual is 0. Sync re-reads, sees the sale, and writes the truth instead. You can opt out with ignoreCompareQuantity: true. Know that it exists; know that reaching for it is usually a decision to accept overwriting other writers.

One thing that diagram hides: quantity and compareQuantity are both 1 there, so they look like the same number. They are not.

FieldMeaningWhere the number comes from
quantityThe new value you want storedGame Locker — its own count
compareQuantityThe value you believe Shopify holds right nowShopify — your read a moment earlier

Read a write out loud as one sentence and it stops being confusing: "make it 2, but only if it is still 3."

The same second, drawn properly

This is the version to draw on a whiteboard, because it shows the two roads leaving the rejection. Game Locker has already taken a counter sale, so it holds 2 while Shopify still holds 3 — and the push that would fix that is only now starting.

TIME SYNC WORKER SHOPIFY GL DB ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────── start: Shopify = 3 GL = 2 :00 ─ read stock ──────────────►│ │ ◄──────── "it is 3" ────────│ │ belief = 3 │ │ :01 online shopper pays for 1 available 3 → 2 webhook queued, NOT applied yet :02 ─ inventorySetQuantities ──►│ stored is 2, │ quantity: 2 │ you claimed 3 │ compareQuantity: 3 │ │ ◄──────── REJECTED ─────────│ userErrors │ WITHOUT compareQuantity │ WITH compareQuantity ─────────────────────── │ ──────────────────── The write succeeds. │ Nothing was written. Shopify = 2. │ │ :03 re-read Shopify → 2 The online sale is ERASED. │ :04 webhook lands, GL 2 → 1 That unit does not exist. │ :05 read GL → truth is 1 Shopify will sell it again. │ write { quantity: 1, │ compare: 2 } Shopify = 2 GL = 1 │ ◄── ACCEPTED ▲ │ └─ silent drift. Both │ Shopify = 1 GL = 1 sales counted once, │ ▲ stock counted once, │ └─ correct. Both nobody notices. │ sales counted.
The sentence that lands this compareQuantity is not a fix. It is a stop sign. It does not know the right answer — it only converts a silent wrong write into a visible error your worker can handle. The re-read is what finds the truth.
Memorise this — it is the single best line in the lesson

"The recurring sync writes absolute quantities with compareQuantity, so it is idempotent and it cannot silently trample a sale that happened while the job was thinking. Replaying it is safe, which is what makes the whole system recoverable."

5. The oversell minute

This is the story they will ask for. Learn to narrate it with times, because times are what make it credible.

ONE CARD. QUANTITY 1. A $400 NEAR-MINT SINGLE. 14:05:00 Counter sale. A collector buys it in the shop. Game Locker: available 1 → 0. Correct. Shopify: available STILL 1. Now WRONG. 14:05:30 Someone online opens the product page. Shopify says "In stock". Shopify is not lying — it is reporting what it was last told. 14:06:10 They add to cart. update.js does NOT check stock. 14:07:00 They check out. MONEY IS TAKEN. Shopify: available 1 → 0. 14:20:00 The sync runs. Game Locker says 0. Shopify says 0. ✅ THE NUMBERS AGREE. ❌ AND TWO PEOPLE BOUGHT THE SAME CARD. THE DRIFT IS INVISIBLE AFTERWARDS. That is the point. The reconciliation job will never find this. It is only findable as an unfulfillable order.
The insight most candidates miss The defect is not the slow sync. Shortening the window to ten seconds makes it rarer, never impossible — and "rarer" on a card site still means a real customer every week. The defect is that Shopify promised a unit it did not have. So the fix belongs at the promise, not at the sync interval. Saying this out loud reframes the whole question and it is the difference between a mid-level and a senior answer.

The four defences, in the order I would ship them

#DefenceWhat it costsWhat it buys
1 Push on an event, not a batch. Counter sale fires an immediate push to Shopify instead of waiting for the 15-minute job. Small. The job stays as a safety net. Cuts the window from ~15 minutes to a few seconds. Biggest win for the least work.
2 Re-verify at checkout. Availability is confirmed at the moment of payment, not at add-to-cart. Some work; a worse moment to disappoint someone. Closes the cart-to-checkout gap, which is where the minutes actually go.
3 Buffer high-value stock. Hold the last unit back on expensive singles so it is never offered online as guaranteed. Shopify's own safety_stock state exists for this. Real money. You are choosing not to sell things. Protects the cards where being wrong is most expensive. Needs a business decision on the threshold.
4 Decide the apology in advance. Refund, substitute, or hold for restock — written down, with who contacts the customer. Nearly nothing. Turns an emergency into a procedure. The one everybody forgets.

Notice that only defence 3 costs real money, and it is the only one requiring a business conversation rather than an engineering one. Being able to separate those two categories is what "explain technical trade-offs to non-technical stakeholders" means in the job description.

The nuance for graded cards Defence 3 does not work on a one-of-one slab. Quantity is 1 permanently, so buffering it means never selling it online at all. For slabs the honest answer is a different shape: either they are online-only and physically set aside, or they are counter-only, or the online listing is an enquiry rather than an instant purchase. Recognising that the general rule breaks on your most valuable stock is exactly the kind of specific thinking they are hiring for.

6. Webhooks are not a delivery guarantee

Orders come back from Shopify by webhook. Three facts to have exactly right, because they determine what you must build around them:

  • At-least-once, not exactly-once. You will receive duplicates. Every handler needs an idempotency key — the order id works.
  • Retries are finite. Shopify retries a failing subscription and after 8 failures over roughly 4 hours it removes the subscription. A silent outage past that point means you stop receiving orders and nobody is told.
  • Order is not guaranteed. orders/updated can arrive before orders/create. Handlers must tolerate arriving out of sequence.
THEREFORE: NEVER DO THE WORK IN THE HANDLER webhook ──► verify HMAC ──► write to a queue ──► 200 OK │ (fast, always) ▼ worker does the real work ├── retries with backoff ├── idempotent by order id └── dead-letter after N tries Two reasons this shape is non-negotiable: 1. A slow handler becomes a FAILED handler, and 8 of those loses you the subscription entirely. 2. Work that can be retried must live somewhere that survives the request. A crash mid-handler = lost order.

And because webhooks can be lost entirely, they cannot be your only path. Which brings us to the part everyone skips.

7. Reconciliation — the unglamorous thing that makes it trustworthy

Every event-based sync drifts eventually. A message is dropped, a deploy happens mid-batch, someone edits stock by hand in the Shopify admin. The question is not whether it drifts; it is whether you find out before your customer does.

JobWhenWhat it does
Full read-backNightly, quiet hoursbulkOperationRunQuery pulls every variant's stock out of Shopify, compares to Game Locker, reports differences
Auto-healSame jobWhere GL is the owner and the gap is explainable, write the absolute truth back. Where it is not explainable, do not silently fix it — flag it
Drift alarmContinuousAlert on the rate of mismatches, not on each one. One mismatch is life. Fifty in an hour is an incident
Unfulfillable reportDailyOrders for stock that is not there. This is the only place an oversell is actually visible
The distinction that sounds senior Auto-heal the explainable, alarm on the unexplainable. A job that silently corrects everything it finds also hides the bug that is causing the drift — so the numbers look healthy forever while the real defect grows. A job that fixes what it understands and escalates what it does not is the difference between a sync you trust and a sync that lies to you.

8. Five sync cases, start to finish

Everything above is a rule. Here are the rules doing their job, on one card — the Charizard from Lesson 1, in store 17 in Sydney. Walk these out loud and the whiteboard question stops being scary, because you are describing something you have already traced.

Case 1 — a new card arrives at the shop

A collector sells a Charizard to the shop over the counter. 1 GL creates a store_inventory row store 17 · card 2 · NM · EN · holo · qty 1 · $480 2 GL asks: does store 17's Shopify already have a product for this PRINTING? no → create the product and its variants yes → reuse it 3 GL sets the inventory level on the NM/EN/holo variant

Owner: Game Locker · Direction: GL → Shopify · Runs: on first acquisition.
Guard: key the "does it exist?" check on the printing, never on the card name. A retry that creates a second product leaves two pages competing for the same search ranking, and merging them later is manual work.

Case 2 — the nightly reprice

Card prices move overnight. 1 the feed updates market_price on the SHARED card row 2 GL works out each store's new selling price 3 GL diffs against the price it last pushed 100,000 variants → maybe 5,000 actually moved 4 ONE bulkOperationRunMutation, a JSONL file, async

Owner: Game Locker · Direction: GL → Shopify · Runs: nightly.
Guard: push the diff, not all 100,000. A price write is not free — it burns your budget and invalidates caches. See L02 §8.

Case 3 — the card sells online

14:07 a collector pays for the Charizard on the website. 14:07:00 Shopify decrements ITSELF: available 1 → 0 14:07:01 webhook orders/create fires 14:07:01 GL enqueues it and returns 200 straight away 14:07:03 the worker applies it: store_inventory 1 → 0 "order 1234 already applied" recorded FIRST

Owner: whoever created it — here, Shopify · Direction: Shopify → GL.
Guard 1: webhooks are at-least-once, so the order id is the idempotency key. You will get the same order twice.
Guard 2: GL must not push that 0 back to Shopify. Shopify already did it. Stamp the origin on every write, or stock drops by 2 for one sale — the echo loop from §3.

Case 4 — the card sells at the counter instead

14:05 the same Charizard sells at the kiosk. 14:05:00 the till sells it. Once GL is told (by import, which is its own delay): store_inventory 1 → 0 14:05:00 the counter-sale EVENT fires a push at once 14:05:01 inventorySetQuantities { available: 0, compareQuantity: 1 } 14:05:01 Shopify is correct again. ⚠ Between 14:05:00 and 14:05:01, Shopify still said 1. One second, not fifteen minutes — because the push is event-driven and the batch job is only a safety net.

Owner: Game Locker · Direction: GL → Shopify.
Guard: push on the event, not the batch — defence #1, the biggest win for the least work. The write is absolute, so a retry is harmless.

Case 5 — both doors sell the last copy

The honest one. You cannot design this away, only shrink it and handle it.

14:05:00 the counter sells it. GL: 1 → 0 14:05:01 a shopper online buys it too. Shopify: 1 → 0 14:05:02 GL's push arrives: { available: 0, compareQuantity: 1 } → REJECTED. GL thought Shopify was 1. It is 0. The rejection is the system WORKING. It has just told you that something happened which GL did not know about. GL re-reads, sees 0, agrees. The number is now right. But one online order still exists for a card that is gone. → it surfaces on the daily UNFULFILLABLE report → the pre-agreed apology runs: refund, substitute, or hold

Owner: contested for one second — and that single second is the whole reason this role exists.
Guard: compareQuantity catches the stale write; the unfulfillable report is the only place the oversell is ever visible, because both systems end up agreeing on 0; the apology is a written procedure rather than an emergency.

Each case is a different part of this lesson doing its job:

CaseThe part of the lesson it exercises
1 — new cardThe ownership matrix. Products are one-way, GL → Shopify.
2 — repriceBulk operations, and the judgment to send a diff rather than everything.
3 — online saleThe echo loop, and idempotency keyed on the order id.
4 — counter saleAbsolute writes, compareQuantity, and pushing on an event.
5 — collisionThe oversell minute, and why reconciliation cannot find it.

Notice that four of the five are calm and only case 5 is a real problem. That proportion is worth saying out loud in an interview: most of the sync is boring one-way plumbing, and almost all of the difficulty sits in the one second where both systems are correct at the same time.

9. What you would actually build

Pulling it together — the shape to draw when they say "design this".

GAME LOCKER SHOPIFY ─────────── ─────── counter sale ──┐ stock edit ────┤ price feed ────┴──► CHANGE LOG ──► PUSH WORKER ──► Admin API (what changed, │ absolute writes when, by whom) │ + compareQuantity │ + referenceDocumentUri nightly│bulk for prices │ RECONCILER ◄────────┴──── bulkOperationRunQuery compare · heal · alarm ▲ │ order applied ◄── QUEUE ◄── WEBHOOK RECEIVER ◄── orders/create (idempotent by + DLQ verify HMAC, orders/updated order id) enqueue, 200 OK inventory_levels/update FOUR PIECES. Say them in this order: 1. a change log, so pushes are replayable 2. a push worker doing absolute idempotent writes 3. a webhook receiver that only enqueues 4. a reconciler that heals what it understands and alarms on what it does not

That is a complete answer to "how would you build reliable inventory sync", and every piece exists for a reason you can defend.
