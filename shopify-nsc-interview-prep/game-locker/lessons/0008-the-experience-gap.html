Lesson 8 · Game Locker · Positioning

The Experience Gap

You have shipped themes and app integrations. You have never owned a service that keeps two systems agreeing. That is one hole, not a missing career. This lesson names the hole exactly, then turns it into four moves — one of which takes an afternoon and changes how you sound.

Your win today: two things. Say your gap in one sentence, with no apology and no overclaim. And decide — now, not later — whether you have the hours to build the 100-line proof in section 4.

Read this early, not at the end. It is the one lesson that changes how you spend the other hours. If you feel underqualified for this job, read it in block 1 of the 24-hour plan.

1. Name the hole exactly

"I have never worked on a system like this" is too vague to act on. Vague gaps grow in your head. Split the job description into what you have actually done and see what is left.

What the job needsWhat you have actually doneVerdict
Liquid, Online Store 2.0, sections, blocks, JSON templates Daily agency work, for years No gap. This is the JD's first bullet.
Fast, responsive front end Theme work, plus the metrics and causes from L05 No gap, if you can name numbers instead of habits.
Admin API, webhooks, custom apps Integrated apps and consumed these APIs — did not author the app that writes Partial. Consumer, not author. Say it that way.
POS UI Extensions Read one closely, found real defects in review; never authored one Known and already scripted — the honesty line in L04.
Node.js workers, queues, retries Studied properly; not shipped as a production sync service Partial. Real knowledge, no production scar.
A two-way inventory sync service that must survive drift Nothing you owned This is the hole. One row.
The reframe that matters Your gap is not "systems". It is owning the writer — the service that decides what to push and what to do when the push is refused. One hole is preparable in a day. A missing career is not. Prepare like a person with one hole.

2. Why the hole is smaller than it feels

Three reasons, and they compound.

ReasonWhy it helps you specifically
They are the system of record. Game Locker owns the data; Shopify is a channel hanging off it (L01). They need someone to own the Shopify side of that boundary. That side is themes, apps, and Admin API behaviour — your side.
The test is reasoning, not résumé. What a design question actually checks is: do you know how sync breaks, and would you notice? That is preparable. "Have you run one for three years?" is not — and it is not the question they can ask, because almost nobody has.
The half you own is the half backend candidates fail. A backend engineer can write a queue. Far fewer can say why render replaced include, why apps are the biggest performance cost, or how one theme serves many merchants. You can. That is not a consolation prize — it is the reason the role sits on the Shopify side.
Labelled as inference, not fact Nothing on their public site says who is on the engineering team. But a platform with 145 million cards and daily repricing already has people who write workers. What such a team is usually short of is someone fluent in the platform on the other end of the wire. Treat that as a reasonable guess, not a claim — and never say it to them as if you know it.

3. The four moves, ranked

Ranked by how much each one changes the interview, not by effort.

#MoveCostWhy it ranks here

4. Move 1 — build the 100-line proof

Not a product. A proof. On a Shopify development store, in an afternoon, thrown away afterwards.

webhook inventory_levels/update │ ├─ verify HMAC → enqueue → return 200 (never work inline) │ ▼ handler ├─ seen this X-Shopify-Webhook-Id before? → drop ├─ write with inventorySetQuantities + compareQuantity └─ userErrors on compareQuantity? → log "DRIFT"

Then deliberately break it, because the breakage is the point:

Make it failWhat you learn to say
Replay the same webhook twice Webhooks are at-least-once, so every handler needs an idempotency key. You have now seen the double-apply, not just read about it.
Send a stale compareQuantity Shopify returns a userErrors entry instead of overwriting. This is compare-and-set, and it is the strongest single answer to "how do you stop two syncs fighting".
Push a delta while another write lands Why absolute writes beat deltas: a lost delta is wrong forever, a lost absolute write is corrected by the next one.
Let your own write trigger your own webhook The echo loop, seen live. You will remember the fix — tag or fingerprint your own writes — because you had to make it stop.

The scaffold already exists: kata-03 — idempotent sync worker. The mechanisms are in L03. Build it against a real dev store, keep the logs, and screenshot the drift line.

Three ways this wastes your afternoon Polishing it. No tests, no Docker, no deploy. Ugly and working beats tidy and unfinished.
Making it real. One product, one location, one card. Scale is a conversation, not a build.
Calling it production experience. It is a toy and you say so — "I built a small one to feel the failure modes". The honesty is what makes it land.
What the afternoon buys you

"I have not run a sync service in production. I did build a small one against a dev store so I would know the failure shapes first-hand — and the thing that surprised me was how fast my own writes came back at me as webhooks. I had to fingerprint my own writes before anything else worked. That is the bug I would look for first in an existing integration."

5. Move 2 — the honest bridge

Never claim you owned a platform. Say the true version, then bridge to the part that transfers. Three beats, in this order:

1. What I have NOT owned ← said once, plainly, no apology 2. What I HAVE owned ← the adjacent real thing 3. Where I met the failure ← proof you have been near the pain
Asked aboutThe bridge
Sync services "I have not owned a two-way sync service. What I have owned is the Shopify surface an app writes into. That is where I met the failures — an app double-writing inventory, a handler with no idempotency key. So I have debugged it from the Shopify end, not the worker end."
POS UI Extensions "I have not authored one. My agency shipped one and I read it closely enough to find real defects — a receive function that returned null on both paths, userErrors read at the wrong response path, and a success banner set inside a finally, so a failed receive told the user it worked."
Marketplaces "I have not built a multi-vendor marketplace. I do know Shopify has no native path for one and that Marketplace Kit was deprecated, so it is an app or a custom build — and that paying sellers is the hard part, not listing them."
The two ways this goes wrong Overclaiming. One probe deeper and it collapses — and then every other confident claim gets re-examined. This is the single most expensive mistake available to you.
Apologising twice. Say the gap once, then move to what you do know. Repeating it turns one honest sentence into a theme, and the interviewer starts agreeing with you.

6. Move 3 — three stories, pre-loaded

Your theme and integration work is the evidence. Write these out before the call. Not memorised word for word — but the numbers and the defect names must be ready.

StoryShapeWhat it proves
A defect you found in someone else's integration The POS extension review. Three named defects, each with the reason it was wrong. Code review skill, and that you read other people's failure paths. Named defects cannot be faked.
A theme you made faster What was slow, what caused it, what you removed, and the number if you have one. Their JD asks for fast front ends. If you have no measurement, name the cause and the fix — "a third-party script blocking render" is still concrete.
Something that failed quietly, and how you noticed An integration that stopped, a feed that went stale, a sold-out product still buyable. The most valuable of the three. Quiet failure is this entire job. If you noticed it before the client did, that is the ownership instinct they are hiring.
Why story three outranks the others Sync does not crash. It drifts. So the skill they need is not "can you build it" but "would you notice it was wrong". Any story where you found the problem before the person paying you did answers the real question, even if the system was only a theme.

7. Move 4 — the two questions that reframe you

AskWhat it does
"When your platform and Shopify disagree on stock, who wins — and do you compare-and-set, or is it last-write-wins?" Uses the exact vocabulary of the problem. Nobody asks this without having thought about conflicting writes.
"How do you find out about drift today — a nightly reconcile, or does a merchant tell you?" The best question in the whole prep. Their answer tells you how mature the integration is, and if it is "the merchant tells us", you have just found your first month's work — and you can say so.

These are the same two ideas as questions 4 and 5 in L07 §4 — kept here because for a candidate worried about experience, asking them is worth more than any answer given. A question proves how you think without requiring you to have done it.

8. What loses it

Do notCost
Claim backend ownership you do not haveThey probe one level down and it collapses. Everything else you said gets re-checked.
Apologise for being "just a theme developer"Theme depth is a hiring reason here, not a weakness. If you frame it as small, they will accept your framing.
Try to learn everything in the time leftHalf-knowing six topics interviews worse than knowing sync cold. Depth in the centre beats breadth at the edge.
Lead with the gapAnswer the question first. The bridge comes when the gap is actually relevant — not as a pre-emptive disclaimer.
