Storefront API & the Marketplace

They are building a TCG marketplace where collectors buy and sell. Shopify does not do multi-seller marketplaces. That gap is real design work — and it is almost certainly why this role is open.

Your win today: say plainly that Shopify has no native multi-vendor marketplace and Marketplace Kit is deprecated, then lay out the three architectures with their trade-offs — and identify paying the sellers as the hard part rather than listing the products.

Ties to your 24-hour plan: blocks 17 and 18. Builds on L0001 (two APIs) and L0027 (payments & fulfilment).

1. Read their sentence again

"A new TCG marketplace that will give collectors and players a fast, trustworthy, and delightful way to discover, buy, and sell cards."

Three verbs, three different systems:

VerbWhat it needsDifficulty
DiscoverSearch across many sellers' stock. One canonical card, many offers, sorted by price and condition.Medium — a search problem
BuyOne cart containing cards from several different sellers, one payment, then split fulfilment.Hard
SellSeller onboarding, listing management, order notification, shipping, and getting paid.Hardest — and it is mostly not a Shopify problem

"Sell" is the word that changes everything. Their existing product serves one store at a time. A marketplace pools many stores into one buying experience. That is a different system, not a feature.

The simple version: who is selling?

If that still feels abstract, the whole difference is how many people are selling on one website.

TODAY — one shop sells Sydney shop's website ──► a collector buys ONE seller. ONE Shopify store. Shopify is happy. THE MARKETPLACE — many people sell Sydney shop ┐ Melbourne shop ├──► ONE website ──► a collector buys A private collector ┘ MANY sellers. ONE site. Shopify has no built-in idea of what a "seller" is.

A Shopify store assumes one owner. One person owns the products, receives the money, and ships the goods. There is no native concept of "this card belongs to seller #7, so pay seller #7".

One shopping trip shows the whole problem

A collector wants three cards. They happen to come from three different sellers:

CardSellerPrice
CharizardSydney shop$480
PikachuMelbourne shop$6
MewtwoA private collector$40

One cart. One payment of $526. Now count what you have taken on:

  1. All $526 lands in one bank account — yours.
  2. You must split it: $480, $6, $40, minus your fee.
  3. Three separate parcels, with three shipping costs.
  4. If the private collector never posts the card, you refund the buyer.
  5. You are now holding other people's money, which is a legal question before it is a technical one.

Not one of those five is a Shopify feature. Every one is your code, your policy, or your problem — which is why §4 argues the hard part is paying the sellers, not building the site.

Why the phrase matters in the interview, in two lines One: most candidates will assume Shopify covers multi-vendor and describe a feature that does not exist — so knowing that Marketplace Kit was withdrawn marks you as current rather than as someone repeating a 2021 blog post. Two: it probably explains why the role is open now — they need someone to keep the existing Shopify integration healthy while a genuinely different system gets built next to it. Saying that out loud shows you read the JD as a strategy document, not a checklist.

2. The fact to state plainly

Say this early — it is the credibility moment of the whole interview

"Shopify does not have a native multi-vendor marketplace. Marketplace Kit was deprecated — the docs now say to treat it as reference only. Shopify Collective exists but it is product sourcing between brands, not a marketplace platform. So a real multi-seller marketplace is either a third-party app or a custom build, and both are genuine trade-offs rather than a lookup in the docs."

Why this matters so much: many candidates will assume Shopify has this covered and will describe a feature that does not exist. Being the person who knows the honest state of the platform — including that a tool was withdrawn — is the difference between sounding current and sounding like you read a blog post from 2021.

Thing people think existsReality
Shopify Marketplace KitDeprecated. Reference only. Not a path to build on now.
Shopify CollectiveReal, but it is product sourcing — one brand selling another brand's products. Not many-sellers-one-checkout.
Shopify MarketsReal, but it is about selling internationally — currencies, languages, domains. Nothing to do with multiple sellers.
Multi-vendor apps (Webkul, Shipturtle, Onport, Puppet Vendors)Real and widely used. A legitimate option — and each imposes its own model on you.

3. The three architectures

This is the design answer. Give all three, then pick one and say why.

Option A — one Shopify store, sellers as a concept inside it

ONE STORE. All sellers' cards are products in it. Seller identity lives in a metafield or a vendor field. ✅ One checkout, one cart, one Shopify — simplest to build ✅ Shopify handles payment, tax, fraud, checkout quality ✅ Discovery and search are trivial — it is one catalogue ❌ Money all lands in ONE Shopify Payments account. YOU now owe every seller their share. You are effectively holding other people's money. ❌ Sellers have no Shopify admin of their own ❌ Order splitting and per-seller shipping is your code ❌ Returns and disputes route to you, not the seller

Option B — each seller keeps their own Shopify store

MANY STORES. The marketplace is a discovery layer on top. ✅ Sellers own their inventory, orders, payouts — clean ✅ No money-handling problem: each buyer pays each seller ✅ Matches Game Locker's EXISTING customer shape — they already have stores with their own Shopify ❌ NO SHARED CART. A buyer wanting cards from three sellers does three checkouts. This is a serious experience problem for a hobby where a typical order is singles from several sellers. ❌ Discovery must aggregate stock from N stores and keep it fresh ❌ "Trustworthy" is harder — quality varies per seller

Option C — headless marketplace, Shopify as the commerce engine

Custom front end + own backend. Shopify per seller (or one Shopify) behind it, reached through the Storefront API. ✅ Full control of discovery, search, ranking, UX ✅ Can present ONE experience over many sellers ✅ Storefront API has NO REQUEST RATE LIMIT, and capacity scales with buyers by IP — built for this ❌ You now own everything Shopify gave you free: SEO, page speed, accessibility, checkout quality ❌ Most expensive, slowest to build, biggest team ❌ Still does not solve seller payouts by itself

The recommendation, and how to give it

Do not pick before asking a question. The right answer depends on something you do not know yet:

The question that decides it "Does a buyer need one cart across multiple sellers?" If yes, option B is out, no matter how attractive its ownership model is. If no — if buyers shop one seller at a time — then B is by far the cheapest and safest, and it fits the customers Game Locker already has. Asking this before answering is itself the senior behaviour; committing to an architecture before knowing it is the junior one.

If pushed to choose: start with A for a controlled launch, with a small number of trusted sellers and payouts handled manually or by a payment provider that supports split payments. Then move toward C once the marketplace has proven demand and the discovery experience is the thing limiting growth. The reasoning is that A tests the market cheaply and C is only worth its cost once you know people want it.

4. The hard part is paying the sellers

Everyone designs the product listing. Almost nobody mentions the money. Raising it first is a strong differentiator.

ONE BUYER BUYS FROM THREE SELLERS. $100 TOTAL. $60 → Seller A $30 → Seller B $10 → Seller C minus marketplace commission on each minus payment processing plus shipping, which each seller charges separately and A ships today, B ships in a week, C cancels QUESTIONS THAT ARE NOT ENGINEERING QUESTIONS: · when does a seller get paid — on order, on ship, or after the return window closes? · who refunds if C cancels? who eats the fee? · if the buyer disputes with their bank, whose money is clawed back? · are you legally holding client money? in which country? (they are Australian — this matters) ⚠ IN OPTION A, ALL OF THIS IS YOURS. Shopify Payments pays ONE account: yours.
The thing to say, and the thing not to say Do say: "The engineering is the easy half. Deciding when a seller gets paid, who carries a chargeback, and whether we are legally holding client money is a business and compliance question, and I'd want it answered before I picked an architecture — because the answer changes the architecture."

Do not say: a confident scheme for splitting payouts. You do not know their payment provider, their jurisdiction's rules, or their risk appetite. Inventing one sounds like you have not considered that this is regulated.

5. Storefront API — the facts to have right

The JD names the Storefront API explicitly, and it is the API a marketplace front end would live on. The contrast with the Admin API is the useful frame.

Admin APIStorefront API
ForManaging the store — products, inventory, orders, fulfilmentBuilding a buying experience — browse, cart, checkout
Who calls itYour backend, with a secretA browser or app, or your server
Rate limitsCost-based (GraphQL) or per-second (REST). You must manage your budget.No request rate limit. Capacity scales with buyers, by customer IP. Automated traffic — bots, crawlers — is throttled.
TokensAccess token, always secretPublic access token for browser use; private access token for server-side, and it must be kept secret
Can it write inventory?YesNo
CartNot its jobThe Cart object. No global rate limits — built to survive a flash sale
The distinction that shows you have used both "The Storefront API has no request rate limit; the Admin API is cost-based and you must budget it." That one sentence tells an interviewer you have hit an Admin API limit in real life and know why the Storefront API is different. And the reason the two differ is worth stating: Admin API traffic is your automation, which Shopify must protect itself from; Storefront traffic is buyers, which Shopify wants to be unlimited because it is revenue.

The token detail matters practically: a public access token is designed to be visible in browser code, so shipping one in your JS is fine and expected. A private token is a secret for server-side calls, and leaking it into client code is a real vulnerability. Getting these the wrong way round is a common mistake.

6. "Fast, trustworthy, and delightful" — decode the brief

Those three adjectives are not marketing filler. Each names a real engineering problem, and mapping them is a good way to show you read carefully.

Their wordThe engineering problem
FastSearch over many sellers' stock, at card-catalogue scale, on a phone. Core Web Vitals from Lesson 5. Probably a dedicated search index rather than Shopify's product search.
TrustworthyThe listing must be true. Stock that is actually there, condition described honestly, a seller who ships. This is the oversell problem from Lesson 3 multiplied by the number of sellers — and now a stale listing damages their brand, not just one shop's.
DelightfulOne cart across sellers, clear condition photos, easy bulk buying. Mostly front-end craft — which is why the JD wants theming and integration in one person.
The connection to make out loud

"'Trustworthy' is the interesting one for me, because it is the same inventory-accuracy problem as the POS sync — just multiplied by every seller. A marketplace listing that is stale is worse than a shop listing that is stale, because the buyer blames the marketplace rather than the seller."

7. What I would actually ask them about it

Have these ready. Asking good questions about the marketplace signals that you are already thinking like someone who works there.

  1. "Does a buyer need one cart across several sellers?" — the architecture-deciding question.
  2. "Are the sellers your existing customers — stores already on Game Locker — or new sellers, including individuals?" Individuals selling cards is a much harder trust and compliance problem than vetted shops.
  3. "Who holds the money between purchase and payout, and has that been settled with finance?"
  4. "Is the marketplace meant to run on Shopify, or is Shopify one channel that feeds it?" — you genuinely cannot tell from the JD, and it is the biggest single unknown.
  5. "What does the first version have to prove?" Launch scope tells you whether they want A or C, without you having to guess.
Ask Claude

"drill me on the marketplace design" — the three options, the payout problem, and the deciding question.

"push back on my marketplace recommendation" — argue the other side so you have heard the objections before they do.
