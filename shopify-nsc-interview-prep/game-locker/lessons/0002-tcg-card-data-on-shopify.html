TCG Card Data on Shopify

One rule — a product may have at most three options — decides the entire model. Everything else in this lesson is a consequence of counting to three.

Your win today: answer "how would you model trading cards in Shopify?" by naming one product per printing, three options: condition · language · finish, and metafields for everything else — then explain why graded cards break the rule and what you do about them.

Ties to your 24-hour plan: blocks 9 and 10. Read Lesson 1 first — this lesson assumes you know the catalogue and the stock are different things.

1. What is a card, as a thing you can sell?

Start at the counter, not the schema. A collector says: "do you have Black Lotus?" That question is not answerable. To sell one physical card you need to pin down all of this:

Nine attributes. Shopify gives you three options. So the first real design decision is which three earn a slot, and where the other six go.

2. The constraint that decides everything

Two numbers, both current, both worth knowing exactly:

LimitValueWhy it matters here
Variants per product2048 (raised from 100 in Oct 2025)Generous. Not your problem any more — but it was, and saying so shows you are current.
Options per product3This is the real constraint. It has not moved.
Media files per product250A card needs 1–2 images, so fine — unless you tried one product per card name.
New variants per day10,000 (if the store has 500k+ variants)Shapes the initial import, not the daily run.
The 100 → 2048 change is a free credibility point Before October 2025 the limit was 100 variants. Condition (5) × language (11) × finish (2) = 110 — which used to overflow, and forced ugly workarounds like splitting by language into separate products. Now it fits comfortably. Mentioning that you know both the old and new number, and what it unblocked, marks you as someone who tracks the platform rather than someone who learned Shopify once.

4. Why those three options and not others

The test for "should this be an option?" is: does a buyer pick between these on the product page, and does each choice have its own stock level and price?

AttributeOption?Why
Condition✓ OptionBuyer chooses it, each has its own stock and price. Non-negotiable.
Language✓ OptionSame. A Japanese NM copy is a different item with a different price.
Finish✓ OptionSame. Foil is a different physical card.
Set✗ Product identityNobody buys "Lightning Bolt" and then picks a set — the set is which card it is. It also drives the image, so it cannot be a variant.
Rarity, card type, artist✗ MetafieldYou filter by these, you do not choose them. Fixed for a given printing.
Grade✗ Its own productSee below — it is the one that breaks the model.
The trap: one product per card name It is tempting to make "Lightning Bolt" one product with set as an option. It collapses immediately. Set is not one of three slots you can spare; each set needs its own image and products cap at 250 media; prices differ by 100× so they cannot share a price range; and the product page would show one card's art for forty different cards. Set belongs to identity, not choice.

5. Graded cards — the honest exception

A graded card is sealed in a plastic slab by PSA, BGS or CGC with a numeric grade and a unique certificate number. It is not a condition — it is a specific individual object.

Why grading exists at all

Worth understanding, because it explains why slabs carry the money. Grading solves a trust problem between two people who never meet.

A collector in Sydney wants $4,000 for a Charizard. A buyer in Berlin cannot hold the card. Without a grade:

Seller says: "Near mint! Great condition!" Buyer thinks: "Says who? A tiny corner ding is worth $2,000 on this card. I cannot see it in a photo. I am not sending $4,000." → the trade simply does not happen

Condition is subjective, and on an expensive card it is most of the price. One seller's "near mint" is another's "lightly played", and that disagreement is worth thousands. A neutral third party ends the argument:

What grading givesHow
An agreed numberA neutral company scores it 1–10. "PSA 10" means the same thing to everyone, everywhere.
A check for fakesThe grader authenticates the card, so the slab is proof it is genuine.
A frozen conditionSealed, so it cannot degrade from handling — and nobody can swap the card inside.
A market priceBecause everyone agrees what PSA 10 means, that exact thing has a published price, the way a share does.

The last row is the point. Grading turns a card into something you can buy sight unseen, from a stranger, in another country.

Why nobody grades a 20-cent common Grading costs a fee per card and takes time, so it only makes sense when the value it unlocks beats the fee. A $4,000 card, yes. A common, never. There is also a gamble: you send it hoping for a 10 and it comes back a 7, and the hope of a 10 is itself worth money. The useful consequence for you is to expect a store's graded stock to be a small, high-value slice of its catalogue — which is exactly the slice where being wrong is most expensive.

Graded is not "limited edition"

These two get confused, and confusing them puts the data in the wrong layer.

Limited editionGraded
Describeshow many were printedwhat happened to one physical card
Decidedat the factory, in 1999whenever an owner sends it away
Applies toevery copy of that printingjust that one card
Who decidesthe game publisherPSA, BGS or CGC
Can a 20-cent common have it?NoYes — any card can be graded

So they live in different places. "1st Edition Charizard" is a different printing, so it gets its own shared card-reference row (see the two layers in Lesson 1) — every 1st Edition Charizard in the world is that one row. "PSA 10 Charizard, cert 12345678" is not a field on any shared row at all, because it is a single object that exists once.

A limited print run is a fact about the card type. A grade is a fact about one piece of cardboard. Which is why the next sentence has to be true:

So it cannot be a variant of anything. Two PSA 10 copies of the same card are still two different items, because each has its own cert number, and buyers of expensive slabs care which one they get.

RAW CARD GRADED CARD ───────── ──────────── product = printing product = ONE PHYSICAL OBJECT variants = condition × variants = 1 language × finish quantity = 12 quantity = ALWAYS 1 fungible: any NM copy not fungible: this exact will do cert number, this photo → variant model works → one-of-one model, real photos, cert number in a metafield

This matters commercially: slabs are where the money is. It also matters technically — a quantity that is always 1 means there is no buffer available. Which connects straight to the oversell problem in Lesson 3.

Memorise this

"Raw singles are fungible, so they fit the variant model. Graded slabs are one-of-one objects with a cert number, so each one is its own product with quantity 1 — and no buffer is possible, which makes them the riskiest thing on the site to oversell."

6. Metafields vs metaobjects — and when each is right

Card attributes have to live somewhere queryable. Two tools, and the difference is worth stating precisely because it is a common interview probe.

MetafieldMetaobject
What it isAn extra field on a resourceA standalone record with its own fields, that resources point at
Use forcollector number, artist, release date — facts belonging to this one productSet and game — shared entities with their own name, code, logo, release date
WhyThe value is only ever about this product"Modern Horizons 3" is referenced by hundreds of products. Store it once, point at it, and you get a set page for free.
Duplication riskHigh if the value is shared — a typo'd set name in 400 productsNone — one record, one spelling

The short version: if two products would hold the same value, it wants to be a metaobject. Set, game and rarity are shared. Collector number and artist are not.

Why the interviewer may care Filters on a collection page are driven by these fields. "Show me all Mythic rares from Modern Horizons 3, NM, in English, under $20" is the query a card site lives on. Get the fields right and filtering is configuration. Get them wrong — set name typed as free text into every product — and filtering is permanently broken in a way that is expensive to repair.

7. Scale — what actually goes into Shopify

Back to the number from Lesson 1. Game Locker's catalogue is ~145 million cards; their Starter plan caps a store at 20,000 items. Those two numbers describe different things, and the gap between them is the answer to their scale question.

GAME LOCKER holds the CATALOGUE ~145,000,000 printings every card that has ever existed, shared by all customers used for: search, matching a scan, looking up a price │ │ a store says "I have 3 of this one" ▼ THE STORE holds INVENTORY e.g. 20,000 items │ │ only what is actually IN STOCK ▼ SHOPIFY holds SELLABLE PRODUCTS ~5,000–20,000 products with maybe 2–6 variants each actually carrying stock 145M → 20k is not a scaling problem. It is a FILTER. The filter is: "do you physically have one?"

So the sizing answer is comfortable: a few thousand to a few tens of thousands of products, each with a handful of live variants. Shopify handles that without drama. The scale problem was never the row count — it was not sending rows that have no stock behind them.

The subtle version of the same trap Should you create a product the moment a store acquires a card, and delete it when it sells? No — churn. Create on first acquisition, then let stock go to 0 and stay, so the URL, its search ranking and any links to it survive. Cards come back into stock constantly. Deleting and recreating products throws away SEO every week and generates pointless API traffic.

8. The daily repricing job

"Pricing automation with daily syncs from global platforms" is on their homepage, which means this job exists and someone maintains it. If you are the Shopify engineer, the Shopify end of it is yours.

Shape of the problem: a store with 20,000 items might have 60,000–100,000 variants whose prices could move overnight. You cannot update those one at a time through the normal API — the cost-based rate limit will stop you long before you finish.

ApproachCapacityUse when
productVariantsBulkUpdateUp to 2048 variants per call, one product at a timeTargeted updates — one product's prices changed
bulkOperationRunMutationA JSONL file, uploaded, processed asynchronously by ShopifyThe nightly reprice. Tens of thousands of rows, one operation, no rate-limit babysitting
bulkOperationRunQuerySame, for readingThe nightly reconciliation read-back — see L0018

Two numbers to keep straight, because they are easy to mix up:

  • 2048 — variants per product, and also the most you can pass to productVariantsBulkUpdate in one call.
  • 250 — the maximum items in one page of a GraphQL list query. A pagination limit, not a mutation limit.
  • 50,000 — the cap on inventory quantities across all variants in a single mutation.
The judgment part of the answer Do not push all 100,000 prices every night. Push a diff. Most card prices do not move materially day to day, and a price write is not free — it invalidates caches and it burns your budget. Keep the last-pushed price, compare, and send only what changed by more than a threshold you agree with the business. That turns 100,000 writes into maybe 5,000, and it is the kind of answer that separates people who have run a nightly job from people who have designed one on a whiteboard.

9. What a collector actually does on the site

Worth closing here, because it is the check on the whole model. A collector wants to:

  1. Search a card by name, and see every printing you have.
  2. Filter by set, rarity, condition, language, finish, price.
  3. See which condition is actually in stock before choosing.
  4. Buy several cards at once — a cart with 30 lines is normal, not unusual.

Point 3 is the one the model must earn: because condition is an option, Shopify knows stock per condition, so the product page can grey out "Damaged — sold out" without a single custom query. Point 4 is a real warning for the theme — a 30-line cart puts pressure on the cart page and on the Cart AJAX API, and connects to TA L03: update.js does not check stock, which is unusually dangerous when most lines have a stock level of 1.

Why a 30-line cart is a technical concern

That warning is worth unpacking, because it is two separate problems and the second one costs money.

Problem 1 — it feels slow. Thirty lines is a lot to draw. Each one carries an image, a name, a condition, a price and a quantity button.

NAIVE: shopper changes one quantity → POST /cart/change.js → reload the whole page → all 30 lines drawn again, 30 images re-requested → feels broken BETTER: POST /cart/change.js with sections=cart-items,cart-footer,header → one request, only what changed comes back

That is bundled section rendering, covered in Lesson 5 §4. It matters because the slowness lands exactly on the most valuable order. It also shows up as a real metric rather than a feeling: INP, how fast the page answers a tap, wants to be ≤ 200ms.

Problem 2 — the risk is multiplied by thirty. update.js performs no stock check, so nothing objects if a card in the cart is already gone. Same cart page, same API, completely different exposure:

T-shirt shopCard shop
Lines in a typical cart330
Stock on each line2001
Chances a line is already sold~030

Every line is a separate chance that somebody else took that card.

The part most people miss: a big cart is held open longer Finding thirty different cards takes time — searching, comparing conditions, checking prices. Call it twenty minutes. The counter is open for all twenty of them. So a 30-line cart is not merely bigger than a 3-line one, it is held for longer, and both facts push the same way: a stale line becomes much more likely. This is the cart-side twin of the oversell window in Lesson 3, and it is why availability is re-verified at checkout rather than trusted from the cart.

A third trap, worth knowing: in the Cart AJAX API, line positions move under you. Remove line 5 and lines 6–30 all shift up by one. Code that remembered "the shopper is editing line 7" is now pointing at a different card. On a 3-line t-shirt cart you might never notice. On a 30-line cart of thirty different cards, you change the quantity of the wrong card.

Say it like this

"Thirty lines is a normal order for a collector, not an edge case. It hurts twice: the cart re-renders thirty rows on every tap, and update.js performs no stock check — which is mild when every item has 200 in stock and serious when every line has 1. So availability gets re-verified at checkout, never trusted from the cart."
