The Buy List and the Credit Ledger

A card shop buys as well as sells. That one sentence adds a whole second order type, a payout pipeline, and a ledger of money the shop owes its customers — which is where the only real money bug in this system lives.

Your win today: explain why a buy list is an order with the direction flipped, name the lifecycle from memory, and describe the double-spend that appears the moment a second system starts issuing store credit — including why it is a money bug rather than a display bug.

Facts here are rows in verified-facts §8 and §9. Read A03 first — a buy list is meaningless without the rule engine that quotes it.

1. Two ways money moves

Every retail system you have built moves money one way: customer pays, shop ships. A card shop moves it both ways, over the same stock, at the same counter, all day.

SELL BUY (a "buy list", or a "sell-in") ──── ───────────────────────────────── customer wants a card customer has cards to get rid of shop quotes SELL price shop quotes BUY price (cash or credit) customer pays money SHOP pays money — or issues credit stock goes DOWN stock goes UP revenue cost of goods fulfilment inspection and grading

The word for the second one differs by who is speaking. Customers call it selling their cards. The shop calls it a buy list, because it is the shop's list of what it will buy. Some teams call one submission a sell-in. Same object.

The insight that makes the build small Game Locker did not build a separate subsystem for this. A buy list is an order with orderType=buy. verified Same object, same table, one field flipped. That is the right call, and it is worth being able to defend: a trade-in has a customer, a set of line items, quantities, per-line prices, a total, and a payment. That is an order. Building a parallel Buylist aggregate would duplicate every one of those concepts and then need reconciling against the first one.

2. The lifecycle, and what each step really is

The real tabs on their buy list screen: All / Quoted / Reviewed / Completed. verified

StatusWhat has physically happenedWhat the system owes
Quoted Nothing yet. A price has been offered on a list of cards. The cards are still in the customer's hands, or in the post A price that was true at quote time, and a record of when that was
Reviewed A human has the cards and has graded them. This is where the quote gets adjusted, because the customer said Near Mint and it is Lightly Played The delta between quoted and accepted, per line, with a reason
Completed The customer has been paid — cash handed over, or credit issued. Stock is now the shop's A payment reference, and a stock increment

The columns on that screen tell you as much as the tabs do:

Buylist ID │ Customer name │ Submitted at │ Buylist status │ Total qty │ Channel │ Payment ref │ Payment status │ Quoted buy price │ Profit %

verified Read those four columns carefully, because each one is a design decision.

ColumnWhat its existence proves
Channel A buy list can arrive from more than one place — the counter, or online. So the system was always meant to accept sell-ins from a website, not only from a person standing at a till
Payment ref + Payment status This is not a review queue. It is a payout pipeline. It tracks whether a human has actually been paid, which means it is money-path software and has to be treated as such
Quoted buy price The quote is preserved separately from whatever was finally accepted. You need both to answer "did we honour what we offered"
Profit % The shop knows, per trade-in, what margin it just made. This is the number the owner actually manages the business on — and it is why bypassing this pipeline is a commercial problem, not a technical one
A correction worth carrying An early version of the discovery this course draws on reported that the platform "lacks a buy list feature". It does not. It has the full pipeline above, and staff can create one by hand. What it lacks is a customer-facing buy list — a page where a collector, at home, searches cards and gets an instant quote. That distinction is the entire reason the integration project existed, and getting it backwards in a room with someone who works there would be bad.
And one honest gap A buy list with Channel = "Shopify" was observed once, quoted at A$14.67 cash. On a later visit it was gone — zero rows in every tab, the tenant appears to have been reset. So what a Shopify-channel buy list actually does was never inspected. gap Likewise the write path: POST /orders/new is inferred from observed routes and was deliberately never called, because calling it would create a real record in a real tenant. gap Do not say "you can create a buy list through the API". You do not know that.

3. The quote is a promise with an expiry

Here is the part that separates a buy list from a shopping cart. In a cart, the price is the price until you pay. In a buy list, the shop makes an offer, the customer physically posts cards, and days pass. Two things drift in that gap.

What driftsHow fastWho is at fault
The market price moved, so the rule now produces a different number ~4.4% of cards move on a given day, and only ~0.04% move hard. So a card is usually unchanged. verified Nobody. It is the market
The shop edited a rule, or added a stop-buy Unmeasured. gap Could be daily The shop
The card is not the condition the customer claimed Every batch. This is the normal case, not the exception The customer, usually honestly

The first row is reassuring and slightly surprising. Because only about one card in twenty-three moves on a given day, a quote from yesterday is very likely still right. So a 24 to 48 hour quote validity is a business choice, not a technical necessity. Say it that way — it hands the decision to the person who should own it.

The third row is the one that actually costs money, and no amount of engineering fixes it. A human has to look at the card. That is why Reviewed exists as a distinct status, and why re-pricing at review time is not optional.

Say it like this

"The quote isn't a price, it's an offer with a timestamp. So I'd store the quoted number, re-evaluate at review, and show the customer both — because the difference between what we offered and what we paid is the number that generates every complaint."

4. The ledger — the sharpest finding in the whole discovery

Their customers screen has these columns: verified

Name │ Email / SHOPIFY CUSTOMER ID │ Mobile Number │ Transactions │ CREDIT BALANCE │ Total Spending │ TOTAL SOLD

Read that as a schema and three things jump out.

It is keyed to the Shopify Customer ID. Not to an internal id with Shopify as a side note. The platform holds Shopify's own identifier as a first-class column, which means it considers the Shopify customer and its own customer to be the same person.

It holds Credit Balance. This is a ledger of money the shop owes its customers. Not points, not a discount — spendable value.

It holds Total Sold. The platform tracks the lifetime value of a customer in both directions: what they have bought, and what they have sold to the shop. A card shop's best customer is often its best supplier.

Why that produces a money bug

Now add the obvious feature request. A customer sells cards on the website and chooses store credit. Shopify has a native store-credit feature. Use it.

THE DOUBLE SPEND Customer trades in cards online, takes $50 store credit. You issue it as Shopify store credit. Shopify: $50 Game Locker: $0 Later that week they issue credit at the counter, through the platform. Same customer, $30. Shopify: $50 Game Locker: $30 The customer now sees $50 online and $30 in store — for one balance. They spend $50 online. Shopify: $0 Game Locker: $30 They spend $30 in store. Shopify: $0 Game Locker: $0 The shop paid out $80 of credit it issued $80 of... no. It issued ONE liability and honoured it TWICE.
Why this is a money bug and not a UX bug Two ledgers holding the same liability, with no write path between them, is not "the balances look inconsistent". It is real money leaving the business twice for one obligation. And it cannot be patched from the Shopify side, because the platform offers no external write access verified — so you cannot decrement their balance when Shopify's is spent. That is the exact capability someone earlier declared unnecessary.

Which ledger should win

This is a design question you should have an opinion on, and the opinion is not close.

Game Locker holds itShopify holds it
Where credit is currently issuedThe counter, which is where most trade-ins happenNowhere yet
HistoryYears of balances, carried across a previous inventory system tooEmpty
Spendable in storeYes, nativelyOnly if the till can read Shopify — and the till is third-party
Spendable onlineOnly if the storefront can read it — needs an API they do not giveYes, natively
Moving to it meansBuilding a read path for the storefrontMigrating every existing balance, and rebuilding in-store spend

Game Locker should hold it, and the storefront should read it rather than mint its own. The balance already lives there, it already spends where most of it is spent, and the alternative starts with a migration of real customer money. A ledger migration is not an automation task.

The honest limit on this finding The tenant inspected had zero customers, so this is schema evidence, not observed balances. gap Whether production balances there are authoritative is not established. Say "appears to be, at schema level" — the design conclusion holds either way, because two ledgers for one liability is a problem whichever one turns out to be fuller.

5. Accepting a buy list changes stock — and that is harder than it sounds

When a trade-in completes, the shop owns cards it did not own before. Stock must go up. Two facts make that awkward.

Stock is written in Shopify. reported Their own guidance: "you never need to read/update inventory from our side… you just need to manage stock updates from that end and it syncs with us." So the increment happens in Shopify and flows back. Convenient — no credential needed.

But the card might not exist in Shopify. A shop only has products for cards it stocks. A customer bringing in a card the shop has never carried has no variant to increment. verified

And you cannot just create one The SKU contract is {cardId}_{locationId}, and the card id is theirs, generated per condition, not yours to mint. Create a product with an invented id and you have made a row their sync does not recognise — a permanent orphan that will not reconcile. Worse, you must also decide which location's stock rises, because the location id is baked into the SKU, and the platform now shows a "Multi-store (Early access)" badge. verified Neither question has an answer in the real project.

So the honest position on a Shopify-side implementation is: it can handle trade-ins of cards the shop already stocks, and new-to-inventory cards need the platform to create the product first. That is a genuine limit to state up front, not a detail to discover in week six.

6. The reporting objection

One more thing, and it is the kind of constraint engineers miss.

If stock syncs from Shopify to the platform anyway, why not skip the buy-list pipeline entirely? Accept the trade-in, increment stock in Shopify, done. No integration needed.

Because of Profit %. And Payment status. And Total Sold.

An inventory increment that arrives as a plain stock adjustment carries no customer, no cost, no margin, and no payment record. The shop loses its buy-list reporting — the numbers it runs the buying side of the business on. The objection raised in the real project was exactly this: the sync route "doesn't work without reporting out of our buy list".

The question nobody asked What reporting, specifically, is lost? That objection was never quantified. It might be three columns in a weekly report, in which case there is a cheap answer. It might be the shop's entire buying strategy, in which case there is not. Asking "which reports break, and can I see one" is a better move than either accepting or dismissing the objection — and it is the sort of question that marks the difference between a developer and an engineer.

7. Build it

NestJS and Postgres, continuing from A03.

The order, both directions

order
  id             uuid
  tenant_id      uuid
  type           'sell' | 'buy'          -- one field, two businesses
  customer_id    uuid
  channel        'counter' | 'online' | 'kiosk'
  status         -- sell: open|paid|fulfilled|cancelled
                 -- buy:  quoted|reviewed|completed|cancelled
  location_id    uuid
  quoted_total   numeric                 -- what we offered
  accepted_total numeric                 -- what we actually paid. NULL until reviewed
  payout_method  'cash' | 'credit'        -- buy only
  payment_ref    text
  payment_status 'unpaid' | 'paid' | 'failed'
  submitted_at   timestamptz
  reviewed_at    timestamptz
  completed_at   timestamptz

order_line
  id             uuid
  order_id       uuid
  sku_id         uuid                    -- printing x condition. See A02
  qty            int
  quoted_unit    numeric                 -- the offer, frozen
  quoted_rule_id uuid                    -- WHICH rule produced it. Not optional
  accepted_unit  numeric                 -- after a human looked. NULL until reviewed
  accepted_sku_id uuid                   -- graded down? The condition CHANGED
  reject_reason  text
Two fields there are doing real work quoted_rule_id — freeze which rule produced the offer, not just the number. When a customer says "you offered me more than this", you can show them the rule as it was, and whether it changed. Without it you have a number and no defence.

accepted_sku_id — a graded-down card is a different sku, because condition is part of the atom. The customer submitted NM and the shop accepted LP. Storing that as a price adjustment on the same line loses the fact that the physical thing is different, and your stock increment then goes to the wrong variant.

The ledger

credit_account
  customer_id    uuid primary key
  tenant_id      uuid
  balance        numeric        -- a CACHE. Never the truth
  updated_at     timestamptz

credit_entry                     -- append-only. THIS is the truth
  id             uuid
  customer_id    uuid
  amount         numeric        -- signed: + issued, - spent
  reason         'buylist_payout' | 'spend' | 'manual_adjust' | 'refund' | 'migration'
  order_id       uuid           -- what caused it
  actor          text           -- who did it
  created_at     timestamptz
The one rule for money Append-only entries are the truth; the balance is a cache you can rebuild. Never UPDATE balance SET balance = balance - 50 as the only record. Insert a signed entry, then recompute. Two reasons: a balance you can rebuild from entries can be audited, and the question you will be asked is never "what is the balance" but "how did it get there". Add a nightly check that SUM(credit_entry.amount) = credit_account.balance per customer and alarm on any mismatch. That check is the cheapest money-bug detector you will ever write.

The state machine

submitQuote(customerId, lines) for each line: quote = quoteBuy(sku, location) <- A03 if quote is null -> reject the line, keep the reason freeze quoted_unit + quoted_rule_id per line status = 'quoted' review(orderId, decisions) <- a HUMAN, holding the cards for each line: accepted_sku_id = the condition actually observed accepted_unit = re-quote against TODAY's rules status = 'reviewed' complete(orderId) <- the money step TRANSACTION if payout_method = 'credit' insert credit_entry(+accepted_total, 'buylist_payout', orderId) recompute credit_account.balance else record payment_ref, payment_status = 'paid' increment stock for each accepted line status = 'completed' COMMIT

Three invariants worth a test each:

InvariantWhat breaks without it
complete() is one transaction covering payout and stockYou pay a customer and fail to take the stock, or take stock and never pay. Both are real-world disputes
complete() is idempotent on order idA retried request pays twice. This is the single most expensive bug in the file
An order can only leave reviewed for completedA quote gets paid out without anyone looking at the cards
