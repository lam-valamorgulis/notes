What Game Locker Actually Is

Everything after this lesson assumes you have this frame. Get it wrong and every later answer is subtly aimed at the wrong company.

1. The mistake to avoid in the first two minutes

The job title is "Shopify Engineer". The obvious assumption is: they sell trading cards on Shopify, and I will maintain their store.

That assumption is wrong, and it is the kind of wrong that shows. Their own site carries no Shopify signals at all — no cdn.shopify.com, no /cdn/shop/ asset paths, no Liquid artifacts. Game Locker is not running on Shopify.

What their site does say is this:

What they publishWhat it tells you as an engineer
"145,012,624 cards" across "18+ games" A catalogue at a scale no single Shopify store can hold. This is card reference data, not one shop's stock. But see the note below — their own API returns a much smaller number, and the smaller one is the one to quote.
"Pricing automation with daily syncs from global platforms" A daily bulk write problem — now measured: the batch publishes at 06:00, and only about 4.4% of cards move on a given day. verified
A self-service kiosk customer-facing Corrected 2026-08-06. The kiosk lets customers browse stock and submit orders and buylists. It takes no money. Game Locker is not a POS at all — so the counter runs on a third-party till (their site publishes CSV import templates for Binder POS and Storepass). That fact still creates the hardest problem in the role, and makes it worse: the till is two hops from Shopify, not one.
Shopify only, directly. eBay is reached through a Shopify app — their site: "Whilst we do not directly integrate with eBay, stores can install various eBay connector apps through their Shopify." Plus CSV import templates for tills like Binder POS and Storepass. Shopify is one channel among several. The integration is a product feature they sell, not an internal convenience.
Tiers: Starter (20,000 items) · Advanced (unlimited) · Multi-store Multi-location is a paid tier. So multi-location inventory correctness is revenue-critical, not a nice-to-have. And "items" is their billing unit.
145 million versus 1.12 million — do not pick a side, explain the difference Their site advertises 145,012,624 cards. Their own /catalogue/metrics endpoint returns totalItems.count = 1,122,612 across 19 games, Magic alone 869,531, growing ~201 a day. verified

Both are presumably real and counting different things — the marketing figure is far too large to be printings, so it is likely copies, price points, or some cross-product of the catalogue. What that 145M actually counts is not established. gap

So quote 1.12 million, because it is the number a system has to hold and you know exactly where it came from. If you use 145M and they ask "where did you get that?", the honest answer is "your homepage", and you will not be able to say what it counts.
How to use this in the call When they ask "what do you know about us?", do not recite the marketing. Say what those facts imply, and get the field split right: "you're the system of record for the catalogue and for pricing, the counter runs on a third-party till, and stock is actually authored in Shopify and mirrored back to you — so the interesting engineering is that ownership is split by field rather than by system." That is a peer talking, not a candidate reciting.

2. The shape of the system

Put the five facts together and the architecture falls out on its own.

GAME LOCKER — THE SHAPE ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐ │ CARD REFERENCE DATA 1,122,612 items · 19 games │ │ every printing, MTG 869,531, +201/day │ │ ← shared by ALL tenants. Not anyone's stock. │ └──────────────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────┘ │ + nightly market feed ▼ ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐ │ PRICING RULES sell · buy · STOP-BUY + rounding │ │ prices are DERIVED, never stored. Published 06:00 daily. │ └──────────────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────┘ │ a store says "I have this one" ▼ ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐ │ PER-STORE INVENTORY this shop, this card, this │ │ condition, this many, at this location │ │ ← Starter caps this at 20,000 items. THE billing unit. │ └───────┬───────────────────────────────────┬──────────────────┘ │ │ ▼ ▼ ┌───────────────────────┐ ┌──────────────────────────┐ │ SELF-SERVE KIOSK │ │ SHOPIFY (the hub) │ │ customer-facing │ │ catalogue + price ──▶ │ │ browse · submit │ │ stock ◀──▶ two-way │ │ NO PAYMENT │ │ eBay via a Shopify app │ └───────┬───────────────┘ └──────────┬───────────────┘ │ │ │ ┌───────────────────┐ │ └───────▶│ THIRD-PARTY TILL │◀───────┘ (CSV, two hops) │ owns the money │ └─────────┬─────────┘ ▼ SAME PHYSICAL STOCK POOL ONE CARD. TWO PLACES TO SELL IT. NEITHER IS WRONG TO WRITE. That is the whole engineering problem.

Two things the diagram makes obvious.

The card reference data and the stock are different things. Over a million catalogue items are shared by every tenant. A single shop might have 20,000. Confusing those two is the most common way to get their scale question wrong.

Pricing sits between them as its own layer. That is not decoration — prices are derived from rules, not stored on rows, and that single decision generates most of the platform's difficulty. A03 is entirely about that box.

The scale trap If asked "how would you put our whole catalogue into Shopify?", the answer is you do not. Shopify holds what a given store actually sells. The million-row catalogue stays in Game Locker and is used for search, matching and pricing. Pushing a catalogue into a storefront that has no stock behind it creates a million dead pages, wrecks the sitemap, and slows the store down — for zero sales. Say that and you have answered a scale question with judgment rather than arithmetic.

The catalogue is a dictionary, not a warehouse

There is a quieter version of the same mistake, and it is worth killing now because it changes how you describe their whole business. It sounds reasonable and it is completely wrong:

The distribution trap "Game Locker has the cards, and each store gets some of them." No. Game Locker holds zero physical cards. It is software that game stores buy. Stores get their cards from sealed product, from distributors, and from customers over the counter — never from Game Locker. Nothing is allocated to a store, nothing is split between stores, nothing runs out.
But it does hold the numbers — do not over-correct "Holds no physical cards" is not the same as "holds no stock data." The catalogue layer holds no quantities. The per-store inventory layer — the middle box in the diagram above — is also Game Locker's, and it is exactly where "this shop has 4, near-mint, at $6" lives. When a card sells at the counter, a number in Game Locker's database goes down while the physical card leaves the shop's box. If Game Locker held no quantities there would be nothing to sync and no oversell to prevent.

Careful with the words though. Holding a quantity is not the same as owning it. Their own guidance is that stock should be managed in Shopify and synced back to them — so they hold a stock number that is frequently written from the other side. "System of record" is the right phrase for catalogue and price; for stock, say two-way, authored in Shopify.

The catalogue's rows are not physical cards. They are card types — about 1.12 million of them — every printing that exists in the world. A store's inventory is a list of pointers into that list, with a quantity and a price attached to each pointer:

THE CATALOGUE — 1.12M card TYPES. Shared by everyone. Never moves. #1 Pikachu, Base Set, #58 #2 Charizard, Base Set, #4 #3 Blastoise, Base Set, #2 #7 Mewtwo, Base Set, #10 ▲ ▲ │ "I have 4 of #1" │ "I have 2 of #1" │ │ STORE A · Sydney STORE B · Victoria #1 NM · EN · qty 4 · $6 #1 NM · EN · qty 2 · $7 ← SAME row #2 LP · EN · qty 1 · $310 #7 NM · JP · qty 9 · $2 ... 50 items ... 50 items Both stores point at #1 at the same moment. The row is not used up. Their 50 + 50 has NO relationship to the catalogue's row count.

So two stores can stock the identical 50 types, or 50 types each with no overlap at all. The catalogue does not shrink when a store lists something, and it does not care how many stores exist. Reading a word out of a dictionary does not use the word up.

The everyday version: a menu definition says "Pizza Margherita exists — tomato, mozzarella, basil." Two restaurants in two cities can both serve it. Neither one took the margherita from the other. The definition is shared; the pizzas in each kitchen are not.

Why two layers instead of one

The reason fits in one sentence: so a card's details are written once, not once per shop.

ReasonWhat it means
Write it once300 shops stock Charizard. Its name, set, artist and picture are stored one time, not 300 times.
Fix it onceWrong artwork on that card? Fix one row and all 300 shops are fixed. One layer would mean fixing 300 rows and missing some.
They change at different speedsCard details almost never change. Stock changes every minute. Putting a "never" field and an "every minute" field in the same row asks for trouble.

Which is why the card row is heavy and the inventory row is light:

CARD REFERENCE (heavy, shared) STORE INVENTORY (light, per shop) card_id 2 store_id 17 Charizard card_id 2 ← the pointer Base Set, 4/102 condition NM rarity, artist, image language EN release_date, market_price finish holo ~10 fields. Stored ONCE. quantity 4 price 6.00 One card row. Many inventory rows, in many shops, pointing at it. The inventory row stores almost NOTHING about the card itself — only WHICH card, WHICH version, HOW MANY, and AT WHAT PRICE. Everything else it looks up.

Collapse the two into one layer and Charizard's artist name sits in 300 places. Change it once and 299 shops are now wrong.

The everyday version A shopping list says "milk × 2". It does not re-write what milk is — the word already means something on its own. The list just points at the word and adds a number. The card catalogue is the word. The inventory row is the "× 2".

Three kinds of data, not two

Once the catalogue is a shared dictionary, a third layer appears that the diagram above does not show. Knowing all three keeps you out of trouble when they ask where something lives.

LayerKeyed byNames a person?Example
Card reference
shared, read-only		 the card No "Lightning Bolt, Modern Horizons 3, #161 exists." Plus the daily feed's market price — one global number, same for every store.
Per-store inventory
the billing unit		 store + card + condition · language · finish No "This shop has 3 near-mint English, and its own selling price is $2.80." This layer is what makes a card buyable.
Transactions
history		 order · counter sale · buylist slip Yes "A collector bought one on 12 March for $2.80 at the counter." Trade history lives here — never on the card.

The same three layers, as tables

If it helps to see it as a schema, this is the shape. The important detail is where quantity appears and where it does not:

-- LAYER 1 · shared. One copy for the whole platform. ~1.12M rows.
cards (
  card_id, game, set_code, collector_number,
  rarity, artist, release_date, market_price
)
--  ^ no quantity column. Not even a zero.

-- LAYER 2 · per store. MANY rows may point at one card_id.
store_inventory (
  store_id, card_id, condition, language, finish,   -- the key
  quantity, price                                   -- the numbers
)
--  ^ Starter caps a store at 20,000 rows here. THIS is the billing unit.

-- LAYER 3 · history. The only layer that names a person.
counter_sales (
  sale_id, store_id, customer_id, sold_at,
  card_id, condition, language, finish, qty, paid
)

So inventory is not a property of a card — it is a property of a (store, card, condition, language, finish) combination. One Charizard row in cards can have six rows in store_inventory for a single shop: near-mint English, played English, near-mint Japanese, and so on, each with its own quantity and its own price.

Which makes the counter sale one small write, and it lands in layer 2 — never in layer 1:

UPDATE store_inventory SET quantity = quantity - 1
WHERE store_id = 17 AND card_id = 2
  AND condition = 'NM' AND language = 'EN' AND finish = 'non-foil';

A push job then copies that new quantity into Shopify. That copy is the thing that goes stale, and keeping it honest is the job — Lesson 3 is entirely about this one write.

The test that sorts any field into the right layer Ask: "if this shop closed tomorrow, does the row still make sense?" The card still exists → reference data, shared, keep it. The three in stock die with the shop → inventory. The sale must survive forever for finance, returns and tax → a transaction. Three answers, three homes. Use this live if they hand you a field and ask where you would put it.

Honesty note. Their site publishes no schema, so the table and column names above are invented for teaching — do not quote them as if you had seen their database. What is grounded is the shape their own published facts imply: a shared catalogue, a per-store item count as the billing unit, a kiosk that takes the money. In the call, say "I would expect" and "the shape I'd assume is" rather than "you have". You keep the credibility of having thought it through without claiming knowledge you do not have — and if they correct you, that is a good conversation rather than a caught bluff.

3. Why the counter is the hard part

From the POS sub-course, the organising question for any retail system is: who owns the transaction?

For Game Locker, the answer is unusual and important. Neither of them owns it — a third-party till does. Not Shopify POS, and not Game Locker, which is not a point of sale. That means:

IF THE MERCHANT USED SHOPIFY POS: counter sale ──► Shopify ──► stock decremented in Shopify online store sees it instantly → there is nothing to sync. The problem does not exist. BUT GAME LOCKER'S KIOSK OWNS THE COUNTER: counter sale ──► Game Locker ──► stock decremented in GL │ then, some time later, ▼ Shopify learns → between those two moments, SHOPIFY IS WRONG. And Shopify is the one taking money from strangers.

This is the single most likely deep technical conversation in the interview, because it is their daily reality. Cards make it worse than normal retail in three specific ways:

  • Stock is often exactly 1. A near-mint first-edition card is a one-of-one item. There is no buffer to hide behind — the difference between 1 and 0 is the entire inventory.
  • Value is concentrated. Overselling a $0.20 common is an apology. Overselling a $2,000 card is a serious problem with a serious customer.
  • Cards are browsed in person. A collector standing at the counter and a collector on the website want the same physical card at the same moment, far more often than in ordinary retail.
Memorise this one

"Because a third-party till owns the transaction and Shopify is two hops downstream, there is always a window where Shopify is wrong — and it is wider than one integration, because the till's data arrives by import. I don't try to remove the window — I make sure Shopify never promises a unit it might not have."

The three arrows in plain words

That table is dense on purpose, so here is the same thing with the jargon removed.

Arrow 2 — "the same stock pool" just means the same box of real cards. The shop has one Charizard, and there are two doors it can leave by:

ONE physical Charizard in one box ╱ ╲ ╱ ╲ THE COUNTER THE WEBSITE (someone standing (someone in another in the shop) city, on a phone) Both doors are open at the same time. Both can sell it. Only ONE card exists. If both sell it, one customer paid for nothing.

Arrow 3 has three words worth pinning down, because two of them are easy to read backwards:

WordPlain meaning
ThemeShopify's word for a website's code and design — the layout, the product page, the search results.
"their customers"The game stores. Not the collectors. Game Locker sells software to shops, so a shop is their customer and a collector is the shop's customer.
The marketplaceThe new site where many sellers sell together, instead of one shop selling alone.

So arrow 3 is: you write the website code that the game shops put in front of collectors.

Why "quietly, loudly, visibly" decides the fix

This is the part worth carrying into the interview. Each arrow tells you it is broken in a different way — and how you find out is what decides the tool.

ArrowHow you find outSo the tool is…
1. Sync — fails quietly You do not. Not until someone counts the shelf. Detect. A nightly job that compares both sides and reports what does not match.
2. POS — fails loudly An angry customer, today. Email, or a public review. Prevent. Push on the event, re-check at checkout, buffer expensive cards.
3. Storefront — fails visibly You open it on a phone and see it. No investigation needed. Test. Performance budgets, real devices, real shop wifi.
The reason you cannot use one tool for all three A nightly reconciler will never catch an oversell. By the time it runs, the counter sale has been pushed and both systems say 0 — the numbers agree. The only trace is an order nobody can fulfil. So detection is the right tool for drift and the wrong tool for overselling, which must be prevented instead. And no amount of reconciliation makes a slow page fast. Three failure modes, three tools — that is why the lesson holds them apart rather than calling everything "the sync problem".
The plain version, if you get flustered

"Three jobs. One: keep the two systems' numbers matching — that breaks silently, so I would build a nightly check. Two: stop the counter and the website selling the same card — that breaks with an angry customer, so I would prevent it rather than detect it. Three: build the website code the shops use — that breaks in a way you can see, so I would test it on a real phone."

5. What "our customers" changes

One phrase in the job description is easy to skim past and it matters: "work with our marketing team and customers to build their desired front ends."

Game Locker's customers are game stores. So you are not maintaining one theme — you are potentially maintaining a theme across many merchants who each want something a bit different. That is a completely different engineering problem from a single storefront:

  • You cannot fork the theme per store, or you will be maintaining N codebases forever.
  • Differences have to become settings — theme settings, section settings, blocks the merchant can rearrange — instead of code branches.
  • A bug you ship reaches every store at once. So does a fix.

This is exactly what Online Store 2.0 was designed for, which is why the job description names sections, blocks and JSON templates specifically. Lesson 5 covers how you would actually do it.

A question you can ask them "When a store wants something the shared theme doesn't do, where do you draw the line between a new theme setting and custom work for that store?" — this shows you already understand that the multi-tenant theme problem is the real one, and it invites them to talk about their actual pain.

6. What they are building next, and why they are hiring

The job description says they are "expanding with a new TCG marketplace… a fast, trustworthy, and delightful way to discover, buy, and sell cards."

Read that carefully. "and sell" means many sellers. Their existing product serves stores one at a time; a marketplace pools them. That is a genuinely different system, and it is almost certainly why this role exists now rather than last year.

Which gives you the strategic read on the role: they need someone who can keep the existing Shopify integration healthy while a marketplace is built alongside it. Lesson 6 covers the marketplace architectures — including the awkward truth that Shopify does not natively do multi-seller marketplaces, so this is real design work with real trade-offs.
