📦 Delivery sub-course: Shopify data migration

"Here is their old data, get it into Shopify" is the job you will actually be handed, and almost every way it fails is silent — the import succeeds and the data is wrong. Nine lessons: the eight-word question that tells a migration from a sync, the three buckets of what moves and what never moves (passwords cannot, and orders have no CSV import), the matching key that makes the whole job re-runnable — Shopify's own custom ID in an id-type metafield, which productSet upserts against — the daily variant throttle that can turn a month into three hundred days, the five defaults in orderCreate that quietly rewrite history, the ID reset that makes a Shopify-to-Shopify move two passes, and a cutover where the rollback is DNS, not data. Complements L11 (the ongoing sync) and L18 (bulk mechanics) rather than repeating them.

The big picture — how it all connects

Think of the whole interview as one question asked many ways: "Can you build and run a Shopify backend that talks to an ERP, at scale, without breaking?" Every lesson below is one piece of that answer. This is the zoomed-out map — each topic links to its lesson, and each chapter shows the "why?" follow-ups the client will push on.

Chapter 1 Know the platform deeper than a front-end developer

It starts with how you talk to Shopify at all: the two APIs and their rate limits (L1) — Admin GraphQL is primary, and the limit is a calculated query cost, not a simple request count. Shopify pushes data to you through webhooks (L2): verify the HMAC on the raw body, reply 200 in under five seconds, and survive retries and duplicates. Before any of that, your app must log in — session tokens and token exchange (L3) — and you should be able to draw the anatomy of a full Shopify app (L4): frontend, backend, scopes, and webhooks as one picture. All of it serves the real goal of this role — the integration patterns (L5) that connect Shopify to an ERP, CRM, or fulfillment system. Large data moves safely with bulk operations (L18) — and when the move is one-time rather than ongoing, it becomes a data migration, a job with its own shape: a matching key that makes it re-runnable, a reconciliation report as the real deliverable, and a rollback that is DNS rather than data. The money-and-shipping edges are payment and fulfillment integrations (L27). At the high end, enterprise clients run Shopify Plus (L25), customize the checkout (L23), and push their own logic inside Shopify with Functions (L24). And when the client also sells in a physical shop, the platform grows a second face — a till, per-store stock, and a pickup order that has to reach the right shelf. That side is POS & In-Store, where the sharpest question is not which vendor to use but who owns the transaction — the same system-of-truth argument as the ERP sync above, moved into the shop.

They will push here
  • Your nightly sync gets throttled at 2am — how do you find which query is burning the cost budget, and how do you fix it?
  • A webhook handler is slow. What happens? Now the same webhook arrives twice — how do you dedupe it?
  • Why token exchange instead of the old OAuth redirect? What problem did it solve?
  • REST is legacy — so when, if ever, would you still reach for it?

Chapter 2 Knowing Shopify is not the job — server-side engineering is

Under every integration sits Node: you already own the event loop (L6), so the client will stretch it into harder "what runs first?" follow-ups. The app itself is likely a NestJS (L7) project — modules, providers, dependency injection, guards. You expose it through careful API design (L8) (REST vs GraphQL, versioning, errors, pagination) and store state in the right database — SQL vs NoSQL (L9). The piece that makes a sync reliable is a message queue — event-driven messaging: Kafka vs RabbitMQ vs SQS (L12) — so Shopify and the ERP never block each other. Finally the code has to ship and run: Docker, CI/CD, and cloud (L13), and it has to be proven with tests (L14).

They will push here
  • In one await chain with a setTimeout and a Promise.then — what order do they run, and why?
  • Why dependency injection at all? What does NestJS give you that a plain Express app does not?
  • This ERP data is relational but the sync log is write-heavy and append-only — SQL, NoSQL, or both? Defend it.
  • How do you test a webhook handler without ever calling the real Shopify API?

Chapter 3 Put the two halves together

Design questions combine everything above. First the vocabulary — system design fundamentals (L10): load balancing, caching, queues, scaling, trade-offs. Then the one design that defines this role — Shopify ↔ ERP/CRM sync at scale (L11), where the two APIs, webhooks, a queue, background workers, and a database all appear on the same whiteboard.

They will push here
  • A product changes in Shopify and in the ERP at the same second. Which one wins, and how do you stop them fighting forever?
  • Shopify sends 50,000 webhooks in a burst. Draw what does not fall over.
  • Where do you add a cache, and how do you stop it serving stale stock levels?

Chapter 4 Round 1 asked you to talk — the client may ask you to build

The katas turn every idea above into muscle memory. Build a webhook handler (K1) from blank; fix a race-condition double-charge (K2); build an idempotent ERP sync worker (K3), a NestJS orders module (K4), and a retry + dead-letter queue (K5). Each kata maps straight back to a lesson — K1→L2, K2→L6, K3→L11, K4→L7, K5→L12 — so live coding is just the theory, typed out loud.

They will push here
  • "Make this handler idempotent" — do it live. Where exactly do you store the dedupe key, and when?
  • The double-charge bug: talk me through how you found the race before you fixed it.
  • A job has failed five times. What happens next, and who gets told?

Chapter 5 The chapter that wins offers

Technical skill gets you shortlisted; the human signals get you picked. Tell a clear senior narrative in plain English (L15), show how you deliver in an agile team (L17), how you own ambiguous scope (L19), estimate and pace solo work (L20), hold several projects at once (L21), and keep a busy, slow client informed (L22). You prove seniority by mentoring and reviewing code (L28), staying calm and thinking out loud under pressure (L29) — the safety net for live coding, and the same routine for a broken live store — and understanding what it takes to be the one they pick (L26). Then rehearse it: the four hard moments for composure, how I run my work for the delivery questions, and your Shopify war stories for the proof you have shipped this.

They will push here
  • Tell me about a time you shipped the wrong thing. What did you change so it would not happen again?
  • The client goes quiet for a week and you are blocked. Walk me through exactly what you send them.
  • You are stuck on the live-coding task with five minutes left. What do you say out loud?
  • A junior's pull request is wrong but they are proud of it. How do you review it?
