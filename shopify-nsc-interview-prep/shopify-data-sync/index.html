Shopify Data Sync

One card shop, built forward. Eleven lessons that start with a Shopify store selling trading cards, put the Game Locker platform behind the shop, and then fix — one decision at a time — every way three systems can disagree about a single $480 Charizard.

Why this sub-course exists: sync is the centre of the Game Locker job, and in this course it was taught everywhere and nowhere — a section inside twelve pages across four sub-courses, never the subject of its own. The data migration sub-course even says so in writing: "It does not teach ongoing sync. Different problem, different lesson." This is that lesson.
Two ways to use this, depending on how long you have One evening? Go to the review path. It routes you through the sync material that already exists in this repo, in the right order, with the one line each page exists to teach. No new reading.
Longer? Read the eleven lessons. About half retell the core in one place around a single store; the other half teach six things no page in this repo covers.

The big picture

The whole sub-course follows one shop. Harbour City Cards is a Sydney card shop with about 12,000 card variants and three systems that each think they know how many cards there are: Binder POS at the counter, Game Locker as the back office, and Shopify as the website. It is store 17 on the platform, and the card everything happens to is the $480 near-mint Charizard. Open that page first and keep it beside the lessons.

Corrected 2026-08-06 — and the correction made the course better An earlier version of this sub-course said Game Locker's kiosk was a POS at the counter, and that Game Locker had its own eBay connector. Both were wrong, and their site says so plainly. The kiosk is customer-facing self-service — collectors browse and submit orders, no money moves. Game Locker is not a point of sale at all; it accepts CSV imports from tills like Binder POS and Storepass. And eBay is reached through a Shopify app, not directly.

Both corrections make the problem harder and the lessons sharper. There are now two gaps, not one — and the wider one, the till reaching Game Locker, is not yours to fix. And because eBay hangs off Shopify, Shopify is the hub every channel depends on rather than one channel of several, which is the opposite of what the old version argued.

Then build it forward. The store on Shopify, and nothing else starts with one system and no integration at all, because a single system is always correct — not because it is well built, but because "correct" means agreeing with itself and there is nothing to disagree with. It fixes where a quantity actually lives (an InventoryLevel, not the variant) and what the product page really promises: not reality, but the last thing Shopify was told. Then at 14:05 on a Saturday the shop sells the same card twice with no bug in any component — the defect is staleness, not incorrectness, and that distinction decides every fix that follows. It also counts the gaps, and there are two: the till reaching the back office, and the back office reaching Shopify. Only the second one is yours.

Game Locker behind the shop puts the second and third systems in place and names the relationship that frames the whole role: the platform is the system of record and Shopify is a channel hanging off it. Then it does the most useful thing a lesson about a platform can do — it says what the platform is not. It is not a point of sale, so there is no counter-sale event to hook and the till's data arrives by CSV. And it has no direct eBay integration, which reverses the obvious conclusion: because eBay hangs off Shopify, Shopify is the hub every channel depends on, so the data you push there is load-bearing. It separates the 145-million-card catalogue from the 12,000 cards in the box, refuses the phrase everybody uses — "keep them in sync" is not a specification — and finds the one genuinely hard row: quantity, because three doors draw on one box and the third one, the kiosk, holds a card without taking any money.

Ownership, per field not per record is where this sub-course starts teaching what the repo does not. "Game Locker owns the product" sounds finished and answers one of nine writable fields. So the merchant rewrites a description on Tuesday night, the nightly push flattens it on Wednesday, and she quietly concludes the Shopify admin does not work — which is the real damage, because a silent ownership conflict does not look like a bug, it looks like a merchant who gave up. The rule is to choose per field by the cost of being wrong: overwrite money, respect craft, alarm when unsure. And because Shopify will not tell you who wrote a field, you answer it yourself by storing what you last pushed — if Shopify's value differs from that, somebody else wrote it.

Absolute writes, and why replay must be safe makes duplicates ordinary rather than exceptional: a timeout, a re-sent webhook, a deploy mid-batch, support clicking re-sync — four normal things, none of them a defect. Absolute writes make all four boring, and compareQuantity is a stop sign rather than a fix — the re-read is what finds the truth. It also splits "use an idempotency key" into the two different keys it really is: the webhook id catches a repeated delivery, the order id catches the same sale arriving by any route.

Deletes, archives and tombstones is the biggest gap in the repo and the most dangerous lesson here. Every other write is a hypothesis you can correct; a delete is a commitment. It sorts the five ways to say "gone" — quantity 0, the newer UNLISTED status, DRAFT, ARCHIVED, and permanent deletion — with what each does to old orders and to the URL, and it says plainly which URL behaviour Shopify's docs do not state. Then the two ideas that make it safe: absence is not a message, so removals need tombstones carrying a reason; and the 2am empty file, where a dropped connection produces a valid empty list and a correctly-reasoning sync deletes a whole catalogue with no bug anywhere.

Clocks, order and staleness takes apart "latest wins". It is not a rule, it is an assumption — that two systems' clocks are comparable. Shopify guarantees no ordering at all, gives you X-Shopify-Triggered-At to order its own events, and the rule that follows is worth memorising: never compare one system's clock to another system's clock — between systems, use ownership. Which is why Lesson 3 exists at all. It also shows that ordering per card is enough, and that your own writes should be ordered by a version number, because a gap in a sequence is evidence while a gap between timestamps looks like a quiet afternoon.

Webhooks are a hint, not a guarantee handles the return path. Three promises Shopify does not make, the retry ladder that deletes the subscription after about eight failures over four hours — including the documented condition most people miss — and the echo loop that drops stock by two for one sale and only appears under load. The structural point is the one to carry: no fast path can detect its own silence, so two failures are invisible to every mechanism inside it.

The backlog is release Saturday at 17:00 with zero errors, no alarms, and every stock number forty minutes old. It kills the reflex first — more workers do not help, because Shopify's limit is per app-and-store pair and a Standard-plan shop has about a hundred points per second no matter what you deploy. So the levers are collapsing, batching and prioritising, with one test that decides what may be collapsed: states collapse, events never do. Stock is a state; an order is money and a customer.

Reconciliation, and numbers that do not lie adds the step that makes "heal the explainable" a real test rather than a nice phrase: for each mismatch, ask your own queue — if a pending message explains it, this is lag, not drift. Without that, a busy Saturday produces thousands of meaningless drift rows and nobody ever reads the report again. And it lands the hardest truth in the sub-course: reconciling stock can never find an oversell, because afterwards both systems agree. Agreement is not correctness, which makes the unfulfillable-orders report the only detector that exists.

Store 17 of four hundred widens the frame. Shopify's per-store limits mean it has already isolated the tenants for you — and then you un-isolate them by putting four hundred shops through one worker pool. So first-in-first-out turns out to be unfair by construction, because it rewards volume: one urgent message from a small shop waits behind somebody's 200,000-row backfill. Round-robin per store, backfill in its own low lane, a per-store circuit breaker, and per-store metrics — because on a platform the average tenant is not the tenant who churns.

Finally the sync conversation, and the interview writes down the promise all that machinery exists to keep. The lag stated as a number the merchant agreed to, the trade-offs sorted into your call and hers — only one defence costs real money, and it is the only one that is not yours to decide — and the apology settled before it is needed. Then the rehearsal: three question shapes, a five-stage design script, six answers that sink candidates, a twenty-question interleaved drill, three timed set-pieces and an honest rubric.

Four reference cards carry the compressed version. The store is the shop itself. The ownership matrix is a blank page to fill in during a scoping call, with the lag promise and the apology on the back of it. The conflict cases put all eleven ways two systems can disagree on one page, with a decision tree for "stock is wrong" and the honest count: six rules kill ten of the eleven, and one stays. The one-pager is the last hour before the call.

How this fits the main course

This sub-course is the forever job — the sync that runs every day after the data is in. Its siblings cover the neighbouring problems and it links across rather than repeating them:

  • Shopify Data Migration — the one-time move. It ends at the cutover; this one starts in the hour after it. L10 §5 is the handover, where a backfill and a live sync write to the same store at once.
  • Game Locker — the real job this shop is modelled on, with a 24-hour interview plan. Its Lesson 3 is the single deepest sync page in the repo; read it alongside L04 and L09.
  • POS & In-Store — the counter. The eight inventory states, safety_stock, and why absolute writes beat deltas, from the platform's side.
  • stockinstore Omnichannel — the vendor's version of the same problem, including ingestion, the event backbone, three-way reconciliation and the identifier mapping registry.
  • Main Lesson 11 · Shopify ↔ ERP sync — the same subject as a system-design interview. Read it after L11 as a second rehearsal with a different accent.

What this sub-course deliberately does not do

  • It does not contain implementation code. Mutation names yes, worker bodies no. The decisions are the hard part, and they are what an interview tests.
  • It does not repeat a page that already exists. Where the repo already teaches something well, the lesson retells it inside the shop's story in a paragraph and links to the deep version.
  • It does not leave the shop. If a lesson stops being about Harbour City Cards it has drifted back into being an abstract integration page, which the repo already has four of.
  • It does not state a fact it could not verify. Every platform number was read from shopify.dev or help.shopify.com on 2026-08-06 and quoted. Two things could not be verified — the URL behaviour of an ARCHIVED product, and whether an inventory_levels/update caused by your own write is distinguishable — and both are labelled as unverified on the page rather than guessed.
