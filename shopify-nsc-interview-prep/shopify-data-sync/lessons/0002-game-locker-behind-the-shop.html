Game Locker Behind the Shop

Now the second system goes in. Get its edges right, because the most useful thing to know about a platform is what it is not — and the two things Game Locker is not are exactly the two things most candidates would assume it is.

Your win today: draw the three systems and two gaps from memory, say the two things Game Locker is not and what each one costs the shop, and answer "keep them in sync" with the better question — when they disagree, who is right?

The shop is Harbour City Cards. Everything factual about the Game Locker platform on this page was read from gamelocker.co on 2026-08-06 and is quoted. See also the company brief. Inferences are labelled.

1. What Mia actually bought

Game Locker is not a shop. It is software that card shops buy — "built with game stores, for game stores". Mia's shop is one tenant on it, and in this sub-course it is store 17.

What the platform gives MiaWhy she cannot get it from Shopify
A catalogue of 145,012,624 cards across 18+ gamesShopify has no idea what a Base Set Charizard is. Somebody has to know.
Automated pricing, with daily syncs from global platformsCard prices move daily on outside markets. Nobody types 12,000 prices.
Inventory management — 20,000 items on Starter, unlimited on AdvancedOne stock record per condition, language and finish, priced per card.
Order and customer management, team activity, custom roles, analyticsShe wants one back office, not several.
A customer-facing kiosk — "let customers browse your inventory through an intuitive, self-service interface"A screen in the shop where a collector searches stock and submits an order or a buylist. See §4.

So the shape of the relationship is fixed, and it is the single most useful sentence to have ready:

The sentence that frames the whole role

"Game Locker is the system of record, and Shopify is a channel hanging off it. So the hard part of this work is not the theme — it is keeping stock honest between a shop counter and a website."

2. The two things Game Locker is not

This section exists because getting these wrong makes every later design decision wrong, and because both are easy to assume. They were both assumed in an earlier draft of this sub-course, which is why they are stated so bluntly now.

Not a point of sale

The assumptionWhat their site actually shows
"They sell a POS kiosk, so their kiosk owns the counter and takes the money." No. The kiosk is described as customer self-service: "Let customers browse your inventory through an intuitive, self-service interface." Customers submit orders and buylists. There is no till, no cash drawer, no payment taking.
"So what runs the counter?" Something else. Game Locker offers CSV imports "compatible with major industry platforms (e.g. Binder POS, Storepass)" with ready-made templates — which tells you plainly that the till is a separate product and Game Locker sits behind one.
Why this is the most expensive thing to get wrong If you believe their kiosk owns the counter, you design a counter-sale event that fires the instant a card is sold, and you promise the merchant a few seconds of lag. Neither exists. There is no event to hook, because you do not own the till — and the real path is a CSV that arrives hours later. A design built on the wrong assumption promises something it cannot deliver, and you find that out in front of the merchant.

Not an eBay integration

The assumptionWhat their site actually says
"They integrate with Shopify and eBay, so Shopify is one channel of several.""Whilst we do not directly integrate with eBay, stores can install various eBay connector apps through their Shopify."
The corrected fact leads to a better conclusion than the wrong one did If eBay were a second direct channel, the lesson would be "do not build a Shopify-shaped sync". Because eBay is reached through Shopify, the lesson is the opposite and stronger: Shopify is the hub every other channel hangs off. So the quality of the data you push into Shopify is load-bearing — a wrong stock number there is a wrong number on eBay too, and on whatever channel the shop adds next. Your one integration is not one of several. It is the one they all depend on.

3. The catalogue is not the stock

This confusion sinks candidates, and the numbers make it obvious once you see them side by side.

GAME LOCKER'S CATALOGUE HARBOUR CITY'S STOCK ─────────────────────── ──────────────────── 145,012,624 cards ~12,000 variants every printing ever made the cards in the box shared by every store store 17's own reference data sellable, priced, counted 145 MILLION vs 12 THOUSAND. Ratio of about 12,000 : 1. Mia's Starter tier is capped at 20,000 ITEMS. The 145M catalogue is not her inventory — it is a dictionary.
The mistake this prevents Never push the catalogue into Shopify. A store's Shopify holds only what it physically has. Sending 145 million reference rows to a shop that sells 12,000 of them would take months of API budget, wreck the store's search, and sell nothing. The catalogue answers "what is this card?" The stock answers "do we have one?" — and only the second belongs on the storefront.
Where this is already taught in depth Game Locker L02 §7 works through what actually goes into Shopify and why. This lesson only needs the ratio.

The observation that will separate you from other candidates

"Before I talk about how I would build the sync, I want to name where the biggest error actually is. The gap between the counter till and Game Locker is a CSV measured in hours. The gap I would be building is an API measured in seconds. I can make my half a hundred times faster and the website will still be a day stale. So I would want to fix my half properly and measure the other one, so the shop can see where its real exposure is."

Why the kiosk needs the eight inventory states, not just a number A kiosk order means a card is spoken for without any money having moved and without leaving the shop. If your design only tracks "how many are here?", the same card is offered to a kiosk customer and a website customer at the same time. Shopify already has the right concept — committed, one of the eight states in POS L03 §3. Recognising that a held card is neither available nor sold is exactly the kind of detail that shows you have thought past the happy path.

6. Refuse the phrase "keep them in sync"

Mia will say it. A project manager will say it. It sounds like a requirement. It is not one, because it does not tell you what to build.

"KEEP THEM IN SYNC" hides the only question that matters │ ▼ "WHEN THEY DISAGREE, WHICH ONE IS RIGHT?" Until that is answered, per fact: · every design meeting goes in a circle · every bug is unfixable — because BOTH systems are behaving correctly by their own rules · every fix breaks something else, since there is no rule to check the fix against
Say this early in any sync conversation

"Before I design anything I want to name, for each piece of data, which system owns it. Sync is cheap when there is one owner and one direction. It is close to impossible when both sides can write the same field."

Why "both systems are correct" is the worst kind of bug A normal bug has a component that is wrong, so there is something to fix. Here, Binder POS recorded a real counter sale and Shopify recorded a real online sale. Neither write can be called the mistake. There is nothing to fix inside either system — which is why the answer has to be a rule agreed in advance, not a patch found afterwards.

7. One owner, one direction — and the rows that break it

Apply the rule to the five facts and something encouraging happens.

FactOwnerDirectionHard?
Card name, imageGame LockerGL → ShopifyNo
PriceGame LockerGL → ShopifyNo
OrdersWhoever took the moneyShopify → GL for online
Binder POS → GL for counter, by import		A bit
CustomersContested — pick one, per sidePrefer Shopify → GL, matched on emailA bit
QuantityThree systems decrement it, legitimatelyMany-way ⚠Yes. All of it.

Read the direction column. Four of five are effectively one-way. One-way sync is close to a solved problem: you push, you retry, you reconcile, and replaying it breaks nothing.

Quantity is different, and the reason is physical rather than technical. Three doors sell out of one box. The counter is right to decrement. The website is right to decrement. The kiosk is right to hold one back. You cannot fix it by choosing an owner, because choosing an owner is the tool that solves every other row.

The judgement to state out loud Because many-way is where all the difficulty lives, the correct instinct is to resist adding any more many-way fields. Each new one multiplies the conflict cases you must reason about, and each one arrives disguised as a small convenience — "it would be handy if staff could also fix the title in Shopify". That specific request is Lesson 3, and it is harder than it sounds.

8. Customers deserve a real answer, not a shrug

Several systems create customers. Shopify at checkout, Game Locker at the kiosk and the back office, and the till may hold a name. Most candidates say "match on email" and stop, which is only half an answer.

Why matching on email is imperfectWhat it causes
Counter customers often give no emailNothing to match on at all
A household shares one addressTwo collectors merged into one person
People buy online and walk inThe same human as two records
The answer worth giving Shopify owns the online customer record, Game Locker owns the in-store one, matched on email where it exists and deliberately left separate where it does not. Forcing a merge on a fuzzy match produces wrong loyalty balances — and a wrong loyalty balance makes a customer angrier than a duplicate row ever does. Choosing the less tidy option because the tidy one fails louder is exactly the trade-off an interviewer is listening for.
