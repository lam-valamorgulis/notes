Lesson 3 · Shopify Data Sync · The decision

Ownership, Per Field Not Per Record

"Game Locker owns the product" sounds finished. Then Mia rewrites a card description in the Shopify admin on Tuesday night, the nightly push flattens it on Wednesday morning, and she does it again — and now you have a fight between two systems that are both doing what you told them.

Your win today: explain why record-level ownership is not enough, name the three honest answers to a human edit and what each one costs, and describe how you would detect a human edit when Shopify will not tell you who wrote a field.

This is a new lesson. The rest of the repo owns data per record; nothing in it owns data per field. Read L02 first — this lesson takes its five-row matrix and breaks the "products" row apart.

1. Record-level ownership is a lie you can live with for a month

Lesson 2 ended with a clean matrix. "Game Locker owns products, one way out." Ship it, and for about a month it is true.

Then look at what "a product" actually contains:

ONE PRODUCT ROW — "Charizard · Base Set · 1999" title ← who owns this? description ← who owns this? images ← who owns this? SEO title/desc ← who owns this? tags ← who owns this? collections ← who owns this? status ← who owns this? price (variant) ← Game Locker. Obviously. quantity ← both. That is Lesson 4. "Game Locker owns the product" answered ONE of nine.
Why this shows up late, and hurts On day one nobody edits anything in the Shopify admin, so record-level ownership looks correct. The conflict appears only once the shop is succeeding — Mia starts writing better descriptions, adding her own photos of the actual card, and tuning SEO because a collector search brought her a sale. In other words, the fight starts exactly when the store starts working. That is a bad time to discover you have no rule.

2. Tuesday night

Follow one field for two days. Nothing here is exotic. This is a normal week.

TUE 21:40 Mia edits the Charizard description in the Shopify admin. She writes three good sentences about the centring and the holo pattern — things only someone holding the card knows. TUE 21:40 Shopify saves it. Correct. Game Locker: never hears. It has its own generic catalogue description. WED 02:00 The nightly push runs. Game Locker sends the product, including its description field. Shopify: Mia's three sentences are GONE. WED 09:15 Mia notices. She writes them again. THU 02:00 The nightly push runs. → Every night, the system deletes her work. Nobody wrote a bug. The push is doing its job.
What Mia concludes, which is the real damage She does not file a ticket saying "field ownership is ambiguous". She concludes "the Shopify admin doesn't work properly" and stops using it. Then the store's product pages stay generic forever, its search traffic stays flat, and nobody connects that outcome to a sync decision made months earlier. A silent ownership conflict does not look like a bug. It looks like a merchant who gave up.

3. The three honest answers

There are exactly three things you can do, and the mistake is picking one for the whole product instead of per field.

A · OverwriteB · RespectC · Alarm

Good forFields where being wrong costs money — price above all.Fields where the human is genuinely better informed — description, images, SEO.Fields where you cannot tell yet, and for the first month of any new field.

The line that makes this a senior answer Pick per field, using the cost of being wrong — not per record, and not by which system feels more important. Price is money, so Game Locker overwrites. A description is craft, so the human wins. Quantity is neither: it is the genuinely two-way row, and it gets its own three lessons. One rule for all nine fields is the actual mistake.

4. But how do you even know a human edited it?

This is the part that is missing from every other page in this repo, and it is the practical heart of the lesson.

You want to ask Shopify "who last wrote this field?" You cannot. A products/update webhook tells you the product changed. It does not hand you an author, and Shopify's webhook documentation does not state whether an update caused by your own app's write is distinguishable from one caused by a human in the admin.

Do not build on an unverified assumption here It is tempting to assume "the webhook will not fire for my own writes" or "it will, and I can tell". Neither is documented. Verify it against a real store before relying on it. And prefer a design that is correct either way — which is what the next section is.

The answer: keep your own copy of what you last sent

Stop asking Shopify who wrote it. Answer the question yourself, with a fact you already have.

STORE THREE VALUES PER SYNCED FIELD, IN GAME LOCKER 1. gl_value what Game Locker believes now 2. last_pushed what we last SENT to Shopify 3. shopify_value what Shopify holds now (from the read-back) THEN THE COMPARISON IS TRIVIAL: shopify_value == last_pushed → nobody touched it. Safe to push freely. shopify_value != last_pushed → SOMEONE ELSE WROTE IT. It was not us — we know what we sent. And that is the whole detection. No audit log, no author field, no guessing from a webhook.

Now each of the three answers becomes something you can actually implement:

When shopify_value != last_pushedWhat the rule does
OverwritePush gl_value anyway. Update last_pushed.
RespectMark the field locked for this product. Never push it again. Show the lock in Game Locker's UI, or it becomes a mystery in a year.
AlarmPush nothing. Record the difference for a human, with both values side by side.
The nice property of last_pushed It is the same idea as compareQuantity for inventory — "I know what I last said, so I can tell whether the world still agrees with me" — but done in your own database, for fields where Shopify offers no compare-and-set. One concept, two places. Lesson 4 is the platform's version of it.

Count the owners: four to Game Locker, four to Mia, one shared, one genuinely contested. "Game Locker owns the product" was wrong about nearly half the fields.

The merchandising insight hiding in row three "Respect Mia's images" is not a politeness. On a $480 single, the photo of the actual card is the product — a collector is judging centring, edges and the holo pattern from it. A sync that overwrites her photo with catalogue stock art does not just annoy her; it removes the reason someone would pay $480 to a small shop instead of a big one. This is the kind of point that shows you understand the business, and it comes out of a field-ownership table.

6. The contested register

Keep one short written list: every field that both sides can write. Not a wiki page nobody opens — a list you defend the length of.

THE CONTESTED REGISTER — Harbour City Cards FIELD WHY IT IS CONTESTED AGREED RULE ───────────────────────────────────────────────────── quantity two doors, one box two-way, L04–L06 tags both add meaning add-only, never remove LENGTH: 2 rows. That is the target. Every row added multiplies the cases you must reason about. A request to add a third row is a DESIGN CONVERSATION, not a ticket.
The sentence for the meeting where someone asks for a third row

"I can make that field two-way, and I want to be clear what it costs. Every two-way field means agreeing what happens when both sides change it in the same hour — and then testing that. The list is two rows long on purpose. What is the problem behind the request? There may be a one-way way to solve it."

Notice the last sentence. Most requests for a two-way field are really requests for something else — usually "I need to fix a mistake quickly and Game Locker is slower to edit". That is a tooling problem in Game Locker, not an ownership problem in Shopify, and solving it there keeps your register at two rows.
