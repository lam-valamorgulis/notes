Lesson 8 · Shopify Data Sync · When it is behind

The Backlog

Release Saturday, 17:00. Nothing has failed. No alarm is red. Every message will eventually be delivered. And the sync is forty minutes behind, which means every stock number on the website is forty minutes old — so the shop is quietly promising cards it does not have, at the busiest hour of its year.

Your win today: explain why adding more workers does not fix this, name what may be collapsed and what may never be, and give the order you would catch up in — with the reason it is that order.

This is a new lesson. The repo covers not asking Shopify too often (stockinstore L02 §6), which is the opposite problem. Nothing covers being behind.

1. The day

SATURDAY — a new set releases 09:00 Mia lists 800 new cards. Each one is a product and several variants. That is thousands of writes. 10:00 The shop fills up. Tom is selling constantly on the till, and Mia runs the CSV import hourly today instead of nightly — so counter sales arrive in big bursts, hundreds at a time. 10:30 Kiosk orders too. Collectors in the shop are holding cards from the same box. 11:00 The website is busy too. Orders coming back. 13:00 The nightly price job from last night is still finishing its retries. 17:00 QUEUE DEPTH: 24,000 messages OLDEST UNPROCESSED MESSAGE: 40 minutes old ERRORS: zero. FAILED JOBS: zero. ALARMS: none. Everything is working. That is the problem.
Why no alarm fired Almost all monitoring watches for failure. Nothing here failed. Every message will be processed and every write will succeed. The system is correct and useless at the same time — because a stock number that is right about 16:20 is wrong about 17:00, and the collector is buying at 17:00. Lag is a category of fault that error monitoring cannot see.

2. Lag is the metric, not queue depth

Two numbers look similar and only one tells you about risk.

Queue depthLag
MeasuresHow many messages are waitingHow old the oldest waiting message is
24,000 messages, drained in 30 secondsLooks alarmingFine. Lag is 30 seconds.
12 messages, stuck for an hourLooks fineAlarming. Lag is 60 minutes.
Tells youHow much work there isHow wrong the website currently is
The sentence to bring to the monitoring conversation

"I would alarm on lag, not on queue depth. Depth tells me how busy we are. Lag tells me how stale the storefront is — which is the thing that actually costs the merchant money."

3. Why adding workers does not save you

This is the part that separates a real answer from a rehearsed one. The instinct is to scale out. Here is the ceiling that makes it pointless.

SHOPIFY'S GRAPHQL ADMIN API RATE LIMIT (read from shopify.dev, 2026-08-06) scope: the combination of the APP and the STORE Standard plan 100 points per second Advanced Shopify 200 points per second Shopify Plus 1000 points per second enterprise 2000 points per second single query max 1000 points, any plan HARBOUR CITY CARDS IS ON A STANDARD PLAN. So this store has ~100 points per second. TOTAL. Run 4 workers → still 100 points per second Run 40 workers → still 100 points per second, plus thousands of throttled retries making everything SLOWER The bottleneck is not our compute. It is the store's share of the platform. You cannot buy your way past it with concurrency.
Read the scope line again, because it cuts both ways The limit is per app-and-store pair: "calls from one app don't affect the rate limits of another app, even on the same store. Similarly, calls to one store don't affect the rate limits of another store, even from the same app." That is good news for a platform — Harbour City Cards cannot slow down another shop through Shopify. But it also means this store's ceiling is fixed and cannot be shared or borrowed. It is the wall of this lesson, and it is the setup for Lesson 10, where the shared thing is our own worker pool rather than Shopify's limit.

So the real levers are only three, and none of them is "more machines":

LeverWhat it does
Send fewer messagesCollapse redundant work. §4 — the biggest win by far, and a bursty CSV import is exactly what collapsing is for.
Send more per callBatch and use bulk operations. One call, many rows.
Choose what goes firstPrioritise. §6 — the difference between 40 minutes of risk and 40 minutes of delay.

4. Collapsing — the biggest win

Look at what is actually in the queue for one card on a busy afternoon.

QUEUE, FOR THE SAME CARD, OLDEST FIRST 16:20 set stock = 4 16:31 set stock = 3 16:44 set stock = 2 16:52 set stock = 1 17:00 set stock = 0 ← the truth, right now PROCESS ALL FIVE: 5 API calls. Four of them write a number that is already wrong. The storefront shows 4, then 3, then 2… catching up to reality slowly and publicly. COLLAPSE TO THE NEWEST PER CARD: 1 API call. set stock = 0. Correct immediately. FIVE TIMES less budget spent. Nothing was lost. Because the write is ABSOLUTE, the newest message CONTAINS the whole truth — the earlier four held no information the last one lacks.
Why an hourly CSV import makes collapsing worth even more A live event stream sends one message per sale as it happens. A batch import hands you the whole day's counter sales at once — so a card sold three times today arrives as three messages in the same second, and only the last one matters. The burstier the upstream, the more collapsing saves you. On a release Saturday with hourly imports that is the difference between fitting inside the store's rate limit and not.
The third payoff from choosing absolute writes Lesson 4 chose absolute writes for replay safety. Lesson 6 used that to make dropping stale messages safe. Lesson 8 now uses it again to collapse a backlog by 80%. One decision in the first week, three separate rescues. If you can trace that chain out loud, you are describing a design rather than reciting tips.

5. What may never be collapsed

Collapsing is not a general technique. It is safe for one specific kind of message, and dangerous for the rest.

MessageCollapse?Why
Stock level for a cardYes, keep newest per cardIt is a state. The newest value is the whole truth.
Price for a variantYes, keep newestAlso a state. Nobody needs yesterday's price written first.
An orderNEVERIt is an event. It is money, a customer and a card that must leave the shop. Two orders are not a newer version of one order.
A refund or cancellationNEVERAlso an event, and also money.
A tombstone (L05)NoEach removal has its own reason, and the reason decides the action.
The test that decides it, every time

"Ask whether the message describes a state or an event. States collapse — the newest one replaces the rest and loses nothing. Events never collapse — each one happened separately and each one has a consequence. Stock is a state. An order is an event. Getting that backwards either wastes your entire API budget or loses somebody's money."

The mistake this prevents A tempting shortcut on release Saturday is "the queue is huge, drop the old messages". Do it across the whole queue and you have just discarded orders — real cards that real people paid for, which the shop will now never pick and never post. Collapsing must be done per key and per type, never as a blanket clear-out.

6. Catch-up order

Even after collapsing you may still be behind. So what goes first? Not first-in-first-out — that treats a $480 single like a 50-cent common.

PriorityWhatWhy it beats the row below it
1 Stock going down to zero on quantity-1 cards This is the only message that prevents an oversell. Everything else can be late without anybody being disappointed.
2 Any stock decrease A decrease that is late risks selling something. An increase that is late only risks not selling something.
3 Orders coming back from Shopify The shop needs to pick and post them. Late is bad, not dangerous — the money is already taken.
4 Stock increases Costs a possible sale. Recoverable and invisible.
5 Prices, and new listings Nobody is harmed by yesterday's price for one more hour.
The asymmetry that makes rows 2 and 4 different A late decrease means the website offers something the shop cannot supply — a taken payment, an apology, a refund, possibly a public complaint in a hobby community where people talk to each other. A late increase means the website says sold out when a card is available — a missed sale nobody ever knows about. Both are "stock is wrong". One costs trust and one costs a maybe. Priority follows the cost, not the symmetry of the data.
Why priority 1 is cheap to implement You already know which cards are quantity 1, and the store page shows why it matters: singles are where the money and the anger live, and sealed product barely cares. So a two-lane queue — "single copies going to zero" and "everything else" — gets most of the benefit with very little machinery. It is a small change that buys the outcome that matters.

7. Shedding, and the honest limit

If the backlog is growing faster than it drains, no ordering saves you. Then there are only two remaining moves, and both are decisions rather than code.

MoveWhat it meansCost
ShedStop generating low-value messages. Pause the price job. Pause new listings. Keep only stock and orders moving.Prices go stale for a day. Cheap, and reversible with a switch.
Reduce the promiseAsk Mia to move high-value singles to safety_stock for the afternoon, so the website stops offering the last copy at all.Fewer sales today. Zero oversells today. Her call, not yours.
The answer to "so what do you do when you cannot keep up?"

"I stop pretending the storefront is current, and I make it promise less until we are caught up. Pausing the price job and holding back the last copy of expensive singles for one afternoon costs the shop a few maybes. Selling forty cards we do not have costs the shop forty apologies and its reputation in a community where collectors talk to each other."
