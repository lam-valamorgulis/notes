Lesson 10 · Shopify Data Sync · At size

Store 17 of Four Hundred

Everything so far has been one shop. Now widen the frame: Harbour City Cards is one tenant on a platform, and on Tuesday a new shop with 200,000 cards starts its first import. Mia's Charizard sells at the counter that afternoon, and her stock push is queued behind somebody else's backfill.

Your win today: say exactly what is per store and what is shared, explain why first-in-first-out is unfair by construction, and describe the two guards that stop one shop's bad day from becoming four hundred bad days.

This is a new lesson. stockinstore L09 covers multi-tenancy in general; nothing in the repo covers the sync queue across tenants.

1. The good news, from the rate-limit docs

Start with the fact that makes this manageable, because it removes a whole class of worry.

shopify.dev, API rate limits — quoted

"GraphQL Admin API rate limits are based on the combination of the app and store. This means that calls from one app don't affect the rate limits of another app, even on the same store. Similarly, calls to one store don't affect the rate limits of another store, even from the same app."

SO, THROUGH SHOPIFY: store 17 ──► its own ~100 points/sec ──► Shopify store 218 ──► its own ~100 points/sec ──► Shopify store 331 ──► its own ~100 points/sec ──► Shopify Store 218 hammering its limit CANNOT slow store 17 down. Shopify has already isolated the tenants for you. ⚠ AND YET store 218 can still ruin store 17's afternoon. Because the thing they share is not Shopify. It is US.

2. What is per store, and what is shared

This table is the lesson. Everything else follows from it.

Per store — isolated for freeShared — your problem to isolate
Shopify rate limit (app + store)The worker pool
The access token and scopesThe queue, unless you partition it
The store's own data and catalogueThe database and its connection pool
Its plan, and therefore its ceilingThe deploy — every store gets it at once
Its webhook subscriptionsEvery bug you write
The sentence that reframes the whole lesson Shopify isolated the tenants. You un-isolated them. Every fairness problem below exists because we put four hundred shops through one pool of workers and one queue. That is a reasonable thing to do — running four hundred separate deployments would be worse — but it means tenant fairness is entirely our responsibility, and it will not appear until a big shop joins.

3. Tuesday: the noisy neighbour

09:00 a new shop signs up. 200,000 cards. Onboarding starts. It enqueues 200,000 messages. 09:05 the queue, oldest first: msg 1 … msg 200,000 ← all store 331's backfill 14:05 store 17's hourly import lands. Tom sold the Charizard at the counter an hour ago, and this is the first the platform hears of it. Store 17 enqueues ONE urgent message: "set stock 0" on a $480 single. It is message number 200,001. 14:05 FIFO says: after the other 200,000. 16:40 it finally runs. For 2 hours 35 minutes Mia's website offered a card that was already in somebody's pocket. AND THAT SITS ON TOP OF THE IMPORT DELAY: 13:05 the card actually left the shop 14:05 the platform first heard about it (gap A) 16:40 the website finally caught up (our queue) → 3h 35m total, and we added 2h 35m of it. NOTHING FAILED. The queue worked perfectly. The POLICY was wrong.
The compounding is the point Gap A already cost an hour and you cannot fix it. Then a queue policy you do control added two and a half more. That is the worst possible split: most of the damage was self-inflicted and avoidable, sitting on top of a delay you had already told the merchant about. When you do not own the whole chain, the part you own has to be excellent — because it is the only part you can be held to.
Why FIFO is not neutral — it is actively unfair First-in-first-out feels like the fair default because it has no favourites. But it rewards volume: the more messages you produce, the more of the shared capacity you take. A shop with three messages waits behind a shop with two hundred thousand. Treating every message equally is what makes it unfair to every small shop. Fairness has to be per tenant, not per message.

4. Fair scheduling — one lane per store

NOT ONE QUEUE. ONE LANE PER STORE, SERVED IN TURN. store 17 │ msg │ ← 1 waiting store 218 │ msg │ msg │ ← 2 waiting store 331 │ msg │ msg │ … 200,000 waiting ▲ └─ workers take ONE from each lane, in turn RESULT store 17's single urgent message waits for AT MOST one message from each other active store — not for 200,000 messages from one of them. Wait time now scales with the NUMBER OF ACTIVE STORES, not with the LOUDEST store's volume.
PolicyStore 17's wait for one urgent message
FIFO across all storesUp to 200,000 messages. Hours.
Round-robin per storeUp to one message per active store. Seconds.
Round-robin plus the priority lanes from L08Its own urgent lane, first. Immediate.
The two policies compose, and that is the real answer Lesson 8 prioritised by message type inside one shop. This lesson schedules fairly across shops. Put them together and the rule is: round-robin between stores, and within a store, urgent stock decreases first. Neither alone is enough — priority without fairness starves small shops, and fairness without priority puts a price update ahead of an oversell.

5. Backfill is not sync, and must not share a lane

Store 331's 200,000 messages are a backfill — the one-time load of an existing catalogue. Mia's one message is steady state. They have opposite properties, and mixing them is what caused §3.

BackfillSteady state
Size200,000 messagesA handful per minute
UrgencyNone. Nobody is shopping there yetTotal. A card is being sold right now
Acceptable durationDays. Genuinely.Seconds
If it pauses for an hourNobody noticesAn oversell
So it should beSlow, interruptible, backgroundFast, protected, foreground
The rule

"Backfill runs in a separate, lower-priority lane with a cap on its share of the workers, and it can be paused at any moment without harm. A store that is not live yet has no urgent messages by definition — so it should never be able to delay a store that is trading."

This is the handover from the migration sub-course Shopify Data Migration ends at the cutover. This lesson is the hour after it: the backfill is still running while the sync has already started, so both are writing to the same store at once. Which means the backfill must use the same idempotent absolute writes as the sync (L04) and the same matching key (Migration L03) — or the two of them fight over rows and the newer truth loses to the older import.

6. One broken store must not burn the pool

The other multi-tenant failure runs the opposite way: not too much work from one store, but too much waiting on one store.

store 288's Shopify is having a bad day. Every call takes 30 seconds, then times out. WITHOUT A CIRCUIT BREAKER workers pick up store 288's messages each one occupies a worker for 30 seconds retries put them straight back in the lane → the pool fills with waiting, and the OTHER 399 stores slow down behind a shop that cannot be helped anyway WITH A PER-STORE CIRCUIT BREAKER N consecutive failures for store 288 → OPEN the breaker for that store → stop taking its messages; let them wait → probe occasionally with a single call → recovers on its own when the store does ONE STORE'S OUTAGE STAYS ONE STORE'S OUTAGE.
Why the breaker must be per store, not global A global breaker would react to store 288's failures by pausing everyone — turning one shop's outage into a platform outage, which is the exact opposite of what it is for. The unit of isolation must match the unit of failure, and here the failure is one store's Shopify. Same principle as the per-store deletion budget in L05.

7. Blast radius

The hardest change of mindset. In a single-shop integration, a bad deploy hurts one shop and one merchant phones you. Here it hurts four hundred at once, and you find out from a Discord thread.

GuardWhat it does
Roll out per storeNew sync logic to 1 store, then 10, then the rest. A destructive bug is caught at store one.
Per-store budgetsDeletion budget, heal budget, write budget — all scaled to the store. A shop with 300 cards must not share a threshold with one that has 200,000.
Per-store kill switchTurn the sync off for one shop without a deploy. The first thing you want at 2am, and the thing nobody builds until they need it.
Per-store metricsA platform average hides everything. "Lag is 4 seconds on average" can mean 399 stores at 1 second and one store at 30 minutes.
The averages trap is worth stating carefully Aggregate dashboards are where multi-tenant problems go to hide. One badly broken store barely moves a mean across four hundred. Look at the worst store, not the average one — the highest lag, the highest mismatch rate, the most throttling. On a platform, the average tenant is not the tenant who churns.
