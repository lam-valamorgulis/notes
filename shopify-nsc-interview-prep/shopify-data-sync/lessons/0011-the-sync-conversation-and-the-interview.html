Lesson 11 · Shopify Data Sync · The capstone

The Sync Conversation, and the Interview

Ten lessons of machinery exist to keep one promise. Nobody has written the promise down. This lesson writes it, in words a shopkeeper can hold you to — and then turns all of it into a performance you can give under pressure.

Your win today: state the lag as a number a merchant agreed to, separate what costs money from what costs engineering time, and get through the twenty-question drill out loud without reaching for a lesson.

1. The lag is a promise, and nobody signed it

Every design in this sub-course accepts a delay. That is not a compromise you hide — it is the product.

WHAT ACTUALLY GETS SAID Mia: "So the website will always know what's in stock?" Us: "It syncs, yes." Mia: "Great." WHAT MIA HEARD: always correct WHAT WE MEANT: usually correct within a few seconds WHAT WE BUILT: seconds from Game Locker to the website HOURS from her till to Game Locker overnight for prices minutes, on a release Saturday FOUR different promises, one word — and the one she cares about most is the one we do not control. The difference surfaces on the day someone is angry, which is the worst moment to start negotiating.
Why this is an engineering problem, not an account-management one An unwritten promise is unbounded, so every design trade-off becomes a broken promise afterwards. If nobody agreed a number, then any delay is a failure by definition, and you can never say "this is working as designed" — because nothing was designed, out loud, with the merchant. Naming the lag is what turns future incidents into expected behaviour with a procedure rather than surprises with blame.

2. The conversation, in plain words

This is the version to say to Mia. No jargon. Short sentences. Numbers she can check.

Say it like this

"Your shop sells the same cards three ways — the counter, the website, and the kiosk. They cannot all know something at the same instant, so there is always a gap. And there are really two gaps, which is the part worth being clear about.

The one I am building takes seconds: once Game Locker knows a card is gone, your website knows almost immediately. The one in front of it is your counter till reaching Game Locker, and today that is a file you upload — so it is hours. That second one is the bigger risk, and fixing it is a decision about your till, not about my code.

Prices update overnight. And if we ever do sell the same card twice, you will hear it from us before you hear it from the customer."

3. Separate your decisions from hers

The job description asks for explaining technical trade-offs to non-technical people. This table is what that skill actually looks like.

DefenceCostsWhose call
Shorten gap A — the till import runs automatically and often, instead of a person uploading a fileCoordination with the shop and possibly the till vendorShared. The biggest lever, and not mine to pull alone
Push on the change instead of a batch, once Game Locker knowsA little engineering timeMine. Just do it — biggest win I can deliver alone
Re-verify availability at checkoutEngineering time; a worse moment to disappoint someoneMine, with a warning about the moment
Hold the last copy of expensive singles backReal money. You are choosing not to sell thingsHers. Never decide this for her
Pause the price job on a busy afternoonStale prices for a dayMine, and I will tell her afterwards
The apology: refund, substitute, or holdAlmost nothing to decide, a lot to improviseHers, agreed in advance
The uncomfortable row, and why naming it is the senior move The single biggest improvement available is not in my code. Gap A — the counter till reaching Game Locker — is wider than everything I can build, and I own neither end of it. An engineer who only reports on their own half will make gap B twice as fast and the merchant will still see day-old stock and conclude the sync does not work. So I would measure both gaps and show both numbers, even though only one of them flatters me. That is the difference between doing the ticket and doing the job.
The line that makes this land Only one row costs the business money, and it is the only one that is not my decision. Being able to sort trade-offs into "my call" and "your call" — quickly, and without asking permission for the engineering ones — is the whole skill. Asking a merchant to approve a queue design wastes her time; deciding on her behalf not to sell her most expensive stock is overstepping.

4. The apology, written before it is needed

Decide in advanceFor Harbour City Cards
Who contacts the customer?Mia, same day, by phone for anything over $100
What is offered?Full refund immediately, plus first refusal on the next copy that comes in
Is a substitute offered?Only if the condition and price genuinely match. Never a downgrade dressed up as a favour
Where is it recorded?On the unfulfillable report, so the rate is visible over months

Four decisions, none of them technical, all of them cheap to make now and expensive to improvise. This is the defence everybody forgets, and it is the one that turns an emergency into a procedure.

6. The five-stage design script

When they say "design this", do not start drawing. Use these five stages in order. It takes about eight minutes and it covers everything.

1. OWNERSHIP (2 min) "First, per field, who is allowed to be right?" → five facts, four one-way, quantity two-way → say the phrase: sync is cheap with one owner 2. THE WRITE PATH (2 min) → absolute writes, compareQuantity → replay is normal: timeouts, duplicate webhooks, deploys, support re-syncs → a rejection triggers a RE-READ, not a retry 3. THE READ PATH (1 min) → verify HMAC, enqueue, 200. Never work inline. → 8 failures over ~4 hours can delete the subscription, silently → idempotent on the order id, written FIRST 4. WHEN IT IS WRONG (2 min) → nightly read-back, bulk query → classify: pending message = LAG, not drift → heal the explainable, ALARM on the unexplainable → the unfulfillable report is the only detector for an oversell 5. THE PROMISE (1 min) → name the lag as a number the merchant agreed → and name it as TWO numbers: the gap I own and the gap upstream of me. Report both. → separate what costs money from what costs time → the apology is written down in advance FINISH ON STAGE 5. Almost nobody does, and it is the stage that sounds like someone who has shipped.

7. Six answers that sink candidates

Red flagWhy it failsSay instead
"We'll keep them in sync in real time, both ways." Promises something unachievable across two systems. An interviewer who has run this knows immediately. "Real-time two-way consistency is not achievable across two systems. I would rather name the accepted lag and design the reconciliation."
"We'll shorten the sync interval to fix the oversell." Makes it rarer, never impossible — and "rarer" on a card site is still a real customer every week. "The defect is that Shopify promised a unit it did not have. The fix belongs at the promise, not the interval."
"We'll use last-write-wins on timestamps." Two clocks. Works in testing, fails inside one second on the busy day. L06 "Never compare one system's clock to another's. Between systems, use ownership."
"If the source no longer has it, we delete it." Absence is not a message, and a truncated read looks exactly like a mass deletion. L05 "Tombstones with a reason, a per-store deletion budget, and an empty read treated as a failed read."
"If we're behind, we scale up the workers." The limit is per app-and-store. More workers get the same points plus throttled retries. L08 "Send fewer messages by collapsing per card, send more per call, and choose what goes first."
"A counter sale fires an event and we push it straight away." Assumes a till you do not own emits events. Here it does not — the data arrives as a CSV import. Promising seconds on a path you cannot see is how you lose a merchant's trust in week two. "How does a counter sale reach the platform today? If it is an import, then my half can be seconds and the honest end-to-end number is still hours — and that is the thing to fix first."
"The reconciler fixes any mismatch it finds." Hides the defect causing the drift while the dashboard stays green. "Heal the explainable, alarm on the unexplainable."
