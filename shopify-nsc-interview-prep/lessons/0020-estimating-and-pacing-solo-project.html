Estimating & Pacing a Solo Project

You get a 150-hour scope and one month to finish. No manager will pace you. Here is how to plan it in numbers, attack the risk first, and never surprise the client at the end.

Your win: answer "How do you plan and pace a 150-hour solo project?" — with math, milestones, and risk-first thinking.
If you remember one thing Do the math out loud, then attack the riskiest part first. Everything else in this lesson hangs off that one sentence.

Ties to your mission: the PM's example was exactly this — "a big scope like 150 hours, finish in one month." This lesson turns that into a plan. It builds on L19 (make scope clear first) and L17 (agile delivery).

1. Start with the math — out loud

Recall — cover the pagePause. Divide 150 by 4 in your head and say the weekly hours out loud before you read on.

Seniors think in numbers, not gut feeling. Do the arithmetic in the interview:

150 hours ÷ ~4 weeks = ~37–38 hours per week That is close to a completely full week with ZERO slack. So: if I also run a second project, real available hours drop — I sequence the risky parts, I do not overlap them.

Saying this shows you sanity-check capacity instead of nodding "yes, one month, no problem." A senior who blindly agrees to any deadline is a warning sign.

2. Attack the riskiest unknown first

Juniors do the easy parts first, because it feels productive. Seniors do the scariest, least-understood part first — as a small spike (a short, time-boxed investigation to remove uncertainty).

Why risk-first wins If the hard part (say, Shopify checkout customization or a tricky ERP sync race) is going to blow up your estimate, you want to find that out in week one, when there is time to react — not in week four, when the deadline is gone. Doing the risky part first buys you the ability to re-plan.

3. Estimate with three points, then buffer

A single number ("this is 20 hours") is a guess dressed up as a fact. Use a three-point estimate for the uncertain parts:

Optimistic (O) — everything goes right Most likely (M) — the normal case Pessimistic (P) — things go wrong Expected ≈ (O + 4M + P) ÷ 6 (this is the PERT formula)

This pulls your estimate toward reality and away from wishful thinking. Then add a buffer of 20–30% for the unknowns you cannot see yet. On a 150-hour scope, that buffer is not padding — it is the difference between "on time" and "another late project."

4. Slice into milestones with a clear "done"

Break the month into 3–4 vertical slices, each a thing you can demo. A vertical slice is a small piece that works end to end — not "all the database, then all the API." Example for a Shopify ↔ ERP sync (L11):

WeekMilestone (demo-able)Definition of done
1Spike: one product syncs Shopify → ERPRuns on a dev store, handles one webhook, idempotent
2Full product + inventory syncBulk import works, retries on failure, logged
3Order sync + error handlingOrders flow both ways, dead-letter queue for failures
4Hardening + handoverTests pass, deployed to staging, docs written, demoed

"Done" is never "it works on my machine." It means tested, deployed to a dev or staging store, and shown. Each milestone is a natural moment to catch "that's not what I meant" while it is still cheap to change.

5. Track your burn and flag early

"Track your burn" just means track the hours you have used. Each week, compare hours used vs hours left against the plan. If you are trending over, you say so the moment you see it, with options — not on the last day. A client hates a surprise on the deadline far more than an early, honest heads-up.

The trap: the 90% done wall "It's 90% done" for three weeks straight is the classic failure. It happens when you do the easy 90% first and leave all the hard, uncertain 10% for the end. Risk-first slicing (step 2) is the cure: the scary 10% is done in week one, so "90% done" actually means 90% done.
Say it like this

"First I do the math — 150 hours over a month is about 38 hours a week, so it's a completely full week with no slack, and I say that up front. I break it into three or four milestones I can demo, and I attack the riskiest, least-clear part first as a small spike, so I hit the hard surprises in week one, not week four. I estimate the uncertain parts with a three-point method and add a 20–30% buffer. Then I track hours against the plan weekly and raise a flag with options the moment the trend goes wrong."
