Communicating with a Busy, Slow Client

The client is busy and slow to reply, but still wants a great result. The way you communicate is what makes you feel senior and trustworthy. This is the client-facing half of ownership.

Your win: answer "The client is busy and rarely replies — how do you still deliver a good result?"

Ties to your mission: the PM said the client is busy, has slow responses, and still expects a good outcome. This is the communication layer over L19 (own the scope) and connects to L15 (senior narrative).

Boundary — L19 vs L22 L19 is how you think about scope inside your own head. L22 is what you type to the client. Same trust test, two sides: L19 builds the method, this lesson writes the message.

1. The one idea: reduce the client's effort to almost zero

A busy client is not ignoring you — they have no spare energy. So your job is to make every message you send need the least possible effort to act on. If a reply needs five minutes of thinking, it waits a week. If it needs a one-tap yes/no, it comes back today.

The one-tap rule Before you send anything, ask: "Can the client act on this in under 30 seconds?" If not, rewrite it. Turn open questions into A/B choices. Turn "what should I do?" into "I plan to do X — okay?" You do the thinking; they only decide.

2. Default to action

This is the most important habit with a slow client. Never send a question that blocks you. Send a decision with a deadline and a default:

"I'll set the sync to run every 15 minutes unless you'd prefer real-time.
 I'll go with 15 min if I don't hear back by Thursday."

Now their silence is a valid answer. You are never stuck, and you never look like you were sitting idle waiting. (This is the same "assumptions, not questions" idea from L19, aimed at the message you actually send.)

3. Communicate async and in writing

A busy client cannot take many calls. Lean on written, async updates they can read whenever they have a minute. Send a short weekly note in a fixed shape so it is easy to scan:

DONE this week: product sync live on dev store NEXT week: order sync + retry handling RISKS: ERP test credentials still pending (blocks order sync) NEED FROM YOU: 1 decision — 15-min sync vs real-time? (default: 15-min)

Even if they never reply, this note quietly builds trust and leaves a clear record. It also makes the one decision you need impossible to miss.

4. Bring options, not problems

When something goes wrong, a junior sends "X is broken, what do I do?" — which hands the work back to the busy client. A senior sends the problem with a recommendation:

Junior messageSenior message
"The ERP API is rate-limiting us. What should I do?""The ERP API rate-limits us at peak. I recommend queuing writes and retrying — adds ~1 day, no data loss. Alternative: sync less often. I'll go with the queue unless you object by Fri."

You did the analysis, you have a plan, you only need a nod. That is what senior looks like in a message.

5. No surprises — ever

The golden rule of client trust: bad news early beats bad news late. If a deadline will slip, say it the moment you know, with a new plan. A client can handle "we'll be two days late, here's why and here's the fix." A client cannot forgive finding out on the deadline day. Over-communicate progress, and under-promise on dates.

The trap: going silent because "there's nothing to show yet" When work is hard or slow, the temptation is to go quiet until you have something impressive. To a busy client, silence reads as "nothing is happening." A three-line "still on track, here's what I'm doing" costs you a minute and keeps their trust. Silence spends trust you cannot easily earn back.
Say it like this

"With a busy client, I make every message cost them almost no effort. I default to action — instead of a blocking question, I send a decision with a default: 'I'll do X unless you say otherwise by Thursday,' so their silence still moves the project. I send short written weekly updates in a fixed shape — done, next, risks, what I need — so they can scan it anytime. When something breaks, I bring options and a recommendation, not just a problem. And I never surprise them: if a date will slip, they hear it early with a fix."
