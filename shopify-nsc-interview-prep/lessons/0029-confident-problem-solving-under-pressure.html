Lesson 29 · Confidence · Live rounds and real work

Confident Problem-Solving Under Pressure

Two rooms, one skill. In the interview they watch how you behave in the ten seconds when you don't know. On a live store they find out how you behave in the ten minutes when checkout is broken. This lesson trains both until they are habits.

Your win today: two fixed routines. Part A — think out loud, buy time, get unstuck, recover from your own bug, so you look senior in a live-coding round. Part B — stabilise, narrow, update, guard, so you stay calm on a real Shopify store when it is broken and the client is watching.

Ties to your mission: the client may run live coding and push "why?" three times deep. Facts you can revise. Composure you have to rehearse. Part A is that rehearsal. Part B is the same composure in your day job as a Shopify theme + backend developer, where the store is live and a wrong move costs the client money.

Part A · In the live-coding round — someone is watching you think

If you remember one thing (Part A) Never go silent. When a hard moment hits, your first move is always to say a short bridge sentence out loud — not to freeze. Everything else in Part A hangs off that one habit.

1. The real thing being scored

When a senior interviewer gives you a problem, they already expect you might not know it. What they watch is different:

What they are actually deciding "When this person hits something they don't know on our project — and they will — do they freeze, guess, and hide it? Or do they stay calm, break it down, and talk me through their thinking so I can trust them alone?"

So the goal is not to avoid the hard moment. The goal is to perform well inside it. A candidate who reasons out loud through a problem they half-know beats a candidate who answers fast but goes silent the moment it gets hard.

2. Think out loud — the narration habit

Silence is the enemy. If you go quiet, the interviewer sees a blank screen and assumes you are lost. Narrate a thin layer above what you are doing. Say the plan before you type it.

The narration order 1. Restate the problem in your own words. 2. State your assumptions out loud. 3. Say the plan in 2–3 steps. 4. Then code, narrating each step. 5. Name trade-offs as you make them.

Concrete example for a webhook task:

Say it like this "Okay — so I need to receive an order webhook and update our ERP. Two assumptions: the payload is Shopify's standard order JSON, and I have an ERP client already. My plan: verify the HMAC on the raw body first, ack 200 fast, then push the job to a queue so the ERP write happens off the request. Let me start with the verify step…"

Notice what that did: it showed judgment (verify first, ack fast, queue the work) before a single line of code. Even if the code stumbles, the thinking already scored.

The mechanics — typing while talking is hard Two practical moves most people miss: narrate in the gaps between typing, not while your fingers move (talking and typing at once makes both worse). And it is senior, not weak, to say "I'd normally check the docs for this exact signature — may I?" — real engineers look things up; pretending you never do reads as junior.

3. Buy time — without going silent

You are allowed to think. You are not allowed to disappear. Use a short spoken bridge that keeps the interviewer with you while your brain works.

Instead of silenceSay this
(long pause, staring)"Let me think about this out loud for a second."
Freezing on where to start"Let me restate the problem to make sure I have it right."
Not sure of the best way"There are two ways I could go here — let me weigh them quickly."
Need to recall a detail"I want to get this detail right, give me a moment to reason it through."

These are not filler. Each one buys 5–15 seconds and signals a calm, structured mind.

Recall — cover the page Say all four bridge phrases out loud now, without looking. You want at least one to come out automatically the instant you feel stuck.

4. When you are genuinely stuck — the recovery ladder

Do not jump straight to "I don't know." Climb these rungs in order. You almost always get unstuck by rung 3.

Rung 1 Restate the problem — half of "stuck" is a misread question. Rung 2 Shrink it — solve the smallest version first ("just one order, no retries"), then add the hard parts back one at a time. Rung 3 Brute force, then improve — say "let me get ANY working version, then optimise." A correct slow answer beats a clever broken one. Rung 4 Ask a scoping question — "Do you want me to handle the failure case now, or get the happy path working first?" Rung 5 Bound the unknown — name exactly what you're missing and how you'd find it (see section 5).
Why "shrink it" is senior Breaking a scary problem into a tiny working core, then growing it, is exactly how good engineers ship real features. Doing it live shows the client how you would actually work on their codebase.
Recall — cover the page Name the five rungs in order from memory. Then pick a task you fear (say, "sync inventory both ways") and say out loud which rung you'd start on and why.

5. "I don't know" — the senior version

Everyone hits a wall sometimes. The junior hides it or bluffs. The senior bounds it and shows a path. Never say a bare "I don't know" and stop.

The trap "Umm… I'm not sure." (silence) — this reads as no ownership. Bluffing a wrong confident answer is worse: a senior interviewer will catch it and now they don't trust you.
Say it like this "I haven't used that exact API, so I won't pretend I have. Here's what I'd expect it to do based on how the rest of the Admin API works, and here's exactly how I'd confirm it — I'd check shopify.dev and test it against a dev store before I relied on it."

That answer is honest, shows real judgment, and tells the client you will not ship on a guess. That is a hire signal, not a miss.

Recall — cover the page Pick a real API you have never used. Say a senior "I don't know" for it out loud: what you'd expect, and exactly how you'd confirm it.

6. Recover from your own mistake

You will write a bug live. Everyone does. What matters is the recovery — it is a free chance to show how you behave when your own code breaks in production.

  • Own it plainly, no long apology. "Ah — I see the bug. My handler isn't idempotent yet; a duplicate webhook would double-apply. Let me fix that." One calm sentence.
  • Explain the fix as you make it — so they see you understand the why, not just patch symptoms.
  • Name how you'd catch it earlier next time — "a test that sends the same event twice would have caught this." That is the senior reflex.
Reframe A calm recovery from a live bug can score higher than clean code with no bug — because the client's real worry is "what happens when this person's code breaks at 2am?" You just showed them.

7. Watch the whole arc — one hard moment, start to finish

The sections above are the parts. Here is what they look like assembled — one candidate hitting a wall on a live task and staying senior the whole way through. Read it out loud; this is the shape you are rehearsing.

TASK: "Write a worker that reads order webhooks and updates the ERP." Candidate: "Let me restate it — a worker consumes order events and [narrate: restate] writes them to the ERP. Two assumptions: events can arrive twice, and the ERP has a REST client." [narrate: assumptions] "Plan: dedupe by event id, then upsert to the ERP. [narrate: plan first] Let me start simple — one order, no retries." [rung 2: shrink it] (writes an in-memory Set to dedupe) "…actually, that Set won't survive a restart or a [rung: caught own bug] second worker. Let me not over-think it live — [buy time] I'll get a working version with a DB unique key, [rung 3: working first] then we can talk scale." [own it, no apology] "One thing I'm not 100% sure of — the exact ERP [senior "I don't know"] upsert call. I'd check their docs; the shape is a find-or-create on our order id. Want me to assume that and keep moving?" [rung 4: scoping question] Interviewer: "Yes, assume it." Candidate: "Great." (keeps narrating, finishes the happy path)

Notice: they were stuck, wrote a bug, and said "I'm not sure" — and still looked completely senior, because every one of those moments was handled out loud with a next step. That is the whole game.

8. The calm signals — voice and pace

Confidence is heard before it is understood. Under stress people speed up and their voice climbs. Do the opposite on purpose.

  • Slow down about 20%. A slight, steady pace reads as "I've got this," even mid-problem. You can only feel this by comparing — see the recording drill in section 9.
  • Pause on purpose. A 2-second silence after a hard question is fine. Rushing to fill it is what looks nervous.
  • End sentences going down, not up. A rising tone turns statements into questions and sounds unsure.
  • Breathe before you answer a "why?". One breath buys the sentence you actually want to say.

9. Drill Part A — do not just read it

Composure is a muscle. Build it with reps, the same way you build recall.

Daily 15-minute pressure drill Open one kata. Set a timer. Code it out loud from a blank file, narrating the plan first. When you stumble, force yourself to say the bridge phrase instead of going silent. Record yourself twice — once at your normal pace, once about 20% slower — and listen back for pace, pauses, and "I don't know" quality. Hearing the slower take is how you learn what calm sounds like.

Then rehearse the four moments cold, saying each answer out loud until it is automatic:

  1. Buy time on a question you don't know yet.
  2. Climb the recovery ladder on a problem that scares you.
  3. Say a senior "I don't know" for an API you've never touched.
  4. Recover out loud from a bug you just wrote.
Carry these four into the room — Bridge · Shrink · Bound · Own Bridge: "Let me think out loud for a second." · Shrink: "Let me get any working version first, then improve it." · Bound: "I won't pretend I've used it — here's how I'd confirm it." · Own: "I see the bug — my handler isn't idempotent yet. Let me fix that."

Part B · In real work — the store is live and the money is real

Part A was performance. Part B is the job. You are a Shopify theme + backend developer, so your hard moments look like this: a deploy breaks the product page, add-to-cart fails only on mobile, the order sync to the ERP stops at 2pm, and the client is typing in Slack while you read a stack trace. Same word — pressure — completely different failure mode.

If you remember one thing (Part B) Stabilise before you understand. On a live store your first move is to make buying work again — usually by undoing something — then debug calmly. Debugging a bleeding store is the most expensive way to be right.

10. Real pressure is a different animal

The two rooms punish opposite instincts. Know which room you are in.

Live-coding round (Part A)Live store at 2pm (Part B)
AudienceOne person, watching your screenA Slack channel, watching the clock
The clock45 minutes, then it's overRuns until you fix it. Every minute is lost orders
Cost of a wrong moveZero — you just try againReal. A bad push can break more than the bug did
Your failure modeFreezing — going silentGuessing fast — changing five things at once on the live theme
The habit that saves youSay a bridge sentence out loudUndo first, then change one thing at a time and write down each one

In the interview, silence kills you. On a live store, guessing kills you. Panic looks different in each room, so the cure is different too.

The trap that gets everyone You edit the live theme directly, change three files, refresh, it's still broken — and now you cannot remember what the store looked like ten minutes ago. You have added a second bug to the first one and lost your way back. This is the single most common way a 5-minute problem becomes a 3-hour one.

11. The first ten minutes — a fixed order

Do not improvise the start of an incident. Run the same five steps every time, in this order. The order is the skill.

Step 0 STOP editing live. Close the code editor on the live theme. Nothing you type on live in a panic will help. Step 1 NAME the blast radius — before any fix: · What exactly cannot the customer do? (see / add to cart / pay) · All products or one? All devices or one? Guest or logged in? · Since when? What changed at that time (a push, an app install, the merchant editing in the theme editor)? Step 2 STABILISE. Undo first, understand later. Republish the previous theme, revert the commit, turn the flag off. Buying works again. The bug is still there — but it is no longer an emergency. Step 3 SAY IT in the channel: what broke, who is affected, what you did, when you will update next. (Section 15 has the wording.) Step 4 NOW diagnose — on a copy, not on live, one change at a time.
Why "undo first" is the senior move A junior debugs on live for an hour while orders fail, because rolling back feels like admitting defeat. A senior undoes in two minutes, then debugs in peace for an hour with nothing at stake. Same bug, same fix — but one of them cost the client an hour of sales. Rollback is a tool, not a confession.
Recall — cover the page Say the five steps in order from memory. Then take a real incident you lived through and say honestly which step you skipped.

12. Know your undo before you need it

You cannot stay calm if you do not know how to get back. Learn the undo for each surface now, while nothing is broken.

What brokeFastest safe undoWhat it will not do
A whole theme deployPublish the previous theme again from the theme library (Online Store → Themes). Instant.Only works if the old theme is still there — so duplicate before you edit.
One Liquid fileCode editor → open the file → Older versions → pick a timestamp → Restore contents.[1]One file at a time. It cannot restore a whole theme, cannot bring back a deleted file, and files in assets/ (CSS, JS, images) have no history at all.
Your own bad commitRevert the commit in git, then push the theme again.Nothing — but see the push trap below before you push to live.
A backend deployRedeploy the previous build, or turn the feature flag off.It does not undo data already written. Writes need their own repair path.
Bad data already syncedThere is no undo. Only a repair job (your reconciliation).This is why data changes get more care than UI changes.
The push trap — read this twice shopify theme push uploads your local files over the remote ones, and by default it deletes remote files that do not exist locally unless you pass --nodelete. It also needs --allow-live to touch the live theme.[1] So a full push can overwrite settings_data.json — the file where the merchant's theme-editor work is saved — with your older local copy. Two sources write that file: the merchant in the editor, and you from git. On a live store, push narrow: --only the files you actually changed.
Recall — cover the page Say the undo for all five rows without looking. If you cannot say one of them, that is your next 10 minutes of work — not reading, doing it once on a dev store.

13. "It works on my machine" — narrowing what you cannot reproduce

Half of daily panic is a bug you cannot make happen. Do not start guessing at code. Cut the search space in half first, with one look at the page source.

The one split that halves the problem Open View source on the broken page. Is the wrong value already in the HTML? Then it is Liquid or data — the server rendered it wrong. Is the HTML correct but the page shows something else? Then it is JS or CSS — the browser changed it after load. One look, and you have thrown away half the codebase.

Then walk the four differences. In Shopify, "works on my machine" is almost always one of these four — name which one before you touch code:

1 THEME Which theme is actually published? You may be looking at your dev copy while the customer sees a different theme. → Preview the live theme, not your local one. 2 DATA Which product, which variant? Sold out, no image, one option vs three, a different market/currency, a discount applied. → Get the exact URL from the person reporting it. 3 APPS A third-party app injects script into the live store that is not in your dev theme. Check the console for errors from a file you did not write. 4 SESSION Logged-in customer vs guest, cart already has items, a saved address, a B2B/company account, a specific device.
Say this instead of "I can't reproduce it" "I can't reproduce it yet — can you send me the exact product URL and whether you were logged in?" That sentence moves the problem forward and sounds like ownership. "It works for me" sounds like the end of a conversation.
Recall — cover the page Name the four differences from memory. Then take a bug you once chased too long and say which of the four it turned out to be.

14. When the sync stops — the backend triage order

"Orders stopped reaching the ERP" feels huge because the pipeline has many parts. It shrinks the moment you ask one question first: inbound or outbound? Did the event fail to arrive, or did our write fail?

Walk it in order and stop at the first NO: Shopify sent the event? → check the order exists and its time We received it? → our access log / handler log HMAC verified? → 401s look like "no events" from outside We answered 200 in time? → 5-second budget (Lesson 2) The job was enqueued? → queue depth: 0 or climbing? A worker is running? → dead worker = full queue, no errors The ERP call succeeded? → rate limit / auth expired / ERP 5xx Failures landed in the DLQ? → that is your list of work to repair

Each arrow is a place you can look, not a guess. That is what keeps you calm: the problem is not "the sync is broken", it is "which of eight links is it" — and you can check all eight in ten minutes.

The one that bites during an incident If your endpoint keeps failing, Shopify retries about 8 times over roughly 4 hours and then removes the subscription (see Lesson 2). So a long outage can end with the store no longer sending you anything — you fix the bug, and it is still silent. During an incident, check the subscription still exists.
Your calm comes from the reconciliation job You do not have to catch every event live. If a repair job can re-read the missed orders afterwards, then a 40-minute outage is a delay, not data loss — and you can say that out loud to the client. Designing that job on a quiet day is what buys you composure on a loud one.

15. Working while the client watches — the status update

This is Part B's version of the bridge phrase. In the room, one sentence buys you ten seconds. In real work, one update buys you thirty minutes of quiet to actually think.

Instead ofSay this
Silence while you digA four-line update (below), then work
"Still looking into it""Buying works again — I rolled back. Now finding the cause. Next update 15:30."
"It should work now""I tested add-to-cart on mobile Safari and desktop Chrome — both work now."
"I don't know how long""I don't know the cause yet. I'll know by 15:30 whether it's a 1-hour or 1-day fix."
The four-line update — memorise the shape What: add-to-cart fails on mobile for variants with no image.
Who: mobile buyers, since about 14:10.
Now: rolled back to the previous theme, so buying works. Bug still in the new build.
Next update: 15:30.

The last line does the most work. "Next update at 15:30" is the sentence that stops the pinging, because the client no longer has to ask to feel safe. And never say "should" — say what you tested. "Should work" is the phrase that costs you trust when it doesn't.

Recall — cover the page Take the last real bug you fixed. Write its four-line update now, out loud: What · Who · Now · Next update.

16. "Can you just push it now?" — pressure to answer instantly

Some pressure is not technical. It is a person asking for a number or a deploy right now. The senior move is never to refuse and never to fold — it is to give the cost back with the decision.

  • Asked for an estimate on the spot? Never one number. Give a bracket, the one unknown that moves it, and when you will confirm: "Between half a day and two days. It depends whether the ERP already has an endpoint for this — I'll confirm by 4pm."
  • "Can you just push it now?" Offer two real options with their price: "I can push in 10 minutes, but nobody has tested checkout with it. Or I test for 20 minutes and push at 3. Which do you want?" You keep the deploy honest, and they own the trade-off.
  • Asked something you don't know, on a call? The same senior "I don't know" from section 5 works word for word — bound it, and say how you will confirm. It reads even better in real work than in an interview.
  • It is 5pm Friday. It is fine to say: "I won't push to live now without a tested rollback. First thing Monday, or I do it now behind a flag." That is not being difficult. That is being the person they can trust with the store.
The shape of all four Never a bare "no", never a rushed "yes". Always: here is the risk, here are the options, you choose. That single move is what makes people stop pressuring you — they learn you will tell them the truth about cost.

17. Close the fire with a guard

An incident is not finished when the store works. It is finished when the same incident cannot happen quietly again. Otherwise you will fight this fire again in six weeks, and the calm you built will not compound.

What went wrongThe guard that closes it
A duplicate webhook double-appliedAn idempotency key, plus a test that sends the same event twice
A push wiped merchant settingsPush narrow with --only, written into the repo's deploy notes so the next person does it too
A theme change broke one breakpointA screenshot check, or one line on the pre-push checklist
The sync died silently for hoursAn alert on queue depth or on "no orders synced in 30 minutes"
Nobody knew how to roll backThe rollback steps in the README, practised once on a dev store
This is also your best interview story "Tell me about a production incident" is answered by the guard, not the fix. Say: what broke, what the customer felt, how you stabilised, the root cause, and the guard you added so it cannot repeat. Interviewers are waiting for that last part. Most candidates stop at the fix.

18. Build the calm before the fire

Here is the honest truth about confidence in daily work: it is mostly preparation, not nerve. People who look calm in an incident are people who already know their undo. Go earn that, on a quiet day:

  • Practise the rollback once, on purpose. On a dev store, break a page, then republish the previous theme and restore one file from Older versions. Doing it once removes most of the fear permanently.
  • Duplicate the live theme before you edit it. Ten seconds. It is the whole reason a rollback exists later.
  • Keep a scratch file open per bug. Two columns: what I changed, what happened. This is Part A's "think out loud" for when nobody is in the room — it stops you changing five things at once.
  • Know where the logs are, before you need them. Can you trace one order from Shopify to the ERP right now, in under five minutes? If not, that is the drill.
  • Never deploy to live with no time left to undo. The fear of pushing is really the fear of not being able to go back.
Do this one for real Pick the one item above you cannot do today. Do it this week on a dev store. That single item is worth more than re-reading this lesson.
Carry these four into the job — Stabilise · Narrow · Update · Guard Stabilise: "Buying works again — I rolled back. Now I'll find the cause." · Narrow: "Is it Liquid or JS? Which theme, data, app, or session?" · Update: "What · Who · Now · Next update at 15:30." · Guard: "Here's the check that stops this repeating."

In a mock, you say a bare "I don't know" (with no path) zero times. 3. You can recite Bridge · Shrink · Bound · Own from memory and use each one live.
Part B — 4. You can say the undo for the theme, for one file, for a commit, and for a backend deploy without looking them up. 5. On your next real bug, you change one thing at a time and have a written log of it. 6. Your first message in an incident is the four-line update with a next-update time — before you start digging.
