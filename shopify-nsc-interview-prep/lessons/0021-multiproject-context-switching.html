Multi-Project Focus & Context Switching

You will run more than one project at once. A senior does not pretend switching is free — the senior manages its cost, protects focus, and is honest about capacity.

Your win: answer "How do you handle several projects at the same time?" — with concrete habits, not "I just multitask well."

Ties to your mission: the PM said one developer can hold multiple projects with context switching. This lesson pairs with L20 (capacity math) and L17 (WIP limits in Kanban).

Boundary — L21 vs L22 L21 = protecting your own focus across projects. L22 = the craft of the message you send out. This lesson is about your head and your time; L22 is about the words you write to the client.

1. The one idea: switching has a real cost

Every time you jump from project A to project B, your brain pays a reload tax — you have to remember where you were, what you decided, and what is left. Do that every 15 minutes and quality falls apart. The senior skill is not "switch faster." It is switch less often, and make each switch cheap.

The browser-tabs picture Think of each project as a heavy browser tab. Keeping ten tabs "open" in your head at once slows everything down and crashes. Better: focus on one tab, save its state, close it, open the next. The saved state is what makes reopening fast. That saved state is a written note (step 3).

2. Batch work into focus blocks

Group work by project into blocks instead of scattering it. For example: mornings on project A, afternoons on project B — or whole days per project when you can. Deep work (system design, hard bugs) needs long unbroken blocks. Shallow work (small fixes, replies) can share a block. You protect the deep work by not letting every notification pull you out of it.

3. Keep a written "state note" per project

This is the highest-value habit and almost nobody does it. Before you leave a project, write three lines:

PROJECT B — state (as of Tue pm)
- Where I am: order-sync webhook works; testing retries
- Next step: add dead-letter queue for failed syncs
- Blocked on: waiting for client's ERP test credentials (asked Mon)

When you come back, you are productive in two minutes instead of thirty. The note also means a blocker on one project does not get forgotten — it is written down, so you can chase it or work around it (the L19 unblock habit).

Recall — cover the pagePick a fake project and write your own 3-line state note out loud (where I am / next step / blocked on) before you scroll.

4. Be honest about capacity

When two clients both say "this is urgent," you do not silently try to do both and let quality drop. You make the trade-off visible and let them choose:

Weak (silent overcommit)Senior (visible trade-off)
"Yes, I'll get both done.""I can fully finish A by Thursday, or split and deliver both by Monday. Which do you prefer?"
Both slip, both clients surprisedClient picks; expectations set; no surprise

Saying "no, but here is what I can do" is a senior signal, not a weakness. It shows you manage your own load so results stay reliable.

The trap: silent overcommitting The most common way good developers lose trust is saying yes to everything and quietly failing to deliver. Taking on all the stress feels helpful, but it ends in late, half-done work and surprised clients. Visible trade-offs protect trust; silent yes destroys it.
Say it like this

"Context switching has a real cost, so I manage it on purpose. I batch work into focus blocks per project instead of jumping around, and before I switch I leave myself a short state note — where I am, the next step, and what's blocked — so I pick up again in two minutes. And I'm honest about capacity: if two things are both 'urgent,' I don't silently absorb both. I surface the trade-off and let the client choose, so nothing slips as a surprise."

Check yourself

Answer from memory. Options shuffle each time, so read them fresh.

Now say it out loud

You've got it when…you can write a 3-line state note from memory and deliver the visible trade-off line ("I can finish A by Thursday, or split and deliver both by Monday — which do you prefer?") without hesitating.
