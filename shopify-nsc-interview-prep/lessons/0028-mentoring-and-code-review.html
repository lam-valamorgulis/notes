Lesson 28 · Behavioral · Closes a JD gap

Mentoring & Code Review

The JD lists "mentor junior developers" and "participate in code reviews" as senior duties. They will ask for a story. This lesson builds yours — a real one, using a frame and a template you adapt.

Your win today: leave with one strong, true mentoring story and one code-review story you can tell in 90 seconds, in the STAR shape, that make you sound like a senior who makes the team better.

Ties to your mission and your STAR worksheet: senior is not just "I write good code." It is "I make the people around me better." The client is hiring a senior — show that.

Read this first — honesty rule: the story below is a template, not your story. Interviewers can tell when an answer is borrowed. Use the shape; fill every blank with something that actually happened to you in your 6 years (Shopify theme teams count — you have mentored). A smaller true story beats a bigger invented one.

1. What a mentoring answer must show

They are not asking "did you help someone." They are checking for three senior signals:

  • You made someone independent — not "I did it for them," but "I taught them so they could do it next time."
  • You were patient and specific — you started from what they already knew, not from where you are.
  • It had an outcome — they grew, the code improved, the team moved faster.

2. The STAR shape for a mentoring story

PartWhat goes hereTemplate (replace with your truth)
SituationWho, when, the context."A junior on my team was new to Shopify. They kept shipping ______ that caused ______."
TaskYour responsibility."As the senior, I owned code quality, and I wanted them to learn, not just to be corrected."
ActionWhat YOU did — the specific mentoring moves."I paired with them on ______, explained the why behind ______, and in code review I left comments that asked questions instead of just giving answers, so they reasoned it out. I gave them a small ______ to own end to end."
ResultThe roughly measurable change."Within ______ weeks they were shipping ______ without that class of bug, and later they mentored the next new hire the same way."

Reflection cue for the Action blank: be ready to say why asking questions worked better than giving answers — a question makes them reason it out and remember it, an answer only fixes this one PR.

"The measure of my mentoring isn't whether I fixed their code — it's whether they stopped needing me to. My goal in every review is to make the next PR better without me in it."

3. Code review — the senior philosophy to state

They may ask "how do you approach code reviews?" This is a philosophy question. Give a crisp, humane answer:

PrincipleSay it like this
Review the code, not the person"I comment on the change, never the coder. 'This function could…' not 'you always…'."
Explain the why"I don't just say 'change this.' I say why — the failure it prevents — so they learn the principle, not just the fix."
Separate must-fix from nice-to-have"I label comments: blocking (a real bug or security issue) vs a suggestion. It keeps small style opinions from blocking a good PR."
Ask, don't command"For juniors I ask questions — 'what happens here if the webhook arrives twice?' — so they find it themselves. It sticks better."
Be fast and kind"I review quickly so I'm not the bottleneck, and I always call out what was done well, not only what to change."
Tie it to this role: your review questions can be domain-sharp — "does this handle a duplicate webhook?", "is this ERP write idempotent?". That shows you review with the exact reliability instinct the job needs.

4. Build yours now (fill these in — out loud)

Answer these about a real moment. Say each answer aloud, then stitch them into the STAR shape above.

  1. Who did you help, and what were they struggling with? (Shopify theme juniors count.)
  2. What did you specifically do — pairing, a review comment that taught, a small task you handed over?
  3. What changed for them afterward?
  4. Now: what did you learn about mentoring from it? (Seniors reflect.)
  5. A time someone disagreed with your review, or a mentee who resisted your feedback: what was the trade-off, and how did you work through it? (Frame it as a trade-off you navigated. Stay generous — never blame the other person.)

5. Say it out loud — recall

6. Quick check

You've got it when… You can tell your mentoring story and your disagreement story from memory, each under 90 seconds, each ending on how the person or the code got better — and state your code-review philosophy (code not person · explain the why · blocking vs suggestion · ask don't command · fast and kind) in one breath.
