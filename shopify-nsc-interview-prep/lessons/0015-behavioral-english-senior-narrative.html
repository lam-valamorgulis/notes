Behavioral, English & the Senior Narrative

The round most engineers under-prepare. A calm, structured story in simple English beats a perfect answer said in a panic.

Your win today: answer any behavioral question with a clear, structured story in calm English — using STAR, prepared in advance, so you never freeze or ramble.

Ties to your mission: this is a remote senior role, so they will test how you communicate, not just what you know. English is your second language — structure is how you stay clear under pressure. Prepare the stories once and reuse them.

The one memory hook for this whole lesson

"A junior talks about the code. A senior talks about the problem they owned." Say every story from that seat.

1. The STAR method

STAR is a four-part shape for any "tell me about a time…" question. It keeps you on track and makes sure you end on impact.

LetterMeansWhat you say
SSituationSet the scene in one or two sentences. Where, when, what product.
TTaskWhat was your job or the problem you owned.
AActionThe steps you took. Say "I", not "we". This is the longest part.
RResultThe outcome, with numbers if you can. This is what they remember.

A worked example (from your real background)

Question: "Tell me about a hard production bug you fixed."

STAR answer — built from your Shopify app integration work

Situation: "In my last role I worked on a Shopify app that synced store orders into a client's ERP system."
Task: "We got reports that some orders appeared twice in the ERP. My job was to find the cause and stop it."
Action: "I checked the logs and found Shopify sometimes sent the same order webhook more than once, and our handler was not idempotent — it created a new ERP record every time. I added an idempotency check on the Shopify webhook id, moved the sync work onto a queue so it could retry safely, and added retry with backoff for ERP timeouts."
Result: "Duplicate orders dropped to zero, support tickets about wrong invoices stopped, and the sync kept working during a big sale spike. I also wrote a test that sends the same webhook twice to make sure it never comes back."

Why this story is strong It shows real Shopify depth (webhooks, idempotency, queues), it uses "I", it ends with a measurable result, and it closes with a test — a quality signal. One good story can answer three different questions.
Grade every story on 4 points Before a story is "ready", check it hits all four: (1) Structure — clear S → T → A → R, not a random pile. (2) "I", not "we" — say what you did. (3) Ends on a number — zero duplicates, 40% faster, half the build time. (4) Under 90 seconds — tight, not rambling. Miss one and it reads as mid-level.

2. The senior signals they listen for

An interviewer is not just checking facts. They are listening for signs you are senior. Weave these into your stories on purpose:

  • Ownership — you drove it, you did not wait to be told. "I noticed… I decided… I took responsibility for…"
  • Mentoring juniors — you make others better, not just yourself. "I paired with a junior… I reviewed their PR and explained…"
  • Trade-off thinking — you weigh options out loud. "We could do A or B; I chose A because…"
  • Measurable impact — numbers, not adjectives. "cut sync errors to zero", "reduced page load by 40%".
  • Cross-team collaboration — you work with QC, PM, and clients, not only code. "I aligned with the PM and the client's ERP team…"
The senior mindset in one line

"A junior talks about the code they wrote. A senior talks about the problem they owned, the trade-offs they weighed, the impact they measured, and the people they brought along."

4. The two "trap" questions

Draft it out loud FIRST, then reveal Before you read either model answer below, say your own full answer out loud to both: (1) "What is your biggest weakness?" and (2) "Why do you want this role?" Only then compare with the models.

"What is your biggest weakness?"

Pick a real weakness, then show you are actively fixing it. Do not fake a strength ("I work too hard"). For you, this is honest and safe:

Model answer

"My background is strong on Shopify and front end, and I am lighter on deep backend testing. I know it, so I have been closing the gap on purpose — learning the test pyramid, writing unit tests for my mapping logic, and mocking the Shopify API instead of calling it live. I am already more confident, and I see testing as part of shipping, not an extra."

"Why do you want this role / why are you leaving?"

Stay positive. Never criticise your current employer. Frame it as moving toward something, not running away.

Model answer

"I have spent six years deep in Shopify, and the part I enjoy most now is the backend integration work — connecting stores to ERP and CRM systems. This role is exactly that, full time, with a senior scope. I want to grow on the server side and own integrations end-to-end, and that is what you build here."

5. English tips for a non-native speaker

Your English does not need to be perfect. It needs to be clear and calm. Structure does most of the work:

  • Speak slowly. Slower speech sounds more senior, not less. Rushing is what makes people sound nervous.
  • Structure out loud. Use "First… then… finally…" so the listener can follow, and so you know where you are.
  • Keep sentences short. One idea per sentence. Short sentences are easier to say and easier to hear.
  • It is OK to pause and think. A three-second silence feels long to you but normal to them. Silence beats filler.
  • Repeat the question to buy time. "So you are asking how I handle a duplicate webhook — yes, so…" This gives your brain a moment and confirms you understood.
Avoid Long, complex sentences; rare idioms; and filler words ("like", "you know", "basically"). If you lose your thread mid-sentence, stop, breathe, and restart the sentence. That is fine.

6. Six strong questions to ask them

At the end they will ask "do you have questions?". Always say yes — good questions signal seniority and real interest. Ask about the work, not the perks:

  • "What kind of ERP and CRM integrations does the team build most often?"
  • "How big is the team, and how is it structured?"
  • "What does the stack look like — Node, NestJS, the database, the queue?"
  • "How does code review work here, and what does a good PR look like?"
  • "Is there an on-call rotation, and how do you handle production incidents?"
  • "What does growth look like for a senior on this team over the next year?"
Why these work They show you already think like a member of the team, and the answers help you judge if the role is right. Asking about code review and on-call is a strong senior signal.

7. A note on salary

Know your target number before the call. When money comes up, let them state a range first — it stops you anchoring too low. If pushed to give a number first, give a small range around your target, not a single figure, and say you are flexible for the right role.

Say it like this

"I am flexible and more interested in the role and the team. Could you share the range you have budgeted for this position? Then I can tell you if we are aligned." — Calm, confident, and it puts the number on them first.
