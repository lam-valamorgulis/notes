Owning Ambiguous Scope

The client hands you a fuzzy job and goes quiet. A senior does not wait — the senior makes it clear. This is the one habit the interview is really testing.

Your win: answer "The scope is unclear and the client is busy — how do you handle it?" — with a clear, repeatable method, out loud.

Ties to your mission: the PM said one developer takes a big scope, the client is busy and slow, yet they still want a good result. That is a trust test, not a coding test. This lesson is the core habit; L20 paces it, L22 is the client side.

Boundary — L19 vs L22 L19 = how you think about scope inside your own head. L22 = exactly what you type to the client. Same problem, two sides: here you build the method; in L22 you write the message.

1. The one idea: you remove the fog

When scope is unclear, there are two moves. The junior move is to wait — ask a question and stop until the client replies. The senior move is to make it clear yourself — write down what you understand, state your assumptions, and keep moving. Lock this in: ambiguity is your job to remove, not the client's job to prevent.

The contract picture Think of a vague request as a draft contract with blank spaces. You do not send it back empty and wait. You fill in the blanks with your best guess, mark them clearly, and ask the client only to initial the page. You did the hard work; they only confirm. That is what "taking ownership" means in one sentence.

2. The loop (memorize this)

Do not try to hold six steps in your head. Learn the SPINE — three moves you must always say. The last two are the "and if blocked" moves; they matter, but they are not the core.

SPINE — the 3 moves you must say, in order: 1. RESTATE + ASSUME Write the scope back (goals · in · out), and turn each open question into a decision: "I'll do X unless you say otherwise" 2. BATCH Collect all questions into ONE short list, each answerable fast 3. SLICE Break the work into a few demo-able milestones And if blocked / a week passes: • UPDATE Send a short weekly written update, even with no reply • UNBLOCK Blocked on one part? Work another part. Log the assumption.

Restate+assume, batch, slice — that is the spine. Update and unblock keep it moving when the client is silent. Every move has one purpose: the project keeps going even when the client says nothing.

Step 1 — Restate the scope in writing

Turn their fuzzy idea into a short written scope note: the goal, what is in, what is out, your assumptions, and a few open questions. This does two things. It shows the client you understood them. And it gives them something they can approve with one word instead of a long reply.

Step 2 — List assumptions, not questions

A busy client hates open questions like "How should the discount work?" — it makes them think and write. They love a decision they can confirm fast. So write it as a default:

Assumptions (I will proceed this way unless you tell me otherwise by Fri):
1. Sync runs every 15 min, not real-time. (Simpler, good enough for stock levels.)
2. If the ERP is down, I retry 3x then queue for the next run.
3. Out of scope: historical orders before Jan 2026.

Now silence becomes permission. You are never stuck waiting, and you have a written record if the result is later questioned.

Step 3 — Batch your questions

Never send one question at a time to a slow client. Collect them, number them, and make each one answerable in seconds — a yes/no or an A/B choice. You respect their time, so you get faster replies.

Steps 4–6 — Slice, update, unblock

Break the work into 3–4 vertical slices you can demo (each is real, working software — see L20). Send a short weekly update — progress, next, risks, decisions I need — even if nobody replies; it builds trust quietly. And when one part is blocked, switch to a part you can do and log the assumption. Waiting is never allowed to stall the whole scope.

The trap: waiting for perfect clarity You will never get perfect clarity from a busy client — and waiting for it looks weak, not careful. The skill is to act on good-enough clarity and make your assumptions visible, so a wrong guess is caught early and cheaply, not on the deadline.
Say it like this

"When scope is unclear, I don't wait for the client to figure it out — I write it up for them. I send a short scope note with my assumptions and the few decisions I need, and I set a default: 'I'll proceed this way unless you say otherwise by Friday.' That keeps the project moving even when the client is busy, and it means the result matches what they actually wanted, because I confirmed the shape early. If I hit a blocker, I switch to another part and log the assumption — I never let one open question stall the whole scope."
