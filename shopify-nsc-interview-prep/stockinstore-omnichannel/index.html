The Map — Omnichannel

One page that connects this whole sub-course into a story: what a stockinstore-like system is, and how you'd build it on Shopify + Node.

Your PM's hint: the client's product is like the stockinstore app — an omnichannel platform that joins an online store to the stock in physical shops. This sub-course teaches that one domain in depth, framed as "you are the backend engineer building it." The story runs in six chapters, from the problem to a full system-design answer. Chapters build on the main course's Shopify and ERP-sync lessons — same reliability patterns, new domain.

The big picture — how it all connects

Think of the whole system as one question: "How do you make an online store and a fleet of physical shops behave like one shop, when you don't control the stock in those shops?" Chapter 1 defines the domain. Chapter 2 gets stock in from many store systems. Chapter 3 answers the shopper's "who near me has this?" Chapter 4 handles buying — Click & Collect and Ship from Store. Chapter 5 makes it reliable and scalable. Chapter 6 turns it into a system-design answer you can whiteboard. Chapter 7 answers the other version of the job — the client bought the platform and needs it wired in. Everything links to its lesson; each chapter shows the "why?" follow-ups the client will push on.

Two tracks, one fork. "Like stockinstore" means the client is either building a platform like it (Chapters 2–6) or integrating the real stockinstore SaaS into their Shopify store (Chapter 7, Lessons 11–20). Ask which on day one; the domain understanding in Chapter 1 serves both. Both tracks are now taught to the same depth — ten lessons each — because the integration job is a real job, not a footnote: the vendor removes the availability engine but not the stock feed, the identifier mapping, the storefront surfaces or the write-back, and those are where integrations actually fail.

Chapter 1 Understand the domain before you draw a box

It starts with the shape of the problem: the omnichannel problem and the stockinstore domain (L1) — the four products (Find in Store, Store Locator, Click & Collect, Ship from Store), the actors (shopper, Shopify, per-store POS/ERP, store staff, courier, and you in the middle), and the one hard truth underneath: store stock is external, uncertain, and often stale. Every later chapter is a response to that truth.

They will push here
  • What's actually hard about this? (Answer: you don't own the stock, and you can't trust the number.)
  • Why is a raw "1 in stock" the most dangerous number in retail?
  • How is this different from a normal single-warehouse e-commerce store?

Chapter 2 Get the stock in — and make it trustworthy

First the input: inventory ingestion & multi-source sync (L2) — pulling stock from many POS/ERP systems via push, pull, and file feeds, normalising messy identifiers into one canonical inventory_levels view, with idempotent, order-guarded upserts and reconciliation. Then the judgement layer: the stock availability engine (L3) — turning a raw, stale count into a safe signal with thresholds, buffers, prediction, and a "call to confirm" fallback, so you never send a customer to an empty shelf.

They will push here
  • Two stores use different product codes for the same item — how do you join them?
  • An old poll lands after a fresh webhook. How do you stop stock going backwards in time?
  • The POS says 3, but the feed is six hours old and the store sells two an hour. What do you show?

Chapter 3 Answer the shopper fast

The read path: Find in Store & Store Locator (L4) answers "which stores near me have SKU X, right now?" — a geospatial "near me" query joined to the pre-computed availability from Chapter 2, served from a cache within a product-page latency budget, behind the "six lines of code" widget. The key move: the heavy availability maths runs on the write side, so the read path is a fast lookup, isolated on a read replica.

Chapter 4 Handle buying and fulfilment

Click & Collect (L5) carries the chosen store to checkout, re-checks and reserves the item atomically so a soft hint becomes a hard guarantee, places it as a Shopify pickup order, sends the store a pick task, and notifies the customer when it's ready. Then Ship from Store (L6) turns every shop into a mini-warehouse: the order-management system scores candidate stores on stock coverage, proximity, and fair load, prefers a single shipment, and reassigns with fulfillmentOrderMove when a store can't deliver.

Chapter 5 Make it reliable and scalable

The event-driven backbone (L7) — webhooks, queues, idempotency, the outbox, and per-resource ordering — glues every part together. The data model & consistency (L8) lays out the schema, the source-of-truth-per-fact rule, the one strongly-consistent reservation, and the finance/stock write-back to the POS/ERP. Then scale, multi-tenancy & failure modes (L9) shows how the three workloads scale on separate axes, how many retailers share one platform, and how every component degrades gracefully.

Chapter 6 Turn it into an interview answer

"Design stockinstore" (L10) is the capstone: a full seven-step whiteboard walkthrough that ties every chapter into one answer, with an 8-sentence summary and a deep follow-up drill. The katas (idempotent upsert, find-in-store query, Click & Collect reservation, allocation scorer) let you type these ideas from a blank file, and the reference cards — design cheat sheet, data-model card, and rapid-fire Q&A — are for fast recall. The study plan lays out the order to move through all of it across 3 days.

Chapter 7 The other job: they already bought it

Chapters 2–6 answer "build the platform." Chapter 7 answers the version of the job that is just as common: the business has bought stockinstore, and you have to wire it into their Shopify store and their tills. Lesson 11 is the map — the responsibility split and the six connection points — and Lessons 12–20 are the territory. The heart of it is that buying removes the engine (availability, reservations, allocation) but not the plumbing: the stock-feed connector (L12), which is a relay rather than an ingest and therefore sends absolute state with a shadow table, and the identifier mapping registry (L13), which is where integrations really fail. Then the surfaces — theme app extensions (L14) with the variant-change bug and a real performance budget, and Click & Collect at checkout (L15), where "does our plan support it?" has three different correct answers. The loop closes with four webhook streams and the write-back (L16), and then the senior work: vendor risk (L17) — scopes, Level 2 protected customer data, and a fail-open/fail-closed policy per surface — running the project (L18), and build vs buy (L19) with the façade that keeps the decision reversible. Lesson 20 is the capstone drill, and Kata 05 makes you type the connector from a blank file. Both tracks are now ten lessons deep, and the whole sub-course is complete.

They will push here
  • "We do the integration" — so what's actually left for you to build?
  • Why send absolute quantities to the vendor instead of the change events the POS gives you?
  • A stocked product shows "no stock" at one shop. What do you check before blaming the vendor?
  • The vendor's API times out. What does the shopper see on the product page, and at checkout?
  • Should we have built this ourselves? What would change your answer?
