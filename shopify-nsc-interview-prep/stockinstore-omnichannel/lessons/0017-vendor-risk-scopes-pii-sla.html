Vendor Risk: Scopes, Protected Customer Data, SLA & Fallback

You have just put a third party in the critical path of every product page and given them your customers' names and addresses. This lesson is the four questions that makes that a decision instead of an accident.

Your win today: review an app's scopes like an engineer, not a checkbox; know Shopify's protected customer data Level 1 and Level 2 requirements well enough to audit a vendor against them; design a fail-open vs fail-closed policy per surface; and write a fallback that keeps the shop selling when the vendor is down.

Expands the two "third-party concerns" from Lesson 11 §5 into the lesson they deserve. Nothing here is optional in a real project, and all of it is asked about in interviews for senior roles.

1. Reading a scope request properly

When the merchant installs the vendor's app, a permission screen appears. Someone clicks Install. That click is a security decision, and it is usually made by whoever happens to be logged in.

ScopeWhy an omnichannel vendor asksYour check
read_productsMatch catalogue items to feed rowsReasonable. Note that write_products is a much bigger ask — question it.
read_inventory / write_inventoryRead Shopify's levels; write per-store quantities if stores are modelled as Shopify locationsWrite is only justified if the design publishes stock into Shopify. If the widget reads from the vendor's own API, ask why write is needed.
read_locationsDiscover the shopsFine. write_locations is not something a stock vendor needs.
read_ordersSee pickup ordersReasonable — but this is protected customer data territory (§2). read_all_orders is a separate, bigger ask that "requires special approval".1
write_merchant_managed_fulfillment_ordersMark pickup ready at the merchant's own store, and fulfilRequired for that flow — see the scope trap in L15 §4. Broad, and correctly so.
write_assigned_fulfillment_ordersOnly if the vendor registers as a fulfillment service with its own dedicated locationDifferent architecture. Ask which one they are — the scope list should follow from that answer.
read_customers / write_customersPickup contact details, notificationsThe most sensitive ask on the page. Demand the specific feature that needs it. write_customers from a stock vendor deserves a hard no by default.
Three questions that make a scope review real (1) "Which feature needs this, and what breaks if we decline it?" A vendor who cannot answer per-scope is asking for convenience, not capability. (2) "Is this read or write, and have we justified every write separately?" Reads leak; writes destroy. (3) "What happens on uninstall?" — the merchant's data should stop flowing, and the vendor's copy should be deleted on a stated schedule. Get that schedule in writing; it is also a L19 exit question.
The trap: re-approval is a merchant action If scopes change later — say, you discover the pickup flow needs write_merchant_managed_fulfillment_orders — the merchant must re-approve the app. That is a person in a business, possibly on leave, possibly nervous about a permission screen. Get the scope list right in discovery and you avoid a mid-project blocker that has nothing to do with code.

2. Protected customer data — the part most people cannot explain

Shopify treats customer information as a controlled resource. "By default, apps don't have access to any protected customer data."1 This is not vague policy language; it has defined levels and named requirements you can audit against.

What counts as protected customer data Data relating to individual customers or prospects: customers (name, addresses, email, phone), orders (orders, draft orders, abandoned checkouts, refunds, transactions), shipping and fulfilment data tied to an individual's order, gift cards used by a specific customer, and webhooks and metafields about individual customers or orders. Data that does not refer to a single customer — a product query — is not protected.1
Level 1Level 2
What it coversCustomer data excluding name, address, phone and emailCustomer data including name, address, phone or email
Fields needing an individual request—Name (first, last), address (line 1, line 2, geolocation, zip — billing and shipping), email, phone
Where Click & Collect sits—Here. Pickup needs a name to hand the parcel to and a phone or email to tell them it's ready.

Level 1 requirements — nine of them, and each one is a question you can ask a vendor:1

  1. Data minimisation — "process only the minimum personal data required to provide app functionality"
  2. Transparency — tell merchants what you process and why
  3. Purpose limitation — "limit your processing of personal data to the stated purposes"
  4. Consent — respect and apply customer consent decisions
  5. Opt-out support — respect a customer opting out of data sharing
  6. Automated-decision opt-out — allow opting out of profiling or scoring with legal effects
  7. Data agreements — "make privacy and data protection agreements with your merchants"
  8. Retention periods — "apply retention periods to make sure that personal data isn't kept for longer than needed"
  9. Encryption — "encrypt data at rest and in transit"

Level 2 adds seven more, and this is the list that separates a serious vendor from a small one:1 encrypt data backups; keep test and production data separate; implement data-loss-prevention controls against unauthorised extraction; minimise staff access to protected data; require strong staff credentials; keep an access log to protected customer data; and have a formal incident-response policy.

How to use this in a vendor conversation — or an interview Don't ask "are you GDPR compliant?" Everyone says yes. Ask three specific things instead: "what is your retention period for pickup customer data, in days?" — a real vendor has a number. "Do you keep an access log to protected customer data, and can we see the policy?" — that is a named Level 2 requirement. "Are test and production customer data separated?" — also named, and the honest answer is sometimes no. Three questions, no legal vocabulary, and you learn more than an hour of assurances.
Who needs approval Public apps require review for both Level 1 and Level 2. Custom apps always have access; for admin-created custom apps, Level 2 varies by plan. Development and testing apps don't need approval after the fields are selected in the Partner Dashboard.1 So a vendor's public App Store app has been reviewed for this — which is a genuine point in favour of using the published app rather than a bespoke custom app someone builds for one client.

3. The three mandatory compliance webhooks

Any app handling this data must implement customers/data_request, customers/redact and shop/redact. Two practical points for you:

  • The vendor's app must implement them — that is their obligation, not yours. Ask to see how a redaction request flows through their system, end to end. "We'd delete it manually" is not an implementation.
  • Your connector may need them too. If your connector or write-back service stores any customer data — a name on a pickup record, an email in a notification log — then a redaction request must reach your database as well. This is the part teams miss: the vendor deletes their copy, yours quietly survives. Decide early that your connector holds no customer PII at all, and this problem disappears. That is a design choice worth making on day one.
The cheapest privacy design is to not hold the data A stock connector does not need a customer's name. A write-back to the POS may need an order reference and an amount, not an email address. Every field of PII you decline to store removes an encryption requirement, a retention policy, a redaction path, an access log and a breach exposure. Reach for that before reaching for a better database.

4. The vendor is now in your critical path

Before the integration, a slow third party could not affect the product page. Now it can. Budget for it explicitly.

// A circuit breaker sized for a storefront, not a batch job.
class VendorBreaker {
  private failures = 0;
  private openedAt: number | null = null;

  constructor(
    private readonly threshold = 5,        // consecutive failures to open
    private readonly coolOffMs = 30_000,   // how long to stay open
    private readonly timeoutMs = 1_500,    // per-request budget: shopper-facing
  ) {}

  async call<T>(fn: (signal: AbortSignal) => Promise<T>): Promise<T | null> {
    if (this.openedAt && Date.now() - this.openedAt < this.coolOffMs) {
      metrics.breakerShortCircuit.inc();
      return null;                          // fail FAST — do not queue behind a dead service
    }
    const ac = new AbortController();
    const t  = setTimeout(() => ac.abort(), this.timeoutMs);
    try {
      const out = await fn(ac.signal);
      this.failures = 0; this.openedAt = null;   // half-open probe succeeded
      return out;
    } catch (err) {
      if (++this.failures >= this.threshold) this.openedAt = Date.now();
      metrics.vendorError.inc({ kind: classify(err) });
      return null;                          // null means "unknown", never "zero stock"
    } finally { clearTimeout(t); }
  }
}
The single most important line in that class return null — and null must never be rendered as "out of stock." "We don't know" and "there are none" are different facts with opposite consequences. A timeout rendered as zero tells thousands of shoppers a stocked product is unavailable, and conversion drops with no error in any log. Type the result as Availability | null so the compiler forces every caller to handle the unknown case.

5. Fail-open or fail-closed — decide per surface

SurfacePolicyWhat the shopper seesWhy
PDP Find-in-Store widgetFail openWidget hides, or "store availability temporarily unavailable". Add-to-cart still works.The core business — selling online — must not depend on a stock-visibility feature.
Store locator pageFail openStore list from your own cached data; availability badges omitted.Addresses and hours are static. Cache them yourself and this page never goes down.
Cached "last known good" availabilityFail open, with an age label"In stock — as of 40 minutes ago"Stale-but-labelled beats blank. Set a maximum age after which you stop showing it entirely.
Choosing a pickup store at checkoutFail closedPickup unavailable right now; shipping still offered.A pickup promise you cannot verify becomes a customer driving to a shop for nothing. Never promise what you cannot check.
Marking an order ready for pickupFail closed, and retryNothing — it is a staff actionNotifying "ready" for an order that was never picked is worse than a delay.
Stock feed to the vendorQueue, never dropNothingA backlog is recoverable; a dropped absolute reading becomes drift (L12).
The rule behind the table Fail open where the vendor adds information; fail closed where the vendor makes a promise. Showing availability is information — losing it costs a little. Accepting a pickup order is a promise — breaking it costs a customer. One sentence, and it decides every row.

6. Kill switch, SLA and the monitoring you actually own

  • A kill switch, tested. One flag that disables the widget everywhere without a deploy and without uninstalling the app. For an app embed block the merchant can deactivate it in the theme editor, which is a real switch you did not have to build — but confirm it works and write down who is allowed to flip it.
  • An SLA with numbers. Uptime percentage, API response-time target, support response time, and what happens when they miss. "Best efforts" is not an SLA. Ask what their status page reports and whether it covers the API your storefront calls, not just their dashboard.
  • Your own monitoring of them. Never rely solely on a vendor's status page — it reflects what they have noticed. Run a synthetic check from outside: one availability query, every minute, timed. That graph is what you bring to a vendor meeting.

7. Failure modes

What breaksHow you handle it
Vendor API downBreaker opens, widget fails open with a neutral message, add-to-cart unaffected, pickup at checkout fails closed. Synthetic check pages you, not a customer tweet.
Vendor slow, not downWorse than down, because retries pile up. A hard per-request timeout is what saves you — 1.5 s and move on.
Timeout rendered as "out of stock"The bug in §4. Type the result as nullable and force callers to handle unknown separately from zero.
Scope change needed mid-projectMerchant re-approval, which is a human dependency. Resolve the scope list in discovery.
Vendor can't state a retention periodA named Level 1 requirement is unmet. Escalate commercially — this is a contract issue, not an engineering one, and it is exactly what you were hired to notice.
Redaction request arrivesThe vendor handles their copy; you confirm yours holds no PII, or you have a documented deletion path. Best answer: your connector never stored any.
Vendor's status page says green, shoppers disagreeYour synthetic check is the evidence. Keep 30 days of it.
Vendor changes pricing or is acquiredNot an engineering failure, but your exposure. The mitigation is architectural and belongs in L19: your own API façade, your own mapping registry, the store code on every order.
Say it like this (vendor risk in 6 sentences)

"Adding a vendor moves risk rather than removing it, so I make four things explicit before launch. Scopes: I ask per scope which feature needs it and what breaks without it, I justify every write separately, and I know the fulfillment-order scope depends on whether the vendor is a registered fulfillment service or working on the merchant's own locations. Protected customer data: Click & Collect is Shopify Level 2, because it needs name, address, phone or email, so on top of Level 1's nine requirements — minimisation, transparency, purpose limitation, consent, opt-out, automated-decision opt-out, data agreements, retention periods, encryption at rest and in transit — the vendor also owes encrypted backups, separated test and production data, data-loss prevention, minimised staff access, strong credentials, an access log to protected customer data, and an incident-response policy; I ask for the retention period in days rather than asking whether they're compliant. Availability: the vendor is now in the critical path of every product page, so I wrap it in a 1.5-second timeout and a circuit breaker that returns unknown, never zero — a timeout rendered as 'out of stock' quietly kills conversion with no error in any log. Failure policy per surface: fail open where the vendor adds information, fail closed where the vendor makes a promise — so the widget degrades and add-to-cart keeps working, but pickup selection at checkout disappears rather than promising a collection nobody can verify. And I keep my own synthetic check and my own hand-sampled availability accuracy, because a vendor's status page reports what they've noticed, and accuracy is the only number that measures the outcome instead of the machinery."
