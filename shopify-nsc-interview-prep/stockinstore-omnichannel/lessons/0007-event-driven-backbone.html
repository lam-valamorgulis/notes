Event-Driven Backbone: Webhooks & Queues

Everything so far — ingestion, availability, orders, allocation — is glued together by events. This lesson is the nervous system: how work moves through the platform without any two parts blocking each other.

Your win today: draw the whole event flow — the sources that emit events, the queues that absorb them, the workers that act, and the internal events that fan out — and explain the four reliability rules (ack fast, idempotency, retries with a dead-letter queue, and per-resource ordering) that keep it correct under load and failure.

This applies your round-1 event-driven-messaging lesson to the omnichannel domain. The general Kafka-vs-RabbitMQ-vs-SQS comparison lives there; here we wire the specific events of this system together.

1. Why event-driven, not request/response

The platform has fast producers (a Shopify order webhook, a burst of POS stock updates) and slow consumers (writing to a database, reserving stock, calling a POS back). If the producer waited for the consumer, a slow ERP or a sale-day spike would stall the storefront. Events decouple them: the producer drops an event and moves on; the consumer works at its own pace.

The one rule that shapes everything Acknowledge fast, process asynchronously. A webhook endpoint verifies the sender, drops the event on a queue, and returns 200 immediately — it never does the slow work (DB write, POS call) inside the request. The queue is the shock absorber between the spiky outside world and your steady workers.

Two kinds of messaging appear, and naming both is a senior signal:

  • Work queues (point-to-point) — one event, one worker does it (reserve this order). Competing consumers pull for throughput, bounded by the number of partitions when you need per-resource ordering (see rule 4).
  • Pub/sub (fan-out) — one event, many independent reactions (an order was allocated → notify the customer and update analytics and invalidate a cache). Each subscriber gets its own copy.

4. The four reliability rules

These are the same rules from ingestion (Lesson 2), now stated as the platform-wide contract. Delivery is at-least-once, so you design for duplicates, failures, and disorder.

  1. Ack fast — but there are two acks. (a) Return 200 to Shopify inside the timeout (~5 s), before processing. (b) Ack the internal queue message only after the handler finishes and its write commits — acking early on the internal bus silently turns at-least-once into lost messages. Verify HMAC (Shopify) or signature (POS) before either. Note HMAC proves the payload is genuine, not fresh — replay protection comes from the dedupe in rule 2, not from HMAC.
  2. Idempotency. Dedupe on a stable event id — X-Shopify-Webhook-Id for Shopify, a source event id for POS — so the same event twice is a no-op (no double reservation, no double notification). Store processed ids in a fast keyed store (a Redis set or a processed_events table) with a TTL longer than the retry window — keep ~24–48 h, because a retried or delayed webhook can arrive long after the first, and a too-short TTL lets a late duplicate re-process.
  3. Retries + dead-letter. A failed handler retries with exponential backoff. A message that keeps failing (a "poison" event) moves to a dead-letter queue (DLQ) after N attempts so it stops blocking the line; an alert + dashboard lets a human inspect and replay it. Because a replayed message arrives late, the version/timestamp guard in rule 4 is what stops a stale replay from overwriting newer state.
  4. Per-resource ordering — and its cost. Global ordering is hard and usually unnecessary. Keep order within a resource — all events for order #1001 in sequence, all stock events for one (location, SKU) in sequence — by partitioning on that key. This trades off against parallelism: to keep a key ordered, only one worker processes that partition at a time, so you scale by adding partitions (Kafka) or message groups (SQS FIFO), not unbounded workers — parallelism is capped by the number of keys. Where order truly doesn't matter, don't pay this cost; instead make writes commutative with a guard: compare X-Shopify-Triggered-At (or a payload updated_at/version) and drop anything older than what you've applied.
The trap juniors fall into Chasing "exactly-once delivery." You can't guarantee it. You accept at-least-once delivery and build idempotent consumers, which gives an exactly-once effect. Say that sentence and you sound senior. The dedupe key is where exactly-once actually lives — not the transport.

5. Fan-out: one event, many reactions

The internal event bus is what keeps features decoupled. When the order/reservation worker finishes, it does not call the notification service and the analytics service and the cache — it emits one order.allocated event, and each of those subscribes independently.

Why fan-out instead of direct calls If the worker called each service directly, adding a new reaction (say, a loyalty-points service) would mean editing the worker, and one slow service would slow the order. With pub/sub, the worker emits once and is done; new consumers subscribe without touching the producer; a slow or failing consumer only delays its own reaction, not the order. This is how availability.changed cleanly invalidates the Lesson-4 cache without the availability engine knowing the cache exists.
The dual-write trap — write and emit can't be atomic A worker that writes a row then publishes an event in two steps has a hidden bug: commit-then-crash loses the event (a reservation with no notification); publish-then-fail-to-commit emits a phantom event. Two systems can't be committed atomically. The fix is the transactional outbox: write the event into an outbox table in the same DB transaction as the business change, and a separate relay publishes it (at-least-once; idempotent consumers absorb re-emits). 6. Scheduled jobs are events too

Not every event comes from outside. Time-based jobs emit work on a schedule, and they are the safety nets under the whole system:

  • Reconciliation (L2) — periodically re-read each source's full stock to repair missed events.
  • Reservation TTL sweep (L5) — release holds that expired so abandoned orders don't lock stock.
  • Adaptive polling (L2) — pull from POS systems that can't push, at a per-source cadence.
  • Stale-feed alerts (L3) — flag a store whose feed has gone silent.

7. Failure modes

What breaksHow you handle it
A consumer (e.g. notifier) is downIts events stay queued and retry; other consumers (reserve, analytics) are unaffected because they're separate subscriptions. Nothing lost.
Duplicate webhookDedupe on the event id → no-op. No double reservation or double "ready" text.
Poison event (bad payload)Retries fail → DLQ after N attempts → alert. The queue keeps moving; a human replays after a fix.
Events out of orderPer-resource partitioning keeps a resource's events in sequence; timestamp/version guards make stale ones no-ops.
Spike (sale day)The queue absorbs the spike (depth grows); scale stateless workers up to a safe limit. Backpressure = capping worker concurrency and rate-limiting calls to the DB and POS so they aren't overwhelmed — you can't backpressure Shopify (it just keeps retrying), so the queue depth is your buffer.
Our ingress endpoint is downShopify retries (~8× over ~4 h). But after ~8 consecutive failures Shopify removes the webhook subscription (for Admin-API-created ones) and emails the app's emergency address — the stream stops, it doesn't just wait. So on recovery you must detect the removal and re-subscribe (or use app-specific subscriptions, which aren't auto-removed), then reconcile the gap. Three safety nets: retries, re-subscribe, reconciliation.
Say it like this (the backbone in 6 sentences)

"The platform is event-driven so fast producers never block slow consumers: a webhook or feed is verified, enqueued, and acknowledged in milliseconds, then processed asynchronously by workers. Events come in three families — inbound Shopify webhooks, inbound POS feeds, and my own internal events — and I use work queues where one worker should act and pub/sub where one event fans out to many reactions. Because delivery is at-least-once, I ack fast, dedupe on a stable event id for idempotency, retry with backoff into a dead-letter queue, and keep per-resource ordering by partitioning on the order or the (location, SKU). Fan-out keeps features decoupled: the order worker emits 'order.allocated' and the notifier, analytics, and cache-invalidator each subscribe without the worker knowing they exist. Scheduled jobs — reconciliation, TTL sweeps, adaptive polling — are events too, and they're the safety nets that repair whatever the live stream misses. If a consumer dies its events just wait and retry, a spike is absorbed by the queue and more stateless workers, and a poison message lands in the DLQ instead of blocking the line."
