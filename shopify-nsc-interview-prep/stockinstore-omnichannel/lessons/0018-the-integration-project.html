Lesson 18 · Track B · Running the project, not just the code

The Integration Project: Discovery to Rollout

Integration projects rarely fail on code. They fail on a POS credential that took five weeks, a mapping nobody owned, and forty shops that were never told the feature existed. This lesson is how you stop that.

Your win today: run a Track B project end to end — the discovery questions that expose the real scope, the data audit you do before estimating anything, the eight phases with an exit test each, a staged rollout that can be reversed, and acceptance criteria that are numbers. Plus the honest answer to "how long will this take?"

The closest relative in the course is the POS migration lesson — POS sub-course L06 — which teaches the same discipline for replacing a till. Same shape: the technical work is the small part.

1. Discovery: twenty questions that decide the project

Ask these before quoting anything. Each one has changed a project I would otherwise have mis-scoped.

GroupQuestionWhy it changes the plan
CommercialWhich vendor products are we buying — Find in Store, Click & Collect, Ship from Store?Each adds a whole surface. C&C alone roughly doubles the work of Find in Store.
What Shopify plan is the store on, today and after this project?Decides the checkout options, and whether the vendor's C&C product is even available (L15 §2).
Is there a signed contract with an SLA and a data-processing agreement?No DPA means you cannot legally send pickup PII. That is a launch blocker, not paperwork.
Who at the retailer owns product data? Name a person.Unmapped SKUs can only be fixed by them. No name means the mapping stalls at 96%.
Data & systemsWhich POS/ERP systems, and how many of each?A franchise with three POS vendors means three adapters, three sets of credentials, three quirks.
Can it push, or must we poll, or is it a file drop?Sets freshness and therefore what you can promise a shopper.
Does it report absolute counts or movements?Movements mean you hold state and convert to absolute (L12 §4). Materially more work.
Do stores report eaches or cases? Any weight-based items?A missing UoM factor is a silent 6× or ⅙ error.
Who can grant POS API credentials, and how long does it take?The most common hidden delay in the whole project. Ask on day one; often 2–6 weeks and outside everyone's control.
IdentifiersIs there one product code across all stores?If not, mapping is the project, not a task in it.
Are barcodes populated and consistent?Barcodes are your best join key. Their absence removes a whole rung of the ladder.
Are Shopify SKUs unique and populated?They are not guaranteed to be either (L13 §2). Count duplicates and blanks before promising a join.
How does a store code map to a Shopify location?Shopify's Location has no store-code field. Decide registry or metafield now.
Stores & opsHow many stores, and are any franchised?Franchises can refuse. A franchisee who won't pick orders is an ops problem no engine solves.
Who picks a pickup order, on what device, and how are they trained?If nobody owns this, orders sit unpicked and the feature dies quietly in week three.
What are the trading hours and the busiest trading period?You will not cut over the week before Christmas. Get the retail calendar early.
Is there a store-level contact for when stock is wrong?Availability accuracy is fixed in shops, not in code.
EnvironmentsIs there a Shopify development store and a vendor sandbox?No sandbox means testing against production POS data. Refuse that in writing.
Every storefront domain — live, myshopify.com, markets, staging?Each needs CORS and key allow-listing (L14 §6). Enumerate now, not at launch.
Who else has apps subscribed to orders/create?Two apps acting on one order is the duplicate-write bug from L16 §1.
The two answers that most change the estimate "How long for POS API credentials?" and "Is there one product code across all stores?" A five-week credential wait and an unmapped catalogue are the two things that turn a six-week project into four months, and neither is engineering. Ask both in the first meeting and put the answers in writing.

2. The data audit — before you estimate, not after

You cannot estimate a mapping you have not measured. Get one sample export per POS system and one product export from Shopify, then run four counts. This takes a day and is the highest-value day in the project.

-- Run against a STAGING copy of the two exports. Four numbers, one day.

-- 1. How much of the catalogue can even be matched? (the mapping estimate)
SELECT
  COUNT(*)                                                        AS shopify_variants,
  COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE sku IS NULL OR btrim(sku) = '')          AS blank_sku,
  COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE barcode IS NULL OR btrim(barcode) = '')  AS blank_barcode,
  COUNT(*) - COUNT(DISTINCT upper(btrim(sku)))                    AS duplicate_sku_rows
FROM shopify_variants_export;

-- 2. What does a dry-run match actually achieve, weighted by stock?
WITH m AS (
  SELECT f.*, v.id AS variant_id
  FROM pos_export f
  LEFT JOIN shopify_variants_export v
         ON upper(btrim(v.sku)) = upper(btrim(f.item_code))
         OR lpad(regexp_replace(v.barcode,'\D','','g'), 13, '0')
          = lpad(regexp_replace(f.barcode ,'\D','','g'), 13, '0')
)
SELECT ROUND(100.0*COUNT(variant_id)/COUNT(*),2)                        AS pct_rows_matched,
       ROUND(100.0*SUM(CASE WHEN variant_id IS NULL THEN qty ELSE 0 END)
                  /NULLIF(SUM(qty),0),2)                                AS pct_units_unmatched
FROM m;

-- 3. How stale is the source, really?
SELECT MIN(updated_at), MAX(updated_at),
       percentile_cont(0.95) WITHIN GROUP (ORDER BY now() - updated_at) AS p95_age
FROM pos_export;

-- 4. How big is the daily change? (sizes every rate-limit sum in L12 §7)
SELECT COUNT(*) AS rows_total,
       COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE updated_at > now() - interval '1 day') AS rows_changed_today
FROM pos_export;
What the four numbers buy you (1) Blank and duplicate SKUs tell you whether "join on SKU" is even a plan. (2) The dry-run match percentage, weighted by units, is your mapping estimate — 80% means weeks of data work with the merchandising team; 99% means days. (3) p95 feed age tells you what freshness you can honestly promise. (4) Rows changed per day sizes every batch and rate-limit calculation. Four numbers, and now your estimate is a claim rather than a hope.
The estimate trap "It's a vendor app, so it's a two-week install." That sentence is why integration projects overrun. The install is two days. The connector, the mapping, the store rollout and the ops training are the project. Run the audit and let the numbers argue for you — a 78% dry-run match is an unarguable fact, and it moves the conversation from optimism to planning.

3. Eight phases, each with an exit test

#PhaseYou are done when…
0Discovery & data auditThe four numbers exist, the scope questions are answered in writing, a DPA is signed, and the credential lead time is known.
1Environments & accessDev store created, app installed, vendor sandbox reachable, POS test credentials working. Nothing points at production POS.
2Connector against sandboxOne store's stock flows source → connector → vendor sandbox, idempotently, with the shadow table populated and the dashboard showing the five metrics.
3Mapping to target≥ 99.5% of units mapped, the unmapped list has a named owner, and the auto-mapper runs on a schedule for new products.
4Pilot store, read onlyOne real shop's stock is live in the vendor. A human compares 30 real SKUs against the shelf: ≥ 98% match. Nothing customer-facing yet.
5Storefront surfaceWidget live on a limited product set, performance measured with the embed on and off, variant-change verified on the actual theme.
6Click & Collect end to endA real order is placed, picked, marked ready (customer notified once, not twice), collected, fulfilled, and written back to the POS exactly once. Do this at the pilot store, in person if you can.
7Staged rolloutStore cohorts enabled on a schedule, each with a watch window, and a documented reverse step used at least once in a rehearsal.
8HandoverRunbook written, alerts routed to a team that exists, reconciliation jobs scheduled, and the retailer knows how to turn it off without calling you.
Phase 4 is the phase people skip, and it is the one that saves the project Getting stock into the vendor without showing it to anyone feels like a wasted week. It is the only chance to discover that the feed is fine but wrong — a UoM factor, a store mapped to the wrong shop, a POS that reports warehouse stock as store stock. Finding that with a clipboard in one shop costs an afternoon. Finding it after launch costs the retailer's trust in the feature, which you do not get back.

4. Parallel run and the baseline count

Before a store goes customer-facing, run the new path beside reality and compare. Two techniques, both cheap:

  • Baseline count. Pick 30–50 SKUs at the pilot store, ideally a mix of fast movers, slow movers and case-packed items. Count them by hand. Compare against what the vendor shows. Record the differences with a cause for each. This one exercise finds UoM errors, location mismatches and stale-feed problems in a single afternoon — none of which any dashboard would have shown you.
  • Shadow period. Keep the feed running for a week with nothing customer-facing. Watch feed age, drift count and 4xx rate settle. A connector that looks perfect on day one often reveals a nightly job, a weekly stocktake, or a Sunday closure in week two.
ROLLOUT SHAPE — cohorts, not a switch wk 1 ▓ 1 store pilot: baseline count, staff standing next to you wk 2 ▓ 1 store shadow period — feed only, nothing customer-facing wk 3 ▓▓ 3 stores first customer-facing cohort; one region, one POS type wk 4 ▓▓▓▓ 8 stores add a second POS type — the first real heterogeneity test wk 6 ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓ 20 widen; watch accuracy sampling, not just uptime wk 8 ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓ all full network, then the handover phase ▲ every step has: a watch window, a named owner, and a reverse step ▲ never widen during the retailer's peak trading week
Why cohorts are grouped by POS type, not by size Your risk is not "more stores", it is "a system you have not seen before". Ten stores on a POS you already handle teach you almost nothing new. One store on a second POS type teaches you everything that is going to go wrong with the other thirty on it. Order the cohorts by variety, not volume.

5. Acceptance criteria that are numbers

"The integration works" cannot be signed off. These can, and each maps to something you built.

Notice who owns the last two Pickup-ready SLA and unpicked-order age are retail operations metrics, not engineering ones. Putting them on the same acceptance list is deliberate: it makes visible, before launch, that this feature needs shops to do something. Projects that only list engineering criteria launch perfectly and then die in the stores, and the postmortem blames the software.

6. The runbook (one page, or nobody reads it)

  • How to turn it off. The kill switch, who may flip it, and what the shopper sees afterwards. First item, deliberately.
  • The five dashboards and what "normal" looks like on each, with a number.
  • Alert → first action. One line each: feed age high → check that source's connector and credentials; 4xx spike → check the mapping and the last deploy; breaker open → check the vendor's status and your synthetic check; outbox age high → check POS reachability.
  • How to replay. The exact command to re-send one store, one SKU, or one day.
  • Who to call — vendor support channel and SLA, POS vendor contact, retailer store-ops contact. With names.
  • What is deliberately not automated, and why. Someone will otherwise "fix" it in six months without knowing the reason.

Say it like this (running the project in 6 sentences)

"I treat a Track B integration as a data project with a software component, because that's where it fails. Discovery first: which vendor products, which plan, how many POS systems and of what kind, whether the source reports absolute counts or movements, who owns product data by name, and — the answer that most often blows the estimate — how long POS API credentials take, which is routinely weeks and outside everyone's control. Then a one-day data audit before I estimate anything: blank and duplicate SKU counts, a dry-run match percentage weighted by units, p95 feed age, and rows changed per day — four numbers that turn my estimate into a claim instead of a hope. I run eight phases, each with an exit test, and the one people want to skip is the read-only pilot: one shop's stock live in the vendor with nothing customer-facing, then a human counting thirty SKUs against the shelf — that's how you catch a unit-of-measure error or a store mapped to the wrong shop for the cost of an afternoon. Rollout is cohorts ordered by POS variety, not store count, each with a watch window, a named owner and a reverse step I've rehearsed, and never widening during peak trading. And acceptance is numbers, including two that belong to retail operations — pickup-ready SLA and unpicked-order age — because a project with only engineering criteria launches perfectly and then dies in the shops."
