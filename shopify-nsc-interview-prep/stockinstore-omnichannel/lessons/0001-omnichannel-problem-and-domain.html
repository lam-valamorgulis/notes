The Omnichannel Problem & the stockinstore Domain

The client's product is like stockinstore. Before you design or code anything, you must be able to explain — in one clear paragraph — what this kind of system does and why it is hard.

Your win today: explain the whole domain out loud — the four products (Find in Store, Store Locator, Click & Collect, Ship from Store), the actors, and the one hard problem underneath them all: knowing what stock really sits in each physical store, in near real time, and acting on it.

Ties to your mission: the PM said the client's system "is like the stockinstore app." This lesson turns that hint into a domain you can speak to with confidence, so every later lesson (sync, availability, orders, allocation) has a home.

Clarify this on day one — it changes your whole answer "Like stockinstore" splits into two very different jobs. (A) Building a stockinstore-like platform in-house — most likely, since they're hiring a senior backend engineer — where you design the ingestion, availability, orders, and allocation yourself (Lessons 2–10). (B) Integrating the real stockinstore third-party SaaS into a Shopify store — where the SaaS does the heavy lifting and you own the data plumbing, mapping, theme placement, and write-back (see Lesson 11). A great early clarifying question: "Are we building this capability in-house, or integrating a platform like stockinstore?" Asking it is itself a senior signal — and the answer tells you which lessons to lean on.

1. What problem does this solve?

A retailer sells online and in physical shops. The two worlds usually do not talk to each other. The website does not know what is on the shelf in the Sydney store; the Sydney store does not know a customer just bought the last one online. Omnichannel means joining those worlds so the customer sees one shop, not two. (You will also hear unified commerce — a stricter version of the same idea, where all channels read from one shared, real-time data layer rather than separate systems synced in batches. For this course, treat them as the same goal.)

stockinstore is an Australian SaaS product that does exactly this. It sits between the online store (Shopify, BigCommerce, Salesforce Commerce Cloud, etc.) and the stock that lives in physical stores, warehouses, and franchise locations. It answers questions the plain e-commerce platform cannot:

  • "Is this in a store near me?" — show real, per-store stock on the product page.
  • "Can I buy online and pick it up today?" — Click & Collect (also called BOPIS: Buy Online, Pick up In Store).
  • "Which store should ship this order?" — route an online order to the best physical store to fulfil it.
The one-sentence definition (memorise this) An omnichannel inventory-and-fulfilment platform collects stock levels from many physical locations, presents them to online shoppers in near real time, and routes pickup and delivery orders to the right store — so the whole retail network behaves like a single shop.

2. The four products (the surface)

These are the four things a customer or store manager actually sees. Each one is a thin front end over the same back-end data. Learn all four names — the interviewer will use them.

ProductWhat the shopper seesWhat the back end must do
Find in StoreOn the product page: "In stock at 3 stores near you." Enter a postcode, see which shops have it.Answer "who has SKU X near location Y, right now?" fast, from a live per-store stock view. (SKU = Stock Keeping Unit — a product's unique stock code.)
Store LocatorA map/list of stores with address, hours, phone, and stock status.Serve store data + a geospatial "nearest stores" query.
Click & Collect (BOPIS)"Buy online, pick up at the Bondi store." Re-checks the item is really there before checkout finishes.Reserve stock at the chosen store, create the order, tell the store to pick it, notify the customer when ready.
Ship from StoreNothing extra — a normal online order. But it ships from a shop, not a warehouse.Decide which store fulfils, hand the parcel to a courier, keep stock and finance records correct.

There is also a fifth capability the platform earns as a by-product: reporting/analytics. Because it sees stock, searches, and orders across every store, it can tell the retailer which stores to restock, which products drive foot traffic, and where demand is going unmet. It is not a fifth "product" you build first, but it is why retailers keep paying.

Why the front end is the easy part stockinstore markets Find in Store as "just six lines of code" to add to a site. That is true — the widget is small. The value, and all the engineering difficulty, is in the back end that feeds it: getting accurate stock out of dozens of store systems and keeping it fresh. That is the part this course is about, and the part the client is hiring a backend engineer for.

3. The actors (who and what is in the system)

Draw these on the whiteboard when they ask you to design it. Naming the actors first is a senior habit.

THE OMNICHANNEL LANDSCAPE ┌─────────────┐ ┌──────────────────────────┐ ┌─────────────┐ │ Shopper │ │ OMNICHANNEL PLATFORM │ │ Store staff │ │ (online) │◄──────►│ (the system you build) │◄──────►│ (pick/pack) │ └─────────────┘ find │ │ pick └─────────────┘ /buy │ • inventory sync │ tasks ┌─────────────┐ │ • availability engine │ ┌─────────────┐ │ Shopify │◄──────►│ • order allocation (OMS) │◄──────►│ POS / ERP │ │ storefront │ widget │ • notifications │ feeds │ (per store)│ └─────────────┘ + API │ │ stock └─────────────┘ └────────────┬──────────────┘ │ ship-from-store ▼ ┌───────────────┐ │ Courier / last│ │ -mile carrier │ └───────────────┘
  • Shopper — browses online, wants to know local stock, may pick up or get it shipped.
  • Shopify storefront — the e-commerce platform. It owns the catalogue, the cart, and checkout. Your platform plugs into it (a widget + APIs + webhooks).
  • POS / ERP (per store) — the Point of Sale (POS: the till/checkout system in the shop) and the Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP: the back-office system for stock, orders, and finance). Together they are the source of truth for what is physically in each shop. In a franchise there may be many different POS/ERP systems, one per store group. This is the hard input.
  • Store staff — receive pick lists for Click & Collect and Ship-from-Store orders; mark orders ready or rejected.
  • Courier / last-mile carrier — collects Ship-from-Store parcels.
  • The platform (you) — the middle. It ingests stock, computes availability, answers shopper queries, allocates orders, and notifies everyone.
A concrete example to hold in your head Picture a shoe franchise: 40 stores run by 12 franchisees, on 3 different POS systems plus one head-office ERP, selling through one Shopify store. A shopper in Bondi opens a sneaker page and wants to know which of the 5 nearby shops has size 42 in stock, then reserve it for pickup in an hour. Your platform has to pull stock from all 3 POS systems and the ERP, normalise it, decide what is safe to show, answer the "near me" query in well under a second, and reserve the pair so no one else takes it. Every lesson in this course is one piece of making that single scene work.

4. The one hard problem underneath everything

Every feature depends on a single hard truth: you do not directly control the stock, and you can never fully trust the number you are given. Physical stock changes without telling you — a walk-in customer buys the last one, a box is dropped, a POS is offline for an hour. So the platform is really an exercise in managing uncertainty about a number that lives in someone else's system.

The trap juniors fall into Treating store stock like a database column you own — "just read the quantity and show it." You do not own it. It comes from an external POS/ERP, it may be minutes or hours stale, and showing "1 in stock" when there is really 0 sends a customer to an empty shelf. Senior answers always name this uncertainty and design around it (thresholds, buffers, "call to confirm", reservations).

This single problem branches into the four engineering themes that make up the rest of this course:

ThemeThe question it answersLesson
Ingestion / syncHow do I get stock out of many different POS/ERP systems, reliably, without losing or double-counting?L2

5. Why this maps onto the Shopify backend role

You already studied "Shopify ↔ ERP sync at scale" as an abstract design. stockinstore is that problem, made concrete. Everything you learned transfers directly:

  • Webhooks — Shopify tells you when an order is created so you can allocate and reserve stock.
  • The two APIs + rate limits — you read products/locations and write inventory levels through the Admin GraphQL API, respecting the calculated-cost budget.
  • Queues + idempotent workers — stock feeds from many stores arrive constantly; you buffer and apply them safely, exactly like ERP sync.
  • Reconciliation — because feeds drift and events get missed, you re-read and repair, exactly like ERP sync.

The new material is the domain shape: multiple stores instead of one ERP, a stock-availability engine, geospatial "near me" queries, order allocation with fairness, and the reservation dance in Click & Collect. Those are what the next lessons add.

Say it like this (the whole domain in 5 sentences)

"An omnichannel platform like stockinstore joins an online store to the stock sitting in physical shops. It has four customer-facing pieces — Find in Store, Store Locator, Click & Collect, and Ship from Store — but the real engineering is the back end. That back end syncs stock from many different store POS and ERP systems, computes a trustworthy availability number from data it does not fully control, answers 'who near me has this?' quickly, and routes pickup and delivery orders to the right store. The single hard problem underneath it all is that store stock is external, uncertain, and often stale, so the whole system is about managing that uncertainty without overselling. On Shopify that means webhooks, the Admin API with its rate limits, queues with idempotent workers, and reconciliation — the same reliability patterns as an ERP sync, applied to a fleet of stores."
