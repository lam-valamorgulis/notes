Scale, Multi-Tenancy & Failure Modes

One platform serves many retailers, each with many stores, under spiky traffic. This lesson is how it holds up: what scales, how tenants stay isolated, what breaks, and how you see trouble before the client does.

Your win today: explain how the three workloads scale independently, how many retailers share one platform without hurting each other, the consolidated failure table for the whole system, and the handful of metrics that tell you it's healthy — plus how each part degrades gracefully instead of falling over.

This is the "what would break?" lesson. It pulls the failure modes from Lessons 2–8 into one place and adds the scale and multi-tenant view an experienced interviewer expects.

1. Three workloads, three scaling stories

Do not scale "the system" as one blob. It has three workloads with different shapes, and you scale each on its own axis.

WorkloadShapeHow it scales
Reads (Find in Store)Huge, spiky, latency-critical (every product-page view)Cache + edge (L4), read replicas, stateless API behind a load balancer. Add read capacity without touching writes.
Writes (stock ingestion)Steady with bursts (nightly full feeds), throughput-criticalQueue + backpressure (L2/L7) — backpressure means slowing intake when workers fall behind — plus more stateless workers, partitioned per source. Add workers to drain faster.
Orders (reserve/allocate)Lower volume, correctness-critical (must not oversell)Strong consistency on a single row (L8). Contention is usually localised to one (location, SKU) row, so unrelated products never block each other — but a hot SKU at one store is a real hotspot (see the trap below).
Say it like this "Reads, writes, and orders scale independently. Reads scale with cache and replicas, writes scale with more queue workers, and orders stay correct because contention is normally isolated to one (store, SKU) row rather than system-wide. Separating them is why a viral product page never stalls stock sync, and a nightly feed flood never slows checkout."
The one case that breaks "orders scale fine" — the hot row A flash sale on one SKU, or heavy Click & Collect at one flagship store, dumps thousands of concurrent reservations onto a single (location, SKU) row. A single row is still a serialisation point — "not global" does not save you. Handle it: keep the lock hold tiny (the guarded update is one statement), queue/retry contenders on that row with short backoff, and for extreme cases use a decrement-with-check counter or shard the hold into sub-buckets you sum. Name the hotspot — don't claim it away.
Back-of-envelope sizing (say numbers, not "it scales") 50 retailers × ~400 stores × ~20k SKUs ≈ 400M availability rows — big, but each is tiny and the hot lookups are keyed and cached. Reads: product-page traffic at peak is a few thousand to ~10k requests/sec, almost all cache- or edge-served, so the database sees a small fraction. Writes: nightly full feeds ≈ 400 stores × 20k SKUs ≈ 8M upserts per retailer, spread over a low-traffic window — that target upsert rate sets your worker and partition count. Orders: far lower volume, but correctness-critical, so they get the one strongly-consistent path. Stating rough numbers like these is what turns "add workers" into an actual capacity plan.

2. Multi-tenancy — many retailers, one platform

This is SaaS: dozens of retailers, each with their own stores, feeds, thresholds, and traffic. They must not see or hurt each other.

  • Isolation of data: retailer_id on every row and every query (row-level tenancy), enforced centrally so a query can never span tenants. Larger tenants can be moved to their own schema or database (shard by retailer) if they outgrow the shared pool.
  • Isolation of load (noisy neighbour — one tenant hogging shared capacity): per-tenant rate limits and per-tenant queue partitions, so one retailer's 500-store nightly feed or flash sale can't starve everyone else. This is the fairness idea from Lesson 6, applied to tenants instead of stores. (A single huge feed is sub-partitioned by store so it still parallelises, rather than jamming one partition.)
  • Respect Shopify's own limits (outbound): the platform also calls Shopify (Admin GraphQL cost budget, REST leaky bucket), limited per shop. A sale-day surge of fulfillmentOrderMove and inventory writes can exhaust that budget regardless of your internal capacity, so put a per-shop client-side limiter with retry-and-backoff on 429/throttle — so your own retries don't self-inflict a denial of service on the shop's budget.
  • Data residency: for a multi-country retailer, tenant data can be pinned to a region (EU / AU / US) to meet residency and privacy rules — the same per-tenant sharding, keyed by region.
  • Per-tenant config: thresholds and buffers (L3), feed modes (L2), capacity and routing weights (L6) are all per retailer. One codebase, per-tenant settings.
The trap juniors fall into Relying on the application to remember to add WHERE retailer_id = ?. One forgotten filter leaks one retailer's stock or orders to another — a serious breach. Enforce tenancy in one place (a scoped repository, row-level security, or a mandatory query wrapper), never by hoping every query author remembers.

4. Observability — the metrics that matter

You cannot fix what you cannot see. A small set of signals tells you the whole platform's health; each has a threshold that pages someone.

MetricWhat it tells you
Feed age per storeIs any store's stock going stale? (silent POS outage)
Queue depthHow many messages are waiting — can be high yet draining fast.
Queue lagHow old the oldest un-processed message is — the true "are we falling behind?" signal.
DLQ depthDead-letter queue size — poison messages (ones that fail every retry) piling up means something is systematically broken.
Read p95 latency & cache hit ratep95 = 95% of requests are faster than this. Is the product-page path within its budget (L4)?
Reservation success / reject rateRising rejects = availability drifting from reality (oversell hints).
Allocation reassign rateStores rejecting often = bad data or capacity problems (L6).
The one that's easy to miss Availability accuracy — sample real outcomes (did the reserved item actually get picked without a "not here" rejection?) and track the rate. It's the closest thing to "are we telling customers the truth?", and it ties the whole system back to the one hard problem from Lesson 1. But it's a lagging signal — you only know at pickup, hours later — so pair it with leading signals that move first: feed age and rising reservation-reject rate.
Turn metrics into SLOs, not hand-set thresholds Each metric above is an SLI (service-level indicator — a thing you measure). Pick an SLO (the target, e.g. read availability 99.9%, or read p95 < 200 ms). The gap between the SLO and 100% is your error budget, and paging is driven by how fast you're burning that budget (burn-rate alerts), not by an arbitrary threshold someone guessed. This is the language the client's senior engineers use.

5. Graceful degradation — bend, don't break

When a part fails, the platform should lose a feature, not fall over. Name the fallbacks:

  • Availability engine down → serve last-known state, or show "call to confirm" — never a blank or a crash.
  • Cache down → read from the replica — but only graceful if replicas are provisioned for the extra load. A cache outage at peak can overload the replicas, so also shed load and serve slightly-stale last-known data rather than let the database tier collapse.
  • Allocation (routing) service down → the reservation still succeeds at checkout (that's the oversell guard, and it must not be skipped); only the routing decision — which store fulfils — is deferred and replayed on recovery. Replayed allocations carry a per-order idempotency key so a re-drained backlog can't double-allocate. If the reservation itself is down, fall back to "call to confirm", never silent acceptance.
  • Reservation database AZ fails → run primary + synchronous standby across availability zones with automatic failover (RPO≈0, RTO seconds). During the brief failover, checkout degrades to "call to confirm" rather than risk a split-brain double-sell. A strongly-consistent write can't cheaply span regions, so this HA is local to a region.
  • A store's feed down → that store degrades to "call to confirm"; every other store is unaffected.
  • Notifications down → orders still process; "ready" messages catch up when it recovers (L7 fan-out).
The principle to name Isolate failure to the smallest blast radius (how much breaks when one thing fails). Because features are decoupled by events (L7) and tenants are isolated (§2), a failure is contained to one feature or one store or one tenant — not the whole platform. "Bend, don't break" is the sentence; decoupling and isolation are how you earn it.
Say it like this (scale & resilience in 6 sentences)

"The platform has three workloads that scale independently: reads scale with cache, edge, and replicas; writes scale with more queue workers and per-source partitions; orders stay correct because contention is normally isolated to one (store, SKU) row — with a hot SKU at one store handled as a genuine hotspot, not scaled away. It's multi-tenant, so every row and query is scoped by retailer_id — enforced with row-level security, never by hoping a developer remembers — load is isolated with per-tenant rate limits and queue partitions, and outbound calls respect Shopify's per-shop API budget. I keep a consolidated failure table and watch SLIs against SLOs — feed age, queue depth and lag, DLQ depth, read p95, reservation reject rate, allocation reassign rate, and lagging availability accuracy — paging on error-budget burn, not guessed thresholds. And every component degrades gracefully: the reservation (the oversell guard) stays strongly consistent on a primary with cross-AZ failover, while availability, cache, allocation-routing, and notifications each fail to a safe fallback, so a failure loses one feature, not the platform."
