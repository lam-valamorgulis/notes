Integrating Shopify with the stockinstore SaaS

The other job: the client doesn't build the platform — they buy stockinstore and need you to wire it into their Shopify store and their POS/ERP. The heavy lifting is the vendor's; the plumbing is yours.

Your win today: draw the responsibility split between the stockinstore SaaS, Shopify, and the retailer's systems — and name the integration surface you own: the stock feed, the identifier mapping, the storefront widget, the checkout step, the webhooks, and the write-back. 1. The mental model: you own the plumbing, not the platform

stockinstore markets "we do the integration, not your developers." That is true for the omnichannel logic — availability, reservations, allocation all live inside their SaaS. But a SaaS is only as good as the data you feed it and the surfaces you connect it to. Those connections are the retailer-side engineering — your job.

The one-sentence framing stockinstore sits between the retailer's Shopify store and their POS/ERP; you own the three connections into it — stock data in, storefront + checkout surfaces, and orders/fulfilment out — plus the mapping that makes them line up.

3. The integration surface (the six connection points)

  1. Install & approve the app. You install the vendor's app and approve the OAuth scopes it requests — you don't build the OAuth login flow or store the access token; the vendor's app does. Review the scopes for least privilege (only the permissions it truly needs): e.g. read_products, read_inventory, read_locations. For pickup fulfilment there are three scope families — write_assigned_fulfillment_orders (the app acts as a registered fulfillment service with its own dedicated location), write_merchant_managed_fulfillment_orders (the merchant's own locations), and write_third_party_fulfillment_orders. "Pick the narrower one" is the wrong rule here: the correct scope is decided by who owns the location, and pickup at the retailer's own shop is merchant-managed — fulfillmentOrderLineItemsPreparedForPickup documents write_merchant_managed_fulfillment_orders as its required scope. See L15 §4.
  2. Stock feed in. The real work: get stock out of the retailer's POS/ERP and into stockinstore, using its supported method (push webhook, polled API, or scheduled file). This is Lesson 2 pointed at the vendor instead of your own DB — same concerns: idempotency (the same message twice changes nothing), freshness, and full-vs-delta (send everything, or only what changed). Respect rate limits on both sides — Shopify's Admin API is cost/bucket-limited and stockinstore's API has its own caps — so batch and back off with jitter.
  3. Mapping. Ensure a product's SKU/barcode and a store's location code line up across Shopify, the POS/ERP, and stockinstore. If they don't, the widget shows "no stock" for items that are on the shelf. Own this mapping table and a report for unmapped items.
  4. Storefront widget. Place the Find-in-Store button on the product page with an app block (a theme app extension dropped into the PDP section via the theme editor) — no theme-code edits, survives theme upgrades, cleaned up on uninstall. An app embed block is the other kind: global/floating, enabled store-wide (good for a script or a floating locator), not for a per-section button. Vintage themes may still need the legacy ScriptTag ("6 lines of code") snippet.
  5. Click & Collect at checkout. C&C rides on Shopify's native local pickup — enable it at each participating location and it works on all plans; the customer picks "Pick up" as a delivery method and store selection normally happens pre-checkout via the widget. Checkout UI extensions can add pickup UI at checkout, but customising the information/shipping/payment steps is Shopify Plus-only — so on non-Plus, C&C still works through native local pickup. Note the second, commercial gate: stockinstore's own Click & Collect app targets Shopify Plus, while their Find in Store app runs on Basic, Advanced or Plus. Platform and vendor gates are separate questions — L15 §2 has the full matrix.
  6. Webhooks & write-back out. Consume stockinstore's webhook notifications (order allocated, pickup ready) and Shopify's fulfillment-order webhooks, and write the resulting stock movement and sale back to the POS/ERP so stock and finance stay correct (Lesson 8 §5).

5. The real work is the data plumbing

Notice what you actually build: almost all of it is the stock feed and the mapping — which are Lessons 2 and 8 again, just aimed at a vendor. The vendor removes the availability/reservation/allocation work, but it cannot remove the truth that garbage stock in = wrong answers out. So the integration succeeds or fails on feed quality, freshness, and correct identifiers — exactly the hard part of the "build it" track, minus the parts the SaaS solved.

The trap juniors fall into Assuming "we do the integration" means there's no engineering. The vendor integrates their logic; nobody but you can get stock out of the retailer's specific POS/ERP and map their identifiers. Underestimating the feed-and-mapping work is the classic integration-project failure.
Two things a senior always raises about a third-party vendor Data privacy: Click & Collect pushes customer PII (name, contact, order, pickup details) across a trust boundary to an outside company — so confirm a data-processing agreement, send only the minimum data pickup needs, and note the vendor's app must implement Shopify's mandatory privacy webhooks (customers/redact, customers/data_request, shop/redact). Vendor dependency: your storefront now depends on the vendor's uptime — so you own their SLA, a graceful fallback when they're down, and awareness of the lock-in.

6. Testing & rollout

  • Dev / staging store + sandbox credentials: install on a Shopify development store, and point the vendor and POS connectors at their sandbox/test credentials — never point staging at production POS data.
  • Verify accuracy: pick real SKUs at real stores and confirm the widget's stock matches the shelf — the same "availability accuracy" signal from Lesson 9.
  • Staged rollout: enable a few stores/products first, watch, then expand — don't flip the whole network at once.
  • Reconcile: confirm the write-back keeps the POS/ERP stock and finance correct before going wide.
  • Monitor the whole path: feed age, webhook delivery success, mapping-coverage %, and widget error rate — with an alert on each, so a broken connector surfaces before customers do.

7. Failure modes (integration POV)

What breaksHow you handle it
Stock feed to stockinstore stopsMonitor feed age on your side; alert; the SaaS shows stale/"call to confirm". Fix the connector, reconcile.
Identifier mismatchWidget shows "no stock" for stocked items. Fix the mapping; keep an unmapped-items report.
Widget not appearingTheme app extension not enabled/placed, or a vintage theme needing the legacy snippet. Check the theme editor.
Webhook not receivedTwo sources, two auth schemes: Shopify webhooks verify with Shopify's HMAC header; stockinstore's webhooks verify with the vendor's own signature/secret — check the right one. Shopify deletes an Admin-API subscription after 8 consecutive failures over ~4 h (warning email first), so detect the removal and re-subscribe (L7).
stockinstore API / SaaS is downThe widget must fail gracefully — fast timeout, hide or show "availability temporarily unavailable", and never block add-to-cart. You depend on the vendor's SLA (a lock-in risk to raise), so monitor their status and alert.
Widget slows the product pageIt's third-party JS: load it async/deferred so it never blocks render, reserve its space to avoid layout shift (CLS), and confirm the vendor's API/CDN latency doesn't drag LCP. A slow vendor script degrades every PDP.
POS/ERP double-counts a saleIdempotent write-back keyed on order/allocation id; feeds replace, don't add (L8).
Checkout pickup missing on non-PlusNative local pickup works on all plans; only checkout-step UI extensions are Plus-only. So C&C still works on non-Plus via native local pickup + the app's PDP/cart surfaces — but check the vendor's own plan requirement too (theirs targets Plus).
An order mixes shipping and pickupSince 2026 a single order can hold both SHIPPING and PICK_UP fulfillment orders (Plus & Enterprise). Iterate every fulfillment order; code reading fulfillmentOrders[0] is silently half-wrong (L15 §5).
Say it like this (integrating the SaaS in 6 sentences)

"When the client buys stockinstore rather than building it, the SaaS owns the hard omnichannel logic — availability, reservations, allocation — and my job is the retailer-side plumbing. I own three connections plus the mapping: the stock feed from their POS/ERP into stockinstore, the storefront widget and checkout step in Shopify, and the webhooks that write stock and finance back to the POS/ERP. The stock feed and identifier mapping are the real work — they're the ingestion and data-model lessons pointed at a vendor, with the same concerns: idempotency, freshness, full-vs-delta, and clean SKU/location mapping. I place the widget with a theme app extension so it doesn't edit theme code and survives theme upgrades, and enable Click & Collect with native local pickup plus checkout UI extensions where the plan allows. I install the vendor's app with least-privilege scopes (it owns OAuth and the token, not me), test on a dev store with sandbox credentials, verify real stock against the shelf, and roll out store-by-store. And because it's a third party, I confirm a data-processing agreement and send only the PII pickup needs, and I make the widget fail gracefully if the vendor is down so it never blocks add-to-cart. Most integration bugs live in the feed and the mapping, not in the vendor — 'we do the integration' never means 'there's no engineering.'"
