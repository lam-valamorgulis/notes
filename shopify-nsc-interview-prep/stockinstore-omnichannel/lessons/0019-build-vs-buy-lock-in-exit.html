Build vs Buy, Lock-In & the Exit

The fork this whole sub-course sits on. Buying removes the availability engine — it does not remove the plumbing. Knowing exactly which half you keep is what makes the recommendation credible.

Your win today: answer "should we build this or buy stockinstore?" like an engineer who has costed both. Name the part of the work that exists in both options, give decision rules instead of opinions, and design the one architectural move that keeps the choice reversible — your own API façade in front of the vendor.

Lessons 2–10 are the build-it answer. Lessons 11–18 are the buy-it answer. This lesson is how you choose, and how you avoid having to choose permanently.

1. The insight that reframes the question

Most build-vs-buy discussions compare "build everything" against "buy everything". Neither exists. Go back to the responsibility split from Lesson 11 and count what changes:

BUILD IT BUY IT ──────────────── ──────────────── Stock feed from POS/ERP YOU ████████ YOU ████████ ← unchanged Identifier mapping YOU ██████ YOU ██████ ← unchanged Storefront placement YOU ███ YOU ███ ← unchanged Write-back to POS/ERP YOU ████ YOU ████ ← unchanged ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────── Availability engine YOU ██████ vendor ← removed Reservations YOU █████ vendor ← removed Order allocation / OMS YOU ███████ vendor ← removed Geospatial read path YOU █████ vendor ← removed Multi-tenant scaling YOU ████ vendor ← removed Run + on-call forever YOU ██████ vendor ← removed Buying deletes the ENGINE. It does not touch the PLUMBING. And the plumbing is roughly the top half of the work — call it 40–60% depending on how messy the retailer's POS estate is.
Say this sentence and the conversation changes "Buying the platform removes the availability engine, the reservation logic and the allocation system — but the stock feed, the identifier mapping, the storefront placement and the write-back exist in both options. So the real comparison is not 'build versus buy', it's 'build the engine, or rent it' — and either way we build the plumbing."

The axis that decides it more often than cost Do you have a team that will still be here in three years? An availability engine is not a one-off build — it needs on-call, reconciliation, seasonal tuning, and someone who understands why the buffer is 2. A retailer with two developers and no backend on-call should not own that, at any price. A retailer with a platform team and unusual rules probably should.

3. Decision rules, not opinions

An interviewer is not looking for your preference. They are looking for whether you have criteria.

If this is true…LeanBecause
Standard requirements: show nearby stock, offer pickup, notify when readyBuyYou would be rebuilding a solved product, and the plumbing you keep is where the value is anyway.
No permanent backend team or on-call rotaBuyAn engine you cannot operate is worse than a rented one.

The cost model people get wrong They compare "engineering cost to build" against "annual licence fee" and stop. Three items are missing every time. (1) The plumbing is in both columns — the connector and mapping do not disappear when you buy. (2) Build has a run cost forever — on-call, reconciliation, upgrades, the person who understands it. (3) Buy has an integration project — Lesson 18's eight phases are not free. Put all three in the table and the gap is much smaller than either side claims.

4. The move that keeps the decision reversible

Here is the most valuable thing in this lesson, and it costs almost nothing on day one.

✘ THE DEFAULT (fast, and a one-way door) theme widget ──────────────────────────────────► vendor API POS connector ─────────────────────────────────► vendor API store notifications ◄──────────────────────────── vendor webhooks The vendor's shape is now baked into the theme, the connector, and every consumer. Replacing them means touching all of it. ✔ THE FAÇADE (one thin layer, enormous option value) theme widget ─────► YOUR API ─────► vendor API POS connector ────► YOUR API ─────► vendor API notifications ◄──── YOUR API ◄───── vendor webhooks │ └── your contract, your field names, your cache, your circuit breaker, your metrics, your log Swapping vendors — or building the engine yourself later — changes ONE layer. Nothing else knows the vendor's name.
Five things the façade gives you before you ever change vendor (1) One place for the circuit breaker, timeout and cache instead of the same logic in the theme and the connector. (2) Your own metrics — vendor latency measured from your edge, which is the number you bring to a contract review (L17 §6). (3) A stable contract for the storefront, so a vendor API change doesn't require a theme deploy. (4) No vendor key in the theme — the browser calls you, you call them, and the credential never leaves your server. (5) Cheap A/B — you can serve some traffic from a cache, or from your own engine, without touching the storefront. The exit option is almost a side effect.
When the façade is not worth it A single-brand retailer, one POS, using only the Find-in-Store widget, with no realistic prospect of changing vendor. Then the extra hop adds latency and a service to operate for benefits they will never draw on. Say that out loud too — recommending a façade unconditionally is as unthinking as never recommending one. The tell is whether anyone can name a scenario in which they would change vendor.

5. The exit checklist — write it on day one, not on the way out

  • The mapping registry is yours, in your database, not a spreadsheet at the vendor. This is the single most expensive artefact to rebuild (L13).
  • The connector is yours, with the vendor's API isolated behind one client class. Re-pointing it should be one module, not a rewrite.
  • The store code is on every order as a cart attribute (L14 §7). Historical pickup orders remain interpretable without the vendor. Impossible to back-fill later.
  • Data export is in the contract: what you can get out, in what format, within how many days, and at what cost. Ask before signing; the answer is very different then.
  • Deletion is in the contract: what they delete on termination, and when. This is also a Level 1 retention requirement (L17 §2).
  • The runbook names the reverse step — the kill switch that turns the vendor off without breaking checkout, rehearsed at least once (L18 §6).
Lock-in is not the licence. It is the shape of your data. A vendor cannot trap you with a contract; contracts end. They trap you when the only record of which shop fulfilled last year's orders lives in their system, and the only mapping between the retailer's item codes and Shopify SKUs is a CSV their support team maintains. Both of those are free to prevent on day one and expensive to reconstruct later. That is the whole of lock-in, and it is decided by you, not by them.

6. The hybrid options people forget

  1. Buy the surfaces, own the rules. Use the vendor's widget and pickup flow, but compute the availability state yourself and feed the vendor a pre-judged signal. Works when your rules are the differentiator but you don't want to build a storefront and an OMS.
  2. Buy now, build later, behind the façade. Ship in six weeks, and swap the engine when volume or requirements justify it. This is the correct answer far more often than either pure option, and the façade is what makes it real rather than aspirational.
  3. Buy for the long tail, build for the flagships. Franchised stores use the vendor; a handful of company-owned flagship stores with unusual rules use your own path. The façade routes by store. Operationally more complex, occasionally exactly right.
  4. Buy the engine, own the read path. Cache the vendor's answers in your own edge layer and serve the storefront from your cache, calling the vendor to refresh. You get your latency budget and your fallback, and their logic.

7. How to answer this in an interview

Say it like this (build vs buy in 6 sentences)

"I'd start by rejecting the framing slightly, because 'build everything' versus 'buy everything' aren't the real options. Buying removes the engine — availability, reservations, allocation, the geospatial read path, multi-tenant scaling and the permanent on-call — but the plumbing stays in both columns: the stock feed from the POS, the identifier mapping, the storefront placement and the write-back, which is roughly 40 to 60% of the work depending on how messy the POS estate is. So my criteria are: buy if the requirements are standard, if there's no permanent backend team to operate an engine, or if it's a franchise group with several POS systems where multi-tenancy is already solved; build if the availability rules are the competitive advantage, if per-order fees dominate at their volume, if data residency forbids the trust boundary, or if inventory is already an internal platform with a team. Whichever we pick, I'd insist on one architectural move on day one — our own thin API façade in front of the vendor — because it gives us one place for the timeout, circuit breaker and cache, our own latency metrics for contract reviews, a stable contract for the theme, no vendor key in the storefront, and the ability to swap or in-source the engine by changing one layer. And I'd write the exit checklist while we're signing rather than while we're leaving: the mapping registry lives in our database, the store code goes on every order as a cart attribute so historical orders stay interpretable, and data export and deletion terms are agreed before signature — because lock-in isn't the licence, it's the shape of your data, and that part is entirely our decision. For most mid-market retailers I'd recommend buy now, build later behind the façade, and I'd say plainly what would make me change my mind: order volume crossing the fee curve, or a rule the vendor can't configure."

The two sentences that make the answer senior End with "here's what would change my mind" and "here's what I'd measure to know". A recommendation with no falsifying condition sounds like a preference. A recommendation with a named trigger — order volume crossing the fee curve, or a required rule the vendor cannot configure — sounds like judgement.
