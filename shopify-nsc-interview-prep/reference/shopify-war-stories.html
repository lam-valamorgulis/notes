Your Shopify War Stories

Most candidates have read about Shopify. You have been burned by it. A war story is how you prove that in twenty seconds — and it is the one thing on your CV that cannot be revised for by a generic Node developer the night before.

Why these win the room: a fact says "I studied". A scar says "I shipped". Each story below has a fixed five-line shape, a 20-second soundbite for dropping into a technical answer, and a 90-second version for when they ask you to go deeper. Learn the soundbites cold. The long version can be improvised if the shape is solid.

This is the drill block for Day 5 of the 7-Day Plan. The strategy behind it is L26 · Be the one they pick, section 2. The facts come from L1, L2, L18 and L29 · Part B.

The five-line shape — use it for every story 1. Setup — one line of context, no life story. 2. What went wrong — the symptom the business felt, not the stack trace. 3. The number — one exact fact or figure that proves you were really there. 4. What I did — the fix, in plain words. 5. The guard — the check you left behind so it cannot happen quietly again.
The mistake almost everyone makes They stop at line 4. A story that ends with "and then I fixed it" is a task report. The interviewer is waiting for line 5 — the guard — because that is the line that tells them what you will be like on their project after the first outage. Never tell one of these stories without the guard.

1. The rate limit that was not a request limit

SetupA sync pulling products and orders out of the Admin API. It ran fine in testing on a small dev store.
Went wrongAgainst the real catalogue it started getting throttled. Not because we made too many calls — because our queries were expensive. We were asking for fields we never used.
The numberThe GraphQL Admin API is limited by calculated query cost, not request count: a 1000-point bucket that refills at a plan-scaled rate — 50 points per second on standard plans. Every response tells you your real budget in extensions.cost.throttleStatus.
What I didCut the query down to only the fields we actually used, then read throttleStatus.currentlyAvailable on every response and slowed down before hitting zero instead of retrying after a 429. For the big reads I stopped paginating and used a bulk operation, which sidesteps the bucket entirely.
The guardThe client now reads restoreRate from the response rather than trusting a hard-coded number, so it adapts if the plan changes. Plus an alert if remaining budget stays low for more than a minute.
20 seconds"One that bites people — Shopify's GraphQL limit is calculated query cost, not request count. So the fix usually isn't 'call less', it's 'ask for less'. I read the throttle status off each response and slow down before I hit zero, and I use bulk operations for anything large."

2. The order that arrived twice

SetupAn order webhook feeding a downstream system. Working, shipped, quiet for weeks.
Went wrongThe client noticed some orders appearing twice downstream. Not many. Enough to break their finance reconciliation and their trust in the numbers.
The numberShopify webhook delivery is at-least-once. If it does not get a fast 200, it retries — about 8 times over roughly 4 hours. Our handler was slow, so a retry arrived and we inserted a second row.
What I didTwo changes. Made the handler idempotent — dedupe on X-Shopify-Webhook-Id and upsert on the order ID, so processing the same event twice is the same as once. And moved the heavy work off the request: verify HMAC, answer 200 immediately, then do the real work on a queue.
The guardA test that sends the same event twice and asserts one row. It runs on every build. That test is the reason the bug cannot come back quietly.
20 seconds"I design for duplicate delivery from the first line, because webhooks are at-least-once — Shopify retries about eight times over four hours if you're slow. So I dedupe on the webhook id, upsert on a stable business key, and I keep a test that fires the same event twice."

3. The backfill that hammered the API

SetupGoing live meant loading years of historical orders into the other system before the live sync took over.
Went wrongRun as one big loop, a backfill does three bad things at once: it burns the rate-limit budget so the live sync starts failing, it dies halfway through, and it leaves you unable to say what did and did not land.
The numberThe live sync and the backfill share one bucket. That is the part people miss — the backfill does not just slow itself down, it starves the real-time path that the store depends on.
What I didMade it a job, not a script. Batched under the limit with a concurrency cap. Resumable with a checkpoint, so a failure restarts from the last batch, not from zero. Idempotent (upsert on a stable ID) so re-running is always safe. Bulk operation for the large read instead of a paginated loop. A 1,000-record test batch first, shown to the client before the full run. Then a reconciliation count at the end so we could prove it, not hope.
The guardThe backfill now runs off-peak and at lower concurrency than the live path, so the real-time sync always wins the budget. And the reconciliation count is a permanent job, not a one-off.
20 seconds"The backfill is always the risky part, because it shares the rate-limit bucket with the live sync — so a careless import breaks the thing that's already working. I batch it, checkpoint it so it's resumable, make it idempotent, do a small test batch first, and reconcile counts at the end."

4. The sync that went silent — and stayed silent

SetupA webhook endpoint doing too much work inline, under a load it had not seen before.
Went wrongThe endpoint got slow and started failing. Orders stopped arriving. The worse part came after the fix: it was still silent, because the subscription itself was gone.
The numberYou get 5 seconds to answer a webhook. Keep failing and Shopify retries about 8 times over roughly 4 hours — and then removes the subscription. So a long outage can end with the store no longer sending you anything at all.
What I didAnswered 200 fast and pushed the work to a queue, so a slow downstream system can never cost us the subscription. Then re-registered the webhook and used a reconciliation job — poll the Admin API for orders updated since the last successful sync — to fill the gap for the hours we had missed.
The guardAn alert on "no orders synced in 30 minutes", and the reconciliation job on a schedule. Webhooks for speed, reconciliation for correctness. With that pair, an outage becomes a delay instead of data loss.
20 seconds"Shopify gives you five seconds to answer a webhook, so I never do the downstream write inline — I ack fast and queue it. And I never trust webhooks as the only source of truth: there's always a reconciliation job polling the Admin API, because if you fail long enough Shopify removes the subscription and you go silent without knowing."

5. The deploy that overwrote the merchant's work

SetupA theme change, pushed from git to a live store. Routine.
Went wrongThe merchant had been editing in the theme editor while we worked. A full push put our older local copy of settings_data.json over the top of their changes. Nothing crashed. Their work was simply gone.
The numbershopify theme push uploads local files over remote ones and by default deletes remote files that do not exist locally unless you pass --nodelete. It also needs --allow-live to touch the live theme. Two sources write settings_data.json: the merchant in the editor, and you from git.
What I didRestored what could be restored, then changed how we deploy: on a live store, push narrow with --only the files actually changed, and duplicate the live theme before editing so republishing the old one is a two-minute rollback.
The guardThe narrow-push rule is written into the repo's deploy notes, so the next person does it too — not just me. A guard that lives only in one person's head is not a guard.
20 seconds"A theme has two sources of truth for settings — the merchant in the editor and you from git — so a full push can silently overwrite their work. On a live store I push narrow, with only the files I changed, and I duplicate the live theme first so rollback is two minutes."

Where each story goes in the interview

A war story is not a set piece you announce. You attach it to a technical answer, one line long, then stop. If they want more, they will ask — and that is when the 90-second version comes out.

When they ask about…Drop this story
Rate limits, GraphQL, "how would you pull all products"1 · The rate limit that was not a request limit
Webhooks, reliability, idempotency, "how do you avoid duplicates"2 · The order that arrived twice
Going live, migration, "how would you load our history"3 · The backfill that hammered the API
Monitoring, "how do you know it's working", failure handling4 · The sync that went silent
Deployment, theme work, working alongside a merchant5 · The deploy that overwrote the merchant's work
The line that frames all five If they ask why they should pick you, these stories are the answer: "Most people you'll meet are strong Node developers who have read about Shopify. I've been burned by it — the five-second webhook timeout, the cost-based rate limit, duplicate delivery, the backfill, the theme push. So I design for those from the first line instead of finding them in production."

Only tell stories that are true These are written in the shape of scars you are likely to have from six years of Shopify work. Use the shape, not the fiction. If a story is not yours, swap in one that is — the interviewer will ask a follow-up detail, and an invented story falls apart in one question. A smaller true story always beats a bigger borrowed one.
You've got it when… 1. You can give all five soundbites cold, each with its exact number. 2. Every story ends on the guard without you reminding yourself. 3. In a mock, you attach at least two of them to technical answers without being asked for a story.
