Behavioral STAR Worksheet

Each slot has a sample answer built from a Shopify + backend background — read it, then write your own real version in the boxes and say it out loud until it flows. The sample is a model, not a script. Your stories, in your words, are what win the behavioral round.

Your win: have 6 ready stories you can tell in ~90 seconds each. When the interviewer asks "tell me about a time…", you pick the closest slot and speak — no freezing.

STAR in 4 lines

The shape of every answer S — Situation: set the scene in one or two sentences (where, when, your role).
T — Task: what was the goal or problem you had to solve.
A — Action: what you did — the biggest part; use "I", not "we".
R — Result: the outcome, with a number if you can (faster, fewer bugs, saved time).
Tip for a non-native speaker Keep sentences short. One idea per sentence. It is easier to say clearly and easier for the interviewer to follow. Practice out loud, not just in your head.

Your 6 story slots

1. A hard production bug

Sample answer — ~90 seconds

S: "In my last role I owned a Shopify app that synced store orders into a client's ERP."

T: "We got reports that some orders showed up twice in the ERP, so the client saw double invoices. My job was to find the cause and stop it."

A: "I read the logs and saw Shopify sometimes sent the same order webhook more than once. Our handler was not idempotent, so it created a new ERP record every time. I added an idempotency check on the webhook id, moved the sync work onto a queue so it could retry safely, and added retry with backoff for ERP timeouts."

R: "Duplicate orders dropped to zero, the invoice tickets stopped, and the sync stayed up during a big sale spike. I also added a test that sends the same webhook twice, so it can never come back unnoticed."

2. Mentoring a junior

Sample answer — ~90 seconds

S: "A junior developer joined my team and struggled with our Shopify theme and app code, especially how webhooks and API auth worked."

T: "I wanted them to ship features on their own, not depend on me for every task."

A: "I paired with them on their first tickets, and in code review I left kind, specific comments. I always explained the 'why', not just the fix — for example, why we verify the HMAC signature on every webhook before we trust it. When a task was too big, I helped them break it into small steps."

R: "Within a few weeks they shipped features alone, and their pull requests needed far fewer changes. One of them later mentored the next new hire the same way."

3. A performance win

Sample answer — ~90 seconds

S: "Our nightly job pulled every product and variant from a large Shopify store to sync stock. As the catalog grew, the job got slow and started hitting Shopify's API rate limit, so it sometimes failed halfway."

T: "I set a clear target: make the sync finish reliably every night without hitting the rate limit."

A: "First I measured, and I saw we were making one REST call per product — thousands of small calls. I moved it to a Shopify GraphQL bulk operation, which lets Shopify prepare the whole dataset as one file we download once. I also added a queue so any failed chunk could retry on its own."

R: "The sync went from about 40 minutes with random failures to under 10 minutes with zero rate-limit errors. The nightly job has run clean since, and it now scales as the catalog keeps growing."

4. A trade-off / disagreement

Sample answer — ~90 seconds

S: "When we built the ERP sync, a teammate wanted the webhook handler to call the ERP directly and return. I wanted to put the work on a queue first."

T: "We had to pick one approach quickly, without turning it into a personal argument."

A: "I laid out the trade-off out loud. The direct call was simpler and faster to build. But if the ERP was slow or down, we would lose orders and Shopify would retry the webhook and create duplicates. I suggested we test the risky part with a small spike: we simulated the ERP being down. In that test the direct version lost data; the queue version retried and recovered."

R: "The test settled it, so we chose the queue. It cost us one extra day but saved us from data loss during a real outage two months later. What I took away: I disagree on ideas, not on people, and I let evidence decide."

5. A project you led

Sample answer — ~90 seconds

S: "A client was entering every Shopify order into their ERP by hand. It was slow and full of mistakes. They asked us to automate it, and I was the lead developer on the build."

T: "I owned the plan, the tech choices, and the delivery — working with the PM, QC, and the client's ERP team."

A: "I broke the work into small slices. First I mapped the Shopify order fields to the ERP fields with the client, so we agreed on the data early. Then I built the webhook intake, the queue, and the sync worker with idempotency and retries from day one. I reviewed the team's PRs, kept the client updated each week, and we tested against a sandbox ERP before going live."

R: "We shipped on time. Manual order entry dropped to zero, and order errors fell sharply. The client later asked us to sync refunds and inventory the same way, because they trusted the first build."

6. Your biggest weakness

Sample answer — ~60 seconds

S: "My background is strongest on Shopify and the front end. For a while I was lighter on deep backend testing and system design."

T: "I noticed it slowed me down when I had to reason about a distributed sync on my own."

A: "So I have been closing the gap on purpose. I studied the test pyramid and started writing unit tests for my mapping logic, mocking the Shopify API instead of calling it live. I also practise explaining system design out loud — queues, retries, idempotency — until it feels natural."

R: "I am already more confident, and I now treat testing and design as part of shipping, not an extra step. Picking a real weakness and showing steady progress is the point — never fake a strength like 'I work too hard'."

6 strong questions to ask the interviewer

  1. "What does the backend stack look like today, and what is the biggest technical challenge the team is facing right now?"
  2. "How are Shopify integrations structured here — one big service, or many smaller ones? How do you handle webhook reliability at scale?"
  3. "What does the path from junior to senior look like on this team? How do you support people growing?"
  4. "How do you handle on-call, incidents, and post-mortems? What happens when something breaks in production?"
  5. "How does the team work across time zones, since this is remote? What are the communication expectations?"
  6. "What would a successful first 90 days look like for the person in this role?"
Why this matters Good questions show you think like a senior and you actually want this job. Never say "no, I have no questions."

6 English-speaking tips (for a non-native speaker)

  1. Slow down. Speaking slower sounds more confident and is easier to understand. There is no prize for speed.
  2. Short sentences. One idea per sentence. Stop. Start the next one.
  3. It is fine to pause. Say "let me think for a second" instead of filling silence with "um". A short pause looks thoughtful.
  4. Ask them to repeat. "Could you rephrase that?" is normal and professional. Answering the wrong question is worse.
  5. Signpost your answer. "There are three parts to this…" then count them. It keeps you on track and easy to follow.
  6. End clearly. Finish with the result: "…and that is how I fixed it." Do not trail off; a clear ending sounds strong.
Remember

The behavioral round is not a test of perfect English. It is a test of whether you are a clear thinker and a good teammate. Real stories, told simply and calmly, do that best.
