How I Run My Work

Twelve questions about time, risk, and being easy to manage. This is the round most candidates have never rehearsed — which is exactly why it is where you win.

How to run this: read a question, then say your whole answer out loud before you tap the card. Only after you have spoken, reveal the model answer and compare. Then tick the four boxes in the score sheet. Twelve questions, about five minutes each, one hour total.

This is the mock block for Day 3 of the 7-Day Plan. The depth is in L19 · Ambiguous scope, L20 · Estimating, L21 · Context switching and L22 · Busy client. The Management Scenarios Drill is the slow rehearsal; this page is the timed test.

What the client is really asking Every question below is one question in disguise: "If I hire you and then go quiet for two weeks, what happens to my project?" They are not testing your process. They are testing whether they will have to babysit you.
The rubric — four things every strong answer has (1) A number or a date — a range, a day, a check-in time. Never "as soon as possible". (2) The risk named out loud — you say what could go wrong before they ask. (3) Options plus a recommendation — never a bare problem, never a bare yes. (4) How they will know — the update, the log, the alert, the demo. Make the work visible. Three out of four is a pass. Four out of four sounds like a lead.
The gate — speak first Do not open a card until you have said your full answer aloud. If you go blank, give yourself 20 seconds, then speak anyway — even roughly. Reading the model answer first feels productive and teaches you nothing.

The three answers that lose the room "As soon as possible." It is not a date, so it is not an answer. "I'll let you know if there's a problem." Everyone says this and nobody means it — say when and how instead. "That depends." True, but useless alone. Say what it depends on, then give a range anyway.
You've got it when… 1. Every answer contains a number or a date without you thinking about it. 2. You name a risk before the client asks about it, on at least eight of the twelve. 3. You never end on a problem — always on a recommendation.
