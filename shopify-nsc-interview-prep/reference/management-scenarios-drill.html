Management Scenarios Drill

Owning the delivery is your top differentiator. This sheet turns the management lessons into six situations you can rehearse. For each one: the junior move (the trap), the senior move, and the exact sentence to say.

How to use this: read the situation, cover the answers, and say your response out loud first. Then compare. The goal is that the senior move becomes your reflex, so it comes out naturally when the client asks "how would you handle…".

Backed by lessons 19 · Ambiguous scope, 20 · Estimating, 21 · Context switching, and 22 · Busy client. This sheet is the drill; those are the depth.

The one idea under all six A senior does not wait to be told, and does not go silent when blocked. They make the work visible, name the risk early, and always come with options plus a recommendation — never just a problem.

1. Vague scope — "just sync the orders"

The client hands you a one-line task with no detail and expects you to run with it.
JuniorStarts coding immediately on their own guess of what "sync" means, and builds the wrong thing for two days.
SeniorTurns the vague line into a short written scope with explicit assumptions, sends it back for a quick yes/no, and only then builds. Ships a thin slice first to confirm direction.
Say"Before I build, here's how I'm reading it: sync new and updated orders, one-way Shopify → ERP, near-real-time, and I'll skip cancellations for v1 unless you need them. If that's right I'll have a working slice for one order by tomorrow to confirm the shape."

2. Scope creep mid-project

Halfway through, the client adds "oh, and can it also handle refunds and push to the CRM?"
JuniorSilently says yes to everything, blows the timeline, and delivers late with no warning.
SeniorWelcomes the request, but makes the trade-off visible: what it adds, what it pushes back, and lets the client choose.
Say"Happy to add refunds and the CRM push. That's about three extra days. I can either fold it into this milestone and move the date, or ship the order sync first and do those next. Which do you prefer?"

3. You're going to miss an estimate

A task you estimated at 2 days is clearly going to take 4. You're on day 2.
JuniorStays quiet and hopes to catch up, then surprises the client with a late delivery on the due date.
SeniorFlags it the moment they know, with the reason and a new date. Bad news early is a sign of ownership, not failure.
Say"Heads-up: this is taking longer than I estimated — the ERP's API needs paging I didn't expect. Realistic new date is Thursday. If you need it sooner I can ship the read path first and add writes after. I'll keep estimating out loud so there are no surprises."

4. The client is slow or silent and you're blocked

You need a decision or an API credential to continue, and the client hasn't replied in two days.
JuniorStops working and waits, then blames the client for the delay when asked.
SeniorKeeps moving on unblocked work, makes the blocker visible with a clear deadline and a default, so silence doesn't cost time.
Say"While I wait on the ERP credentials I've built and tested the sync against a mock, so we lose no time. One decision I need by Friday: retry for 24h or dead-letter after 6? If I don't hear back I'll go with 24h retry — it's the safer default — and we can change it later."

5. Two projects, competing priorities

You're solo on this and another project both need you the same week (your real situation — see lesson 21).
JuniorTries to do both at once, context-switches all day, and does both badly.
SeniorProtects focus in blocks, is honest about capacity, and asks the client to help rank if both are truly urgent.
Say"I want to give this real focus, not half-attention. I block mornings for your sync work and afternoons for the other project. If both become urgent the same day, tell me which moves the needle more and I'll put it first — I'd rather finish one thing well than start two."

6. A risky change — the big backfill or migration

You need to backfill 200k historical orders into the ERP, or run a data migration that could break the store.
JuniorRuns it in one go against production, hits the rate limit or a bad record, and half the data is now wrong.
SeniorNames the risk before touching anything, proposes a safe rollout — small batch, dry run, checkpoint, rollback — and reports progress.
Say"This backfill is the risky part, so I'd de-risk it: run it in batches under the rate limit, dry-run against staging first, make it resumable with a checkpoint so a failure doesn't restart from zero, and reconcile counts at the end. I'll do a 1,000-order test batch first and show you the result before the full run."

Drill routine

Fifteen minutes, twice across Days 2–3:

  1. Pick a scenario. Read only the situation line. Cover the rest.
  2. Say your full response out loud — as if the client just asked you.
  3. Uncover the senior move and the "Say" line. What did you miss? Usually: naming the risk, or giving options + a recommendation.
  4. Redo it until your answer lands the three moves every time: make it visible · name the risk early · options with a recommendation.
The one line that ties them together Whenever you're stuck on how to answer a management question, reach for: "Here's what I know, here's the risk, here are the options, and here's what I'd recommend." That sentence pattern is what a senior owner sounds like.
