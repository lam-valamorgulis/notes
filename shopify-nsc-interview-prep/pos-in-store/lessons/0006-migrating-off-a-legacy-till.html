Migrating Off a Legacy Till

The expensive version of Lesson 1's question. A retailer's old till owns the in-store transaction, so the ecommerce platform has never seen an in-store sale. This is how you move it — and why the first phase writes a document, not code.

Your win today: run a POS migration as six phases in the right order, defend why ownership comes before configuration, and describe a parallel run well enough that an interviewer believes you have thought about a cutover date.

Ties to your mission: this is the highest-value system-design drill in the sub-course, because it is a real scoped programme rather than a puzzle. It is built from a real statement of work, anonymised — no client, no vendor, no commercial terms.

1. The situation

A jewellery retailer. Two showrooms and a workshop where pieces are made. Two systems:

  • The ecommerce platform (Shopify) — runs the website.
  • A legacy retail system — runs the in-store tills and manufacturing: production scheduling, costs, the making of the thing.

Its integration is closed. You cannot extend it, you cannot bypass it, and reverse-engineering it is explicitly off the table. The symptoms the business feels:

SymptomRoot cause
Customer data is fragmentedSomeone who buys in a showroom and online is two different people to the business.
Reports conflictBoth systems produce a sales number. They disagree. Nobody can say which is right.
Staff have manual workaroundsEvery gap between the systems has become a human process nobody wrote down.
No cross-channel visibilityCannot answer "did our email campaign drive showroom visits?" at all.

This is Lesson 1's Case B — two systems of truth — at full operational cost. Notice that not one symptom is a technical fault. They are all consequences of an unanswered question: who is allowed to be right?

2. The trap: starting with the sync

The wrong first move, and it is tempting "Let's build a two-way real-time sync between the two systems." It sounds like the helpful engineering answer. It is the answer that fails, for three reasons:
  • You cannot make two databases agree in real time over shop networks. Lesson 1 covered why.
  • Two-way sync means both systems stay authoritative — so every conflict needs a resolution rule, and you will not think of them all.
  • It preserves the actual problem. The business is not suffering from slow sync. It is suffering from ambiguity about which number is real.
The scope note in the real programme says it outright: real-time two-way sync is out of scope, and so is modifying the legacy system.

3. The move: split ownership by data type

Instead of syncing everything both ways, decide — per kind of data — which system is authoritative. One owner each. The other system becomes a consumer or is retired from that concern.

OWNERSHIP MATRIX — the deliverable of phase one ┌────────────────────────┬──────────────┬──────────────────┐ │ Data │ OWNER │ The other system │ ├────────────────────────┼──────────────┼──────────────────┤ │ Sellable inventory │ Shopify │ no longer asked │ │ Online sales │ Shopify │ — │ │ IN-STORE sales ←NEW │ Shopify │ stops recording │ │ Customers │ Shopify │ stops recording │ │ Sales reporting │ Shopify │ reports retired │ ├────────────────────────┼──────────────┼──────────────────┤ │ Manufacturing workflow │ Legacy │ Shopify unaware │ │ Production lifecycle │ Legacy │ Shopify unaware │ │ Manufacturing cost │ Legacy │ Shopify unaware │ └────────────────────────┴──────────────┴──────────────────┘ Every row has EXACTLY ONE owner. A row with two owners is a future incident.
The sentence that reframes the whole project

"We are not integrating two systems. We are decoupling them — deciding which one owns each kind of data, and then removing the other one's claim to it. The integration surface that remains should be narrow and one-directional."

Notice what the matrix does not do: it does not try to replace manufacturing. The legacy system is genuinely good at production, so it keeps it, untouched. Rebuilding manufacturing in Shopify is explicitly out of scope — and resisting that temptation is what keeps the programme deliverable.

4. Six phases, in this order

#PhaseThe deliverableRough size
1Discovery & architecture alignment Ownership matrix · target-state architecture diagram · current-vs-future data-flow map · decision log for boundary cases 1–2 weeks
2aPOS implementation POS configured per store: catalogue, pricing, tax, hardware, staff accounts and permissions. In-store sales create real orders and update customer records. 2–3 weeks
2bInventory model & accuracy Locations set up (each showroom + the workshop), stock allocated, rules written for how inventory enters and leaves, low-stock alerts 2–3 weeks, parallel with 2a
3Reporting & data alignment A reporting ownership matrix — which report comes from which system — configured dashboards, and the duplicate reports switched off —
4Operational enablement Written workflows for sale, return/exchange, pickup, store-to-store transfer. Training delivered. A quick-reference card staff will actually use. ~1 week
5Parallel run & cutover Both systems live, numbers compared, stock reconciled. Then cutover, then a stabilisation period with on-call support. 1–2 weeks
Why phase 1 produces documents, not code A junior plan starts at 2a because that is where the visible work is. But every decision in 2a depends on the ownership matrix — if inventory ownership is still unsettled, you will configure locations twice. The real programme says it plainly: this phase "sets the foundation for all subsequent work" and any later change to its assumptions changes scope, timeline and cost.

Being able to defend "the first two weeks produce a diagram and a decision log" is a senior signal. Juniors apologise for that phase; seniors protect it.

5. The parallel run

You cannot test a POS migration in staging, because the thing you are testing is whether the numbers match reality — and reality is a shelf in a shop. So you run both systems side by side, compare, and only cut over once they agree.

PARALLEL RUN → CUTOVER → STABILISE ┌──────────────┐ │ BASELINE │ take a physical stock count. │ │ without it you cannot tell a │ │ migration bug from pre-existing drift └──────┬───────┘ ▼ ┌──────────────────────────────────────────┐ │ PARALLEL RUN │ │ both tills live · both recording │ │ daily: compare stock, sales, customers │ │ every mismatch → explain it, don't │ │ just "fix" the number │ └──────┬───────────────────────────────────┘ ▼ only when they agree ┌──────────────┐ │ CUTOVER │ new POS becomes the only till │ │ NOT during peak trading └──────┬───────┘ ▼ ┌──────────────┐ │ STABILISE │ on-call for the edge cases │ │ staff questions · early bugs └──────────────┘ ▼ sign-off: the team operates it WITHOUT you

Two details in there carry most of the value:

  • Explain mismatches, do not just correct them. A number you silently fix is a bug you will meet again at higher volume. Each discrepancy is evidence about a broken rule.
  • Take a baseline count first. Retailers always have some pre-existing drift. Without a baseline, every old discrepancy gets blamed on your migration — and you cannot prove otherwise.

6. Risks, and the mitigation that is really a calendar

RiskMitigation
Inventory mismatch during transitionParallel run plus a reconciliation period. Expect drift; catch it deliberately.
Staff struggle with the new tillTraining and simplified workflows. If a workflow needs a long explanation, redesign it.
Dependency on legacy manufacturingClear documented boundaries. Do not touch what you are not replacing.
Peak trading disruptionDo not cut over near a major sales period. For a jeweller that means avoiding Christmas, Valentine's Day, and Mother's Day.
The answer that separates you from a candidate reciting patterns

"I would not pick the cutover date from the engineering plan. I would pick it from the retail calendar, then work backwards. For a jeweller that rules out Christmas, Valentine's Day and Mother's Day — so realistically you are cutting over in a quiet month, and the plan has to fit that window rather than the other way round."

7. Assumptions — the part juniors leave out

The real programme lists its assumptions explicitly. Copy this habit: an assumption written down is a risk you can manage; one left implicit is a surprise.

  • The legacy system keeps running independently for manufacturing.
  • The new POS genuinely meets the in-store requirements. Verify this early — it is the assumption that would invalidate the whole plan.
  • Inventory changes outside sales and transfers are rare. If the workshop constantly adjusts stock by hand, the inventory model needs rethinking.
  • Stakeholders actually agree the ecommerce platform becomes the sales system of truth. This is a political assumption, not a technical one, and it is the most likely to be false.
Definition of done, stated as outcomes Not "the POS is installed", but: the new POS is live for all in-store sales · the platform is the trusted source for sales, inventory and customers · duplicate reporting is gone · the legacy system still runs manufacturing untouched · and the client's team is confident operating it without you. That last one is the real finish line.

For the rest, ask someone who has done it — the r/shopify and Shopify Partner communities have candid migration accounts, including failures that never appear in a case study. Good question to take there: "what did your POS cutover break that you did not predict?"
Remember this
  • The symptoms are organisational, not technical — split customers, conflicting reports, undocumented workarounds. All from an unanswered ownership question.
  • Do not build two-way real-time sync. It cannot be made reliable and it preserves the actual problem.
  • Split ownership by data type. Exactly one owner per row; a row with two owners is a future incident.
  • Do not rebuild what you are not replacing. Manufacturing stays where it works.
  • Phase 1 delivers documents — ownership matrix, target architecture, data-flow map, decision log. Protect that phase.
  • Baseline-count before the parallel run, or old drift gets blamed on you.
  • Explain every mismatch rather than correcting it silently.
  • The cutover date comes from the retail calendar, never from the engineering plan.
If you remember one thing: a POS migration is a decoupling project, not an integration project — you succeed by removing one system's claim to data, not by wiring the two together more cleverly.
