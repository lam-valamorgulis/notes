POS Lite, POS Pro & the Retail Order

A pricing tier is not usually an architecture concern. Here it is — because Click & Collect, stock transfers, and staff permissions all live behind the paid tier, and it is billed per shop.

Your win today: say what POS Lite gives you for free, name the Pro-only features that change a design (pickup in store, transfers, retail staff permissions), and identify an in-store sale in the API by its delivery method: RETAIL.

Ties to your mission: this is the lesson that stops a scoping conversation going wrong. "Can we do Click & Collect?" has a commercial answer before it has a technical one, and knowing that is a consultant's instinct as much as an engineer's.

2. Three Pro-only features that change an architecture

Most of that list is convenience. Three items are different, because a design decision depends on them.

"Pickup in store" is Pro-only

This is the big one. A client says "we want Click & Collect." That is not primarily a build question — it is a subscription question, multiplied by the number of shops. Six shops means six Pro licences before a line of code exists.

The question that makes you sound senior

"Before I scope Click & Collect — are the stores on POS Pro, or POS Lite? Pickup in store is a Pro feature and it is billed per location, so that decides whether we are configuring Shopify or building around it."

"Receiving stock transfers" is Pro-only

Moving stock between shops is how a retail chain balances inventory. If the shops are on Lite, transfers cannot be received on the shop floor — so either stock moves get done in the admin by a manager, or you are building a workaround. Either way it is an operational cost you must surface, not discover during rollout.

"Retail staff permissions and management" is Pro-only

Lite has staff PINs; Pro has roles. The difference matters the moment you ask "who is allowed to discount?" or "who can process a refund without a manager?" Those are the controls that stop shop-floor fraud, so on Lite the answer is a process rather than a permission.

Note what is NOT Pro-only Multi-location inventory is in Lite. So the data model from Lesson 3 — locations, per-location stock, the eight states — is available to everyone. The tier gates workflows on the shop floor, not the inventory model underneath. That distinction is worth stating precisely, because it is easy to assume the cheaper tier cannot handle several shops. It can.

3. How to spot an in-store sale in the API

An in-store sale creates a normal Shopify order. So how do you tell it apart from a website order? At the fulfilment level, by its delivery method.

DeliveryMethodTypeDocumented asMeans
RETAIL"In-store sale, no delivery needed."A POS sale. They walked out with it.
PICK_UP"The order is picked up by the customer."Click & Collect — bought online, collected in store.
SHIPPING"The order is shipped."Ordinary online order.
LOCAL"The order is delivered using a local delivery service."Local delivery from the shop.
PICKUP_POINT"The order is delivered to a pickup point."A third-party collection point, not your shop.
NONE"Non-physical items, no delivery needed."Digital goods, gift cards.

4. A mixed cart splits

One more mechanic worth carrying. If a customer orders two items and only one is in their chosen shop, Shopify does not refuse — it creates separate fulfilment orders, one PICK_UP at the shop and one SHIPPING from elsewhere.

So "an order" is not one unit of fulfilment work. Any system you build that reads orders must handle one order producing several fulfilment orders with different delivery methods, different locations, and different completion times.

Where the stock goes during pickup When a pickup order is placed, the units move from available into committed at that location — sold, still on the shelf, waiting to be picked. That is the state from Lesson 3 doing its job, and it is why a staff count of the shelf will exceed what the website offers.
