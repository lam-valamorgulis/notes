The POS Interview

The capstone. Every POS question an interviewer asks is one of three shapes. This lesson names them, gives you an opener, walks a full whiteboard, and lists the six answers that sink candidates.

Your win today: deliver a 60-second opener on POS that is honest and still impressive, run the full design question in five stages without prompting, and recognise the six red-flag answers before they leave your mouth.

Ties to your mission: this is the lesson that converts the previous six into a performance. Do it last, and do it out loud — reading it silently builds fluency that will collapse under pressure, which is exactly the illusion this course is designed to avoid.

Diagnose before answering If the question is short and about you, it is shape 1 — answer briefly and stop. If it starts with "how would you" or "design", it is shape 2 — ask clarifying questions first. If they put code in front of you, it is shape 3 — hunt for the failure that produces no error.

2. The 60-second opener

You will be asked some version of "what's your POS experience?" early. This answer has to do three jobs at once: be honest, show real mechanism, and not sound apologetic.

Say this, then stop talking

"I've worked daily in storefronts integrated with third-party POS and omnichannel systems, so I know that integration layer well — per-store stock lookups, and the cart-attribute bridge that gets a shopper's chosen store onto the order so fulfilment can read it.

What I haven't done is ship a POS UI Extension or run a till migration end to end. I know how they work — Preact rendered into native components, and migration as a decoupling exercise rather than a sync problem — but I'd be learning the toolchain, not the concepts.

The thing I find most interesting is that most POS pain isn't technical. It's that two systems both think they own the truth."

Why this works, mechanically:

  • It leads with what is true and specific. "Cart-attribute bridge" is a detail only someone who has looked would say.
  • It volunteers the gap before being asked. Volunteered limits read as confidence; extracted limits read as evasion.
  • It ends on an opinion, not a disclaimer. The last sentence invites the next question and shows you think about the domain, not just the API.
Never say this "Yeah, I've done POS work" — when what you maintained was a stock-lookup widget someone else built. One follow-up ("so how did you handle the offline case at the till?") and the whole interview turns into damage control. The honest version is stronger anyway, because it lets you spend your credibility on the parts you genuinely know.

3. The design question, in five stages

"A retailer sells online and in eight shops. Design how in-store stock and sales connect to Shopify." Do not start drawing. Run these stages in order.

Stage 1 — Ask who owns the transaction (60 seconds)

Before anything else: "Is the plan for Shopify POS to be the till, or is there an existing system staying in place?" Everything downstream forks here. Asking it first is itself the senior signal — see Lesson 1.

Stage 2 — Separate the read from the write

"Do we need to show in-store stock online, sell in store, or both?" Showing is a read and much smaller. Selling is three writes and a system of truth. Scoping these together is how projects get mis-estimated.

Stage 3 — Name the ownership, per data type

Do not sync everything both ways. State a matrix out loud: Shopify owns sellable inventory, sales and customers; the legacy system keeps what it is genuinely good at. Exactly one owner per row — Lesson 6.

Stage 4 — Design the stock flow, and mention idempotency unprompted

Ask the POS for totals, write them with inventorySetQuantities because it is absolute and therefore retry-safe. Put the oversell buffer in safety_stock. Leave committed to Shopify. Use fulfillsOnlineOrders: false for shop-only stock — Lesson 3.

Stage 5 — Volunteer the failure mode and the calendar

Describe the oversell timeline yourself. Then say the cutover date comes from the retail calendar, not the sprint plan. Volunteering the thing that will go wrong is what distinguishes experience from revision.

Pacing Stages 1–3 should take about a third of your time. Candidates rush to stage 4 because APIs feel like the "real" answer. The interviewer is mostly judging stages 1–3, because that is where a senior engineer saves or wastes six months of someone's money.

4. The six red flags

What you sayWhat they hearSay instead
"We'll sync both ways in real time." Has never done this. Will over-promise to a client. "Pick a direction, state the lag, reconcile the drift."
"I'd build a custom stock buffer." Has not read the platform docs. "safety_stock is a first-class state for exactly this."
"Just decrement by one on each sale." Will ship silent drift that surfaces months later. "Deltas need exactly-once delivery; I'd prefer an absolute write."
"Yes, I've built POS integrations." One follow-up away from being caught. The 60-second opener above.
"We'd detect pickup from the shipping rate name." Fragile heuristics instead of reading the model. "Read deliveryMethod.methodType — PICK_UP or RETAIL."
"Click & Collect? Yes, we can build that." Hasn't checked what it costs the client. "Are they on POS Pro? Pickup is Pro-only, billed per location."
Cover the screen — this is the highest-value drill on the page Say the correction for each of the six out loud, from memory. If you can only produce the "wrong" column, you will produce it under pressure too.

5. Rapid-fire drill

One line each, answered aloud, no pauses. Cover the answers and work down. These deliberately jump between lessons — mixing topics is harder than blocking them, and that difficulty is what builds retention that lasts.

Remember this
  • Three question shapes: definition (be brief and honest), design (ask first, then structure), critique (hunt silent failure).
  • The opener is 60 seconds: what you know specifically, the gap volunteered, then an opinion.
  • Stage 1 of any design answer is "who owns the transaction?" — never start by drawing.
  • Separate the read from the write before estimating anything.
  • Raise idempotency unprompted — absolute writes over deltas.
  • Ask "Lite or Pro?" before scoping anything in-store. It is billed per location.
  • Volunteer the oversell, and put the cutover date on the retail calendar.
If you remember one thing: they are not testing whether you have used Shopify POS. They are testing whether you can be trusted to decide which system is allowed to be right — and to say honestly what you have not done.
