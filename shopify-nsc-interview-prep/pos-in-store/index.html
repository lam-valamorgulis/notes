POS & In-Store on Shopify

How selling in a physical shop connects to a Shopify storefront — from what a till actually does, through the inventory model and the code that runs on the counter, to migrating a retailer off a legacy system and answering for all of it in an interview.

Why this sub-course exists: "How does in-store stock get onto the site?" is a question you will be asked, and it is the one part of your Shopify story built by other people. Seven lessons close that gap with mechanism you can point at — and a clear, honest line about what you have and have not shipped.

The big picture

Begin at the counter, not the API. What a POS actually is takes twenty seconds at a shop till apart and finds three writes: an order is created, inventory drops at that location, and a payment is recorded. Two words there carry the whole subject. "At a location" means stock is a number per place, not one number. And "writes" means a POS is a system of truth — which is why Shopify POS is not an integration at all, but the same backend with a different front end. That lesson ends with the question that sorts everything: who owns the transaction? One system means nothing to sync. Two systems mean every hard problem in this field, starting with the oversell.

The same distinction reappears in code. Three ways in-store stock reaches a storefront shows that nearly every "POS integration" in a theme is only the read — it looks up shops and never touches money. There are three patterns: a vendor drop-in, cheapest, where the browser queries the POS directly and Shopify learns nothing; a theme-woven widget, where the theme serialises its own catalogue and you own the write-back; and native Shopify POS, which removes the problem. That lesson's real payoff is the cart attribute — the mechanism that carries a shopper's chosen store across checkout onto order.attributes with no app and no webhook — plus nine lines of shipped code containing four textbook failure modes.

Then down into the platform. Locations & multi-location inventory is the data model under "inventory drops at a location": eight inventory states of which only available can be sold, committed meaning sold-but-still-on-the-shelf, and safety_stock — which turns out to be Shopify's own built-in answer to the oversell from Lesson 1. It also settles the decision that makes or breaks a sync job: absolute writes are retry-safe, deltas are not.

POS Lite, Pro & the retail order is the lesson that stops a scoping conversation going wrong, because Click & Collect is a subscription question before it is a build question — pickup in store is Pro-only and billed per shop. It also gives you the field that identifies an in-store sale: deliveryMethod.methodType of RETAIL. Then POS UI Extensions is the one place your code runs on the counter — Preact rendered into native components, tiles and actions and blocks, and an API surface full of scanners and pin pads and printers that tells you plainly this is not a web app.

Migrating off a legacy till is the expensive version of the whole thing: a retailer whose old system owns the in-store transaction, so the platform has never seen a shop sale. The insight is that it is a decoupling project rather than an integration project — you win by splitting ownership per data type and removing one system's claim, not by syncing more cleverly. Six phases, and the first one deliberately produces documents rather than code.

Finally the POS interview converts all of it into a performance: the three shapes every POS question takes, a 60-second honest opener, a five-stage script for the design question, the six red-flag answers that sink candidates, an interleaved rapid-fire drill, and a rubric to score yourself against. The glossary keeps the vocabulary consistent across all seven.
