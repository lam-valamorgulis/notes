When you do not know

The hard questions are not the danger. The seconds after them are. Rehearse those seconds.

sprintDay 7 of 7. This lesson builds one domain-expert ability: explain it from first principles, with no jargon and no notes. Under pressure, with no page to read from. Day 7 in the plan · Glossary

Your win today: twelve sentences arrive without thinking. You can say "I do not know" and gain ground. You never apologise for your English again.
Nothing new here either This lesson adds no Node knowledge. It adds sentences. Sentences are the part that fails first when you are nervous, and the part almost nobody rehearses.

Start from the problem

Think back to any interview that went badly. The damage was almost never the hard question itself.

The damage was the ten seconds after it. The silence, the guess, the apology, the lost thread.

Two failures cause almost all of it, and they arrive together.

  • You do not know the answer. So you guess, or you freeze.
  • Your English deserts you. Not your grammar — your access to words, under stress.

Both feel like knowledge problems. Neither is. More Node facts fix neither of them.

Derive the fix from how you are graded

Start from what the interviewer is actually doing. They are predicting what you will be like as a colleague.

So they are grading reasoning, not recall. That single fact settles everything else.

Step one: silence cannot be graded. If they hear nothing, they score nothing. So silence is always the worst option.

Step two: a confident wrong answer is worse than no answer. It tells them you cannot tell what you know from what you do not.

That is a frightening thing in a colleague. It is how a wrong claim reaches production.

Step three: so the best answer must do two things. It must show reasoning, and it must be honest about the gap.

The shape that works — say this out loud

"I have not used that. What I know that is close is X. My guess would be Y, and I would check it by Z."

Step four: under stress you cannot compose that shape. Working memory is already full.

So it has to be rehearsed, not invented. That is the whole method of this lesson.

Say it in the simplest words — the Feynman check

1 · In plain words When you are nervous, your brain has no spare room to build a sentence. So build the sentences beforehand.

Then, in the room, you are only choosing one. Choosing is cheap. Composing is not.

2 · One analogy A pilot does not invent words during an emergency. They read a checklist that was written when nobody was frightened.

The calm version of you writes the sentences today. The nervous version of you only has to pick one tomorrow.

…and where the analogy breaks A pilot reads the checklist off a card. You cannot read anything tomorrow.

So the sentences must be small enough to hold in memory. Say them out loud enough times and they arrive on their own. Twelve is about the limit. A written list of forty is a list you will not have.

3 · The sticking point People believe "I do not know" costs them the job. It does not. The guess costs them the job.

An interviewer is imagining you in a code review, or in an incident at 2am. Someone who says "I am not sure, I would check" is safe. Someone who sounds certain and is wrong is dangerous.

4 · Now you explain it back

In two or three sentences, say why a confident guess scores lower than an honest "I do not know". Then say what you add after those four words. Send it to me and I will tell you where the gap is. Say it out loud, not in your head. That is the whole point of this lesson.

The twelve rescue sentences

Four groups of three. Learn them until they arrive without effort.

GroupSay this
Did not understand"Sorry, could you say that again a little slower?"
"Just to check I understood — you are asking about X. Is that right?"
"I am not familiar with that term. Could you describe what it does?"
Need a moment"Let me think about that for a second."
"There are two ways to look at this. Let me start with the simpler one."
"Can I draw this? It is easier to show than to say."
Do not know"I have not used that. What I know that is close is…"
"I do not know. My guess would be X, and I would check it by Y."
"I would rather look that up than give you a confident wrong answer."
Lost the thread"Sorry, let me start that again."
"The short version is…"
"Does that answer it, or would you like me to go deeper?"
How to learn twelve sentences in one evening Read them aloud once. Then cover the table and say each group from memory. Repeat until all four groups arrive with no pause. This takes about fifteen minutes and is the highest-value fifteen minutes of Day 7.

The one-second pause

Before every answer, stop for one second. Deliberately.

It does two things at once, and both of them matter.

  • It sounds calm. A fast answer sounds nervous, even when it is right.
  • It stops you answering a question they did not ask. That is the more common failure.

One second feels like a very long time from the inside. It is almost invisible from the outside.

Thinking out loud

Never go silent for more than about ten seconds. They cannot grade what they cannot hear.

If you are stuck, say what you are stuck on. That sentence is worth more than the decision that follows it.

The stuck sentence

"I am deciding between two shapes here. One is a worker pool with a fixed limit. The other is a queue with workers pulling from it. I will take the pool, because it is easier to test."

That sentence shows two designs, a decision, and a reason. Silence would have shown none of them.

The five Node questions most likely to catch you out

These are the gaps this sprint did not close, on purpose. Prepare the honest answer, not the fake one.

QuestionThe honest shape to say
"Have you used AsyncLocalStorage?""Not in production. What I know that is close is passing a request id explicitly. I understand it keeps context across async calls without threading it through."
"How would you use diagnostics_channel?""I have not used it. My guess is a publish-and-subscribe hook for instrumentation without patching. I would check the docs before claiming more."
"Talk about raw TCP with net.""I have built on http, not below it. What I do know is that http is built on net, and both are emitters and streams."
"Which message broker have you run?""I have used a queue in this design, not operated a broker. I know the properties I need: retries, backoff, and a dead-letter queue."
"How deep is your Kubernetes experience?""Shallow, and I would say so. I know what my service needs from it: a health check, a SIGTERM, and time to drain."
Trap — the answer that sounds like a memorised page If you have never used a thing, do not describe it in the words of its documentation. Interviewers hear that instantly, and they will go one level deeper to check. Naming the boundary of your experience is stronger than covering it. "I have not run one, but I know the properties I need" is a senior sentence.

Speaking English under pressure

Three things, and only three. Do not add a fourth.

Do thisWhy it works
Slow downSpeed is what makes an accent hard to follow, not the accent itself.
Finish final consonantsEndings carry tense and number. Dropping them costs more meaning than any vowel.
Never apologise for your EnglishIt moves their attention from your answer onto your accent. You never want that trade.

If a word will not come, describe it instead. "The thing that holds open connections" is a perfectly good sentence.

Drill · 20 minutes, out loud, tonight

Sit alone. Say every rescue sentence from memory, group by group, until none of them needs a pause.

Then run the harder half.
  • Answer three questions you genuinely cannot answer, using the "do not know" shape.
  • Read one of your own stories aloud, deliberately finishing every final consonant.
  • Record 60 seconds. Listen back once, only for pace. Do not judge the accent.
  • Practise the one-second pause five times, until it stops feeling long.
  • Say the stuck sentence out loud twice, so it exists before you need it.
