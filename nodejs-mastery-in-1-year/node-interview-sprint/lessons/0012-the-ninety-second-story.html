The 90-second story

The answer you will give more than any other. Ninety seconds is short, so every sentence has to earn its place.

sprintDay 4 of 7. This lesson builds one domain-expert ability: explain it from first principles, with no jargon and no notes. Day 4 in the plan · Glossary

Your win today: you have three stories, ninety seconds each, rehearsed out loud and recorded. Each one has a hook in the first sentence and a number in the middle. You never say "we" when you mean "I".

Start from the problem — ninety seconds is very short

Say a normal speaking pace of about 130 words a minute. Ninety seconds is roughly 200 words.

That is one short page. You cannot fit context, history and detail into it.

Watch what most people do with those 200 words. They spend 150 on background nobody asked for.

"So the company had this product, and the team was five people, and we were using…" The clock is gone.

So the question is not what to include. It is what to cut.

The rule that decides every cut

Keep a sentence only if it makes the next question better. You are not trying to finish the topic. You are trying to be asked about the right part of it.

Derive the five sentences

The interviewer needs four things to ask a good follow-up. Each one costs one sentence.

#SentenceWhy it earns its place
1What it is, in one lineGives them a picture to attach everything else to
2The one hard constraintTurns a project into a problem
3What you didSeparates you from your team
4The numberMakes the whole thing checkable
5What you would change nowShows judgement, and invites the next question

Five sentences, about 200 words, and it fits. Everything else is answered after they ask.

The spine API in five sentences

Say this, then stop talking

"I built and ran a link shortener in Node. The interesting part was the read side. Reads outnumber writes heavily, and every redirect had to stay fast. We also had to count every click. I owned the HTTP layer, the error model and the shutdown path.

Then I moved click counting onto a queue and fixed graceful shutdown. Deploys went from dropping requests to dropping none. Shutdown fell from about 3.5 seconds to about half a second. If I started again, I would put an explicit timeout on every outbound call. That was the failure that surprised me most."

Count what that does. It gives them four different doors to open.

  • The read-heavy shape → caching, and why a redirect is a 302.
  • "I owned the error model" → the status code questions.
  • The shutdown number → SIGTERM, server.close(), keep-alive sockets.
  • The timeout regret → pool exhaustion, and how one slow thing takes everything down.

Every one of those doors leads somewhere you are strong. That is the point of the design.

"I" and "we" — be exact, never inflate

This matters more than people think. Interviewers listen for it closely.

Use we for decisions the team made. Use I for work you did with your own hands.

Saying "we" for everything hides you. Saying "I" for everything is a claim they will test.

Do not saySay instead
"We built a link shortener.""The team built it. I owned the HTTP layer and the shutdown path."
"I designed the whole system.""I proposed the error model. We agreed it in review, then I implemented it."
"We fixed a memory leak.""I took the snapshots and found the retainer path. The fix was reviewed by two people."
"I have five years of Node.""About two years of Node in production, and before that front-end work."
Trap — padding experience A padded claim does not fail when you say it. It fails two questions later, when the detail is missing. And once one claim breaks, the interviewer re-checks everything else you said. An exact small claim is stronger than a vague large one.

Your three stories

Three is enough. Almost every behavioural question maps onto one of them.

StoryThe hook — first sentenceEnds on
The hard thing"The redirect had to stay fast while we also counted every click.""Nothing that a user waits for should do a write it does not need."
The bug"Memory climbed all day and never came back, so we restarted it every night.""Any long-lived Map now has to say how entries leave. That went on the review list."
The failure"Every deploy dropped requests, and my first fix made it worse.""process.exit does not flush. I now measure a fix before I trust it."

Each hook does one job. It makes the listener want the next sentence.

Each ending does one job too. It states a rule you now carry, so the story is not just an anecdote.

The three-level chain — they always go deeper twice

Assume the ninety seconds worked. Now the real interview starts.

LevelThe questionYour job
1"Tell me about something you built."Ninety seconds. Five sentences. Then stop.
2"Why did you do X that way?"Name the alternative, and what it would have cost.
3"What broke?"One of the two bug stories from Lesson 11, with its number.
Trap — the story with no number "It got much faster" is not a story, it is a feeling. Say 3540 ms to 538 ms, or heap 3.4 MB to 43.5 MB. A number is the only part of your story the interviewer can hold on to. If you truly never measured it, say that plainly instead of inventing one.
Say it in the simplest words — the Feynman check

1 · In plain words You have room for about five sentences. Use them to hand the listener five things to ask about.

You are not summarising the project. You are choosing which part of it gets discussed next.

2 · One analogy A menu, not a meal. A menu is short and it makes you want to order something.

Nobody wants the whole kitchen described. They want a few clear options, each one sounding worth choosing.

…and where the analogy breaks A menu lets the customer pick anything, and every dish must be equally ready.

Here you decide what is on it. So never list something you cannot go deep on. Mentioning a technology you touched once is offering a dish you cannot cook. The follow-up is coming, and it is your own fault.

3 · The sticking point People believe a longer answer sounds more experienced. The opposite is true.

A long answer sounds like someone who cannot tell what matters. Stopping after ninety seconds, in silence, reads as confidence.

4 · Now you explain it back

In two or three sentences, say why the ninety-second answer is designed around the next question. Then say what you would cut first. Send it to me and I will tell you where the gap is. Do not skip this. Saying it in your own words is the part that proves it.

Drill · record all three, listen back once

Write your three stories in your own words. Then record each one on your phone, standing up, at full volume.

Listen back once only. Check four things, and nothing else.
  • Length. Is it between 75 and 100 seconds? Over two minutes means cut, not speed up.
  • The hook. Does the first sentence make you want the second one?
  • The number. Did you say one out loud?
  • "I" and "we". Is each one accurate?
Then re-record only the story that failed a check. Do not re-record all three.

Remember this · the 20-second recap
  • Ninety seconds is about 200 words, which is five sentences. Everything else waits.
  • The five: what it is, the constraint, what I did, the number, what I would change.
  • Keep a sentence only if it makes the next question better.
  • We for the team, I for your own hands. Be exact, never inflate.
  • Never offer a technology you cannot go two levels deep on.
  • Three stories cover almost everything: the hard thing, the bug, the failure.
  • Say a real number, or say plainly that you never measured it.

If you remember one thing: you are not finishing the topic. You are choosing the next question.
