The questions you ask, and closing

The last ten minutes are scored too. Most candidates give them away. This is the cheapest ground in the whole interview.

sprintDay 7 of 7. This lesson builds one domain-expert ability: judge someone else's work in it, and say what is wrong and why. Today the work you judge is their engineering culture. Day 7 in the plan · Glossary

Your win today: six questions written down, a salary sentence you can say without flinching, and a closing that takes sixty seconds. Then you stop working.

Start from the problem

Near the end, they say: "any questions for us?"

Most candidates hear that as the end of the interview. It is not. It is still the interview.

The common answers waste it. "No, I think you covered everything." Or a question about holiday. Or something already on the careers page.

None of those are punished. They simply score nothing, in a slot where scoring is easy.

Derive what a good question is

Start from what the interviewer can learn here. They cannot learn Node facts from your question.

What they learn is what you think matters. That is a strong signal about how you would work.

So the question is a claim about your priorities, whether you mean it as one or not.

Now ask what a backend engineer's priorities actually are. Not features. Not the roadmap.

A backend engineer cares about what breaks, and who fixes it. That is the job.

The test for any question you plan to ask

"Does the answer change how I would do the work?" If yes, ask it. If no, it is small talk, and small talk scores nothing here.

There is a second benefit, and it is not a trick. You genuinely need these answers.

You are also deciding. A team with no rollback story and weekly 2am pages is a real thing to know about.

Say it in the simplest words — the Feynman check

1 · In plain words When you ask a question, you are telling them what you care about. You cannot avoid that.

So choose questions about the things a backend engineer really lives with. What breaks, how often, and who gets woken up.

2 · One analogy Someone viewing a house asks about the roof and the drains, not about the paint colour.

The estate agent learns something from that question. This person has owned a house before, and knows what actually costs money.

…and where the analogy breaks A house survey is one-directional. You inspect, the house does not inspect you.

Here both things happen at once. You are learning whether you want the job, and they are learning how you think. So a question asked only to look clever fails both jobs. Ask things you genuinely want the answer to.

3 · The sticking point People prepare questions to impress, then ask them without listening to the answer.

The answer is the point. If they say the last incident took six hours to roll back, that is real information. Follow it with one more question, and you are having a conversation between engineers.

4 · Now you explain it back

In two or three sentences, say why "any questions for us?" is still part of the scoring. Then name the one test for a good question. Send it to me and I will tell you where the gap is. This is the last teach-back of the sprint. Say it out loud, then close the laptop.

Six questions only a backend engineer asks

Write these on one page. Take that page into the room.

#AskWhat you are listening for
1 "What does on-call look like, and how often does it actually fire?" Good: a number, and a story about reducing it. Bad: "it is quiet", with no number.
2 "What is the deploy story, and how long does a rollback take?" Good: minutes, and someone has done one recently. Bad: "we have never needed to."
3 "Where does the Node service sit, and what is behind it?" Good: a clear drawing in words. Bad: vagueness, which usually means nobody owns it.
4 "What was the hardest production incident this year, and what changed after it?" Good: a real story with a fix. Bad: a blameful story, or "nothing much happens."
5 "How do you decide between fixing something and rewriting it?" Good: a rule, or an example. Bad: "we rewrite when it gets bad", with no test.
6 "What would a good first three months look like for this person?" Good: something concrete and owned. Bad: a list of onboarding tasks only.
Ask three, not six You will not have time for six, and asking all of them turns it into an interrogation. Prepare six, ask two or three, and follow the most interesting answer with one more question. That follow-up is worth more than a fourth prepared question.
Trap — the question you do not want answered Do not ask something to look clever. If they answer in depth and you have nothing to say back, the question is exposed as decoration. Only ask what you would genuinely act on. Every one of the six above changes whether you take the job.

Salary, in one sentence

They will ask. Decide your range today, not in the room.

Say it in one sentence, then stop. The silence after it is not yours to fill.

Say this, then stop talking

"I am looking in the range of X to Y, depending on the whole package. Does that sit inside your band?"

Two things make this work. A range shows flexibility without giving ground.

And the question at the end hands the next move back to them. You are not negotiating yet, only checking the fit.

Decide the range before the interview.Work it out live, out loud.

The last sixty seconds

Three moves. Say them in order, then stop.

MoveSay something like
1 · Interest, specific"This is the kind of backend I want to work on. Real traffic, and problems that only appear under load."
2 · One reason you fit"What I would bring straight away is a habit of measuring before changing anything."
3 · Next step"What are the next steps, and when should I expect to hear?"

Then be quiet. Silence at the end reads as confidence, not as an awkward gap.

Remember this · the 20-second recap
  • "Any questions for us?" is still the interview. It is easy ground, and most people give it away.
  • Your question shows what you think matters. A backend engineer asks what breaks and who fixes it.
  • The test: does the answer change how I would do the work? If not, it is small talk.
  • Prepare six questions. Ask two or three, then follow the best answer with one more.
  • Salary: one sentence, a range you decided today, then stop talking.
  • Closing: specific interest, one reason you fit, then ask the next step directly.
  • Then be quiet. Silence at the end reads as confidence.

If you remember one thing: you are not taking an exam. You are showing someone what it is like to work with you.
