Day 6 of 7 · Lesson 18 · Scale and design

System design — one million notifications an hour

The design round has no right answer. It has a method, and the method is what they grade.

sprintDay 6 of 7. This lesson builds one domain-expert ability: say why it was built this way, and what that choice gives up. Day 6 in the plan · Glossary

Your win today: you can take a one-line design brief and run a five-step method on it. Questions, arithmetic, a drawing, the failure modes, and the trade-off you chose. Twenty minutes, out loud, with a pen.

Start from the problem — the brief is deliberately empty

The brief, exactly as they will give it

"Design a service that sends one million notifications an hour."

That is the whole brief. It is short on purpose. What you do in the next two minutes is the test.

Most candidates start drawing boxes. That is the mistake.

The brief is missing everything that decides the design. Channel, latency, duplicates, failure behaviour.

So the first move is not a box. It is a question.

Say this before you draw anything

"Before I draw, I want four things. What channel is this — email, push, or SMS? How fast must one notification arrive? Is a duplicate acceptable, or must it be exactly once? And what should happen when the provider is down?"

Every one of those four changes the answer. Say why, briefly, as you ask.

The questionWhy the answer changes the design
Which channel?Push is fast and cheap. SMS is slow, costly and rate limited.
How fast?Seconds means a queue. Milliseconds means no queue, and a much harder problem.
Duplicates allowed?This decides whether you need idempotency keys and a sent log.
Provider down?This decides retries, backoff, and whether you drop or hold.
Is it a burst?One million spread evenly is easy. One million in five minutes is not.

For the rest of this lesson, assume these answers. Push notifications. A few seconds is fine. A rare duplicate is acceptable. The provider does go down.

Step two — do the arithmetic out loud

Numbers turn a vague brief into a real one. Divide in front of them.

1,000,000 per hour ÷ 3600 seconds = about 278 per second, on average peak is not average — assume 5x = about 1,400 per second at peak one provider call takes about 100 ms (I would measure this) one worker with 50 in flight → about 500 per second so about 3 workers at peak, plus headroom

Say plainly which of those numbers is an assumption. That honesty scores.

Say the assumption out loud 278 per second is arithmetic, and it is certain. The 5x peak is an assumption I am choosing. The 100 ms provider call is a guess I would replace with a measurement on day one. Naming which is which is the difference between an engineer and a guess.

278 per second is a small number. Say that too.

It tells the interviewer that the volume is not the hard part. The failures are.

Step three — draw it

Five boxes. Keep it small, and add only what a question forces you to add.

Say why the queue is the centre. It does three jobs at once.

  • It absorbs the peak, so a 5x burst becomes a longer drain, not a failure.
  • It gives retries with backoff for free when the provider fails.
  • It lets you add workers without touching the producer at all.

Step four — name the failures before they ask

This is the step that wins the round. Volunteer the failure modes.

FailureWhat actually happensWhat you do
Provider rate limitIt returns 429, and naive retries make it worse.Respect the limit, back off, and slow the workers down.
Provider downJobs fail and retry. The queue grows fast.Backoff with jitter, plus a circuit breaker so you stop hammering.
DuplicatesDelivery is at least once, so a crash after sending re-sends.An idempotency key and a sent log. Check before you send.
Poison messageOne bad job fails forever and blocks a retry slot.Cap the attempts, then move it to the dead-letter queue.
Queue growsYou are producing faster than you drain. It never recovers alone.Alarm on queue depth and on age. Add workers, or shed load.
Thundering restartEvery worker reconnects at once after a deploy.Stagger the start, and warm the cache slowly.
Trap — retrying a 4xx A 429 means slow down. A 400 means the request is wrong, and it will be wrong every time. Retrying a 400 burns the queue and never succeeds. Retry on 5xx, on timeouts, and on 429 with backoff. Never on other 4xx. Say that rule out loud — it is short and it lands.

Step five — the Node part, and the trade you chose

Now make it a Node answer. Two facts from this week carry the whole thing.

First, a worker sending notifications is I/O-bound. It waits on the network almost the whole time.

So one Node process holds many calls in flight cheaply. That is exactly what the runtime is good at.

Second, in flight must be bounded. An unbounded Promise.all over the batch would flood the provider.

So each worker uses the pool pattern from Kata 2, with a limit you can tune.

For more throughput, add processes: cluster or more containers, as in Lesson 16.

Close with the trade — say this

"I chose at-least-once delivery with idempotency keys. That gives up exactly-once, so a user can very rarely see a duplicate. I took that because exactly-once needs a distributed transaction with the provider, and no provider offers one. A rare duplicate is cheaper than a lost notification."

How would you know it is working?

They almost always ask this last. A design with no way to watch it is not finished.

Name three graphs, and say what each one tells you. Three is enough, and it sounds decided.

GraphWhat it tells youThe alarm
Queue depth and ageWhether workers keep up with producersDepth rising for several minutes in a row
Send outcome rateSuccess, retry and dead-letter counts per minuteAny dead-letter arrivals at all
Provider latencyp50 and p99 of the outgoing callp99 rising while the success rate holds

Queue age matters more than depth. A short queue that is not moving is worse than a long one that is.

The sentence that closes this question "Queue age is my main alarm, because it turns 'the system is behind' into a number with a threshold." Then add the honest part: a dead-letter queue nobody reads is the same as no dead-letter queue.
Say it in the simplest words — the Feynman check

1 · In plain words Do not send a million things one by one from the place that asks for them. Write them all on a list.

Then a small number of helpers read the list at a steady pace. If a helper fails, the item goes back on the list and is tried again later.

2 · One analogy A post office. Letters go into sacks, and a few vans do rounds all day at a fixed pace.

A busy morning fills the sacks faster than the vans empty them. Nothing is lost. Everything simply arrives later.

…and where the analogy breaks A letter is delivered once. A queued job can be delivered twice, and that is normal.

If a worker sends the notification and then crashes before saying "done", the job comes back and is sent again. That is why every job needs an idempotency key and a sent log. The post office never has this problem.

3 · The sticking point People size the design for the average and stop. One million an hour is only 278 per second.

That number is easy, and it is not the question. The real question is what happens at a 5x peak. And what happens when the provider returns 429 for ten minutes.

4 · Now you explain it back

In two or three sentences, say why the queue is the centre of this design. Then say what at-least-once delivery costs you. Send it to me and I will tell you where the gap is. Do not skip this. Saying it in your own words is the part that proves it.

Trap — the design with no cost in it A candidate who lists only benefits sounds like a brochure. Every choice here costs something. The queue costs latency and duplicates. The cache costs staleness. More workers cost provider pressure. Name the cost of your own design, unprompted. Interviewers use that single habit to sort seniors from mid-level engineers.
Drill · 20 minutes, out loud, with a pen

Set a timer for twenty minutes. Design it from the empty brief, alone, out loud, on paper.

Then score yourself against the five steps above. Anything you skipped is tomorrow's warm-up.

Remember this · the 20-second recap
  • Ask four questions before you draw. Channel, latency, duplicates, and provider failure.
  • Divide out loud. One million an hour is about 278 per second, which is a small number.
  • Say which numbers are arithmetic and which are your own assumptions.
  • The queue is the centre. It absorbs the peak, retries for you, and lets you add workers.
  • Retry on 5xx, timeouts and 429 with backoff. Never retry other 4xx.
  • Workers are I/O-bound, so one Node process holds many calls in flight — but bounded.
  • At-least-once delivery is the normal choice, so every job needs an idempotency key.

If you remember one thing: the volume is never the hard part. The failures are.
