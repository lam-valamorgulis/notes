Day 4 of 7 · Lesson 11 · Your own service

Two bugs you fixed

The strongest thing you own. Nobody can copy a bug you actually chased, and nobody can fake the mechanism.

sprintDay 4 of 7. This lesson builds one domain-expert ability: debug a problem in it that has no ready answer online. Day 4 in the plan · Glossary

Your win today: you have two bug stories, ninety seconds each, in your own words. Each one names a measurement, a mechanism, a fix and a guard. You can survive two levels of follow-up on both.

Start from the problem — why most bug stories earn nothing

Almost every candidate tells the same story. "It was broken, I looked into it, then I fixed it."

Ask yourself what an interviewer learns from that. Nothing at all.

The story has no test in it. Anyone can say those words about any bug, real or invented.

So what can be tested in a spoken story? Only one thing: the mechanism.

A mechanism is a chain of causes. Each link has to fit the previous one. That is why it cannot be faked.

The one sentence to remember

A bug story is graded on the part between the symptom and the fix. Most people delete exactly that part.

Derive the four parts

Work backwards from what has to be checkable. Four parts fall out, in this order.

PartWhat it must containWhy it is there
1 · SymptomWhat a person saw, in one sentenceGives the interviewer a place to stand
2 · MeasurementA number, and the tool that produced itSeparates debugging from guessing
3 · MechanismThe chain of causes, each link namedThe only part that cannot be faked
4 · Fix and guardThe change, plus what stops it returningShows you finished, not just patched

Part 4 has two halves on purpose. A fix without a guard is a bug waiting to come back.

Bug A — memory that went up and never came down

This is your leak story, from the spine API. Rewrite every line in your own words.

PartSay this
SymptomThe service was restarted every night. Memory climbed all day and never fell back.
MeasurementI watched used_heap_size, not RSS, because RSS is noisy. It went from 3.4 MB to 43.5 MB with 200,000 objects retained.
Not yet a leakRising memory is normal. The signal is memory that does not return across two collections.
MechanismTwo heap snapshots, compared and sorted by delta. The retainer path named a Map used as a cache with no eviction. Every new short code added an entry and nothing ever removed one.
FixA bounded cache with an eviction policy and a size limit.
GuardAn alert on heap that does not return after a collection. And a review rule: any long-lived Map must say how entries leave.
ProofAfter the release the heap came back to 3.4 MB.
The sentence that makes this story land "The collector asks whether an object is reachable. It never asks whether it is needed." That is the mechanism in one line. The cache was reachable, so nothing was ever freed. The collector was working correctly the whole time.

Interviewers love this story for a reason. Four shapes cover nearly every Node leak.

  • A growing collection — a Map or array with no eviction.
  • A forgotten listener — one added per request, never removed.
  • A fat closure — a small callback keeping a large buffer alive.
  • An uncleared timer — a setInterval nobody stops.

Bug B — requests dropped on every deploy

This is your shutdown story. It has a first fix that was wrong, which makes it better.

PartSay this
SymptomEvery deploy produced a small burst of failed redirects. Nothing in the logs explained it.
First attemptSomeone had added process.exit(0) on SIGTERM, to make shutdown fast.
MeasurementI proved it was wrong. Writing 300,000 lines into a slow pipe and then calling process.exit(0) delivered only 320 of 300,000 lines. The exit code was still 0.
Mechanismprocess.exit does not flush. Anything still queued is simply lost, and the process reports success anyway. So the platform saw a healthy deploy.
Second measurementThe correct handler used server.close(), but it took 3540 ms. Keep-alive sockets were idle and still open.
Mechanismserver.close() stops new connections and waits for existing ones. An idle keep-alive socket counts as existing. keepAliveTimeout is 5000 ms, so it waits.
FixCall server.closeIdleConnections() as well. Shutdown dropped to 538 ms.
GuardA load test that sends SIGTERM at full traffic and asserts zero failed requests.
Trap — the story with no measurement "I noticed memory was high so I added a cache limit" is a guess that happened to work. Say the tool and the number. Two heap snapshots and a retainer path, or 3540 ms down to 538 ms. Without a number, the interviewer cannot tell you apart from someone who changed things until the symptom went away.
Say it in the simplest words — the Feynman check

1 · In plain words A good bug story is a chain. Each link explains the next one, and the whole chain reaches the symptom.

If any link is missing, the listener notices at once. That is why a real story is hard to invent on the spot.

2 · One analogy A doctor explaining an illness. Not "you were ill, now you are better".

Instead: here is the test result. Here is what the result means. Here is why that produced your symptom. Here is what stops it returning.

…and where the analogy breaks A doctor can order the same test again. You often cannot.

Production evidence disappears. The process restarts, the logs roll over, the load goes away. So the measurement must be taken while the problem is happening, and written down. A story you never measured is a story you can no longer tell.

3 · The sticking point People pick their hardest bug. That is the wrong filter.

Pick the bug whose mechanism you can still explain. A simple bug told with a full chain beats a hard one told as "it was some race condition somewhere".

4 · Now you explain it back

In two or three sentences, say why the mechanism is the part that cannot be faked. Then say what a story with no number sounds like. Send it to me and I will tell you where the gap is. Do not skip this. Saying it in your own words is the part that proves it.

The follow-up chain — they will always go two levels deeper

Expect this shape. Prepare each level once and you never get cornered.

LevelThey askYou have ready
1"Tell me about the system."The five-layer drawing from Lesson 10.
2"Tell me about a hard bug in it."Bug A or Bug B, four parts, ninety seconds.
3"How did you know that was the cause?"The tool and the number. Snapshots and the retainer path, or 3540 to 538.
4"What would you change now?"One concrete change, and one thing you would deliberately leave.
5"Has it happened again?"The guard. The alert, or the load test that asserts zero failures.
Drill · write both, then record both

Write Bug A and Bug B in your own words. Four parts each. Then say each one out loud and record it.

Listen back once. Check three things only.
  • Is it under ninety seconds?
  • Did you say a number, and the tool that gave it?
  • Can a listener repeat the mechanism back to you?
If the answer to the third is no, the mechanism is still missing. Rewrite that part and record it again.

Remember this · the 20-second recap
  • Four parts: symptom, measurement, mechanism, fix and guard. Never drop the middle two.
  • The mechanism is the only part that cannot be faked. It is the whole grade.
  • Pick the bug you can still explain, not the hardest one you ever met.
  • Leak story: heap 3.4 MB to 43.5 MB, two snapshots, the retainer path named a Map.
  • The collector asks whether an object is reachable, never whether it is needed.
  • Shutdown story: process.exit(0) delivered 320 of 300,000 lines, and still exited 0.
  • server.close() took 3540 ms; adding closeIdleConnections() made it 538 ms.

If you remember one thing: no number means an opinion. No mechanism means a guess.
