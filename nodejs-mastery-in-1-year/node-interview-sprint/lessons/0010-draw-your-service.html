Draw your service

Today is the day that decides the interview. Everyone can talk about the event loop. Only you can talk about this.

sprintDay 4 of 7. This lesson builds one domain-expert ability: explain it from first principles, with no jargon and no notes. Day 4 in the plan · Glossary

Your win today: you can draw the spine API on a whiteboard in about ninety seconds. Five boxes, the arrows between them, and one arrow labelled with what fails. Every box survives a follow-up question.

Start from the problem — what can the interviewer actually check?

They have five minutes. They cannot read your code. They cannot call your team.

So they cannot check your features. A list of features is unfalsifiable, and both of you know it.

What they can check is whether your description holds together. They ask a question. Your answer must fit what you already said.

That is the whole test. So give them something with a shape, not a list.

The reframe — say this to yourself before you start

They are not asking what you built. They are asking whether you know why it is shaped that way. A drawing is the fastest way to show that.

Derive the five layers

Every request-serving system answers the same four questions. The layers fall out of the questions.

The questionThe layer it creates
Who is asking?Client
What happens before my code sees it?Edge — TLS, load balancing, timeouts
Where does my code run?Your process — routes, validation, errors
Where does the truth live?Data — the store and its constraints
What is slow, and moved off the path?Behind — cache and queue

Five layers. Always the same five. That is why it is repeatable under pressure.

Then add one arrow labelled with what fails. A drawing with no failure on it looks like marketing.

The drawing, grounded in the spine API

Defend each box in one sentence

A box you cannot defend is worse than no box. Learn these five sentences.

BoxThe sentence
GET /:codeThe hot path. Reads vastly outnumber writes, so this is the route I optimise.
302, not 301A 301 is cached by browsers and cannot be taken back. I chose control over one round trip.
The 409A custom code that is already taken. The unique index is what makes it real, not my if.
The error shapeOne sendError. code is stable, message is free to reword, details is structured.
The queueCounting a click must not slow a redirect. The write goes to a queue and lands late.

The 400 against 409 test

This is the status-code question interviewers actually ask. Do not recite a definition.

Say the test, not the definition

"I ask one question: could this exact request have succeeded a minute ago? If no, it is a 400. If yes, and it only fails because of what exists now, it is a 409."

Broken JSON is a 400. Retrying never helps.

A short code someone else just took is a 409. It was free a minute ago.

That difference is actionable. A 409 means pick another code. A 400 means fix your request.

The two objects inside the box

They may point at your process box and ask what your handler receives. Most candidates wave.

Both objects come from the same family. Every stream, socket and process in Node is an EventEmitter.

req: IncomingMessage -> Readable      -> Stream -> EventEmitter
res: ServerResponse  -> OutgoingMessage -> Stream -> EventEmitter
Measured — Node v24.14.0, 2026-08-28 req instanceof Readable is true. res instanceof Writable is false — it goes through OutgoingMessage and only implements the interface. Also: emit is synchronous, emit('error') with no listener throws, and defaultMaxListeners is 10.

Saying that res is not really a Writable proves you checked. Nobody guesses that.

Say it in the simplest words — the Feynman check

1 · In plain words A drawing is a promise. Each box says "this part has one job", and each arrow says "this is what travels".

The interviewer then pokes at it. A real drawing keeps its shape. A made-up one falls apart at the second question.

2 · One analogy A floor plan of a house. It does not list the furniture. It shows the rooms and the doors between them.

Anyone can check a floor plan by walking it. Say the kitchen is next to the hall, then say it is upstairs, and the plan is wrong.

…and where the analogy breaks A floor plan is still. A service is not. Nothing on a floor plan can be slow.

Your drawing must show time and failure. That is why one arrow is labelled with what breaks. Without it you have drawn a picture of a healthy day. Healthy days are not what they ask about.

3 · The sticking point People draw the system they wish they had built. They add a cache they never wrote, or a queue that is still a plan.

The second question always finds it. Draw what exists, then say plainly what is planned and why it is not there yet.

4 · Now you explain it back

In two or three sentences, say why the drawing has an arrow labelled with a failure. Then say what a drawing without one looks like. Send it to me and I will tell you where the gap is. Do not skip this. Saying it in your own words is the part that proves it.

Drill · draw it three times from memory

Close this page. On paper, draw the five layers, the five routes, the store, the cache and the queue. Then add the failure arrow.

Do it three times today, with a gap between each one. Time yourself. The target is ninety seconds. Say each box out loud as you draw it, because you will have to do that in the room.
  • Run 1 — page open beside you. Just get the shape.
  • Run 2 — page closed. Note what you forgot.
  • Run 3 — page closed, out loud, on a clock.
Trap — the tour of the code Do not walk through your folders. "There is a routes folder, then a services folder…" tells the interviewer nothing about the system. It tells them about your editor. Layers and failures, never folders.
