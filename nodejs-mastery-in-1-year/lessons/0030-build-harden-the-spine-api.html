The new idea is the timeout you did not set. Every outbound call has a timeout whether you chose one or not — and the default is usually “forever”.

Start from the problem — every wait is unbounded until you bound it

Your service calls a database, a cache, another API. Ask a simple question about each one: if it never answers, what happens?

Without an explicit timeout, the answer is that your request waits forever. One request holding a connection is fine. But requests keep arriving, and each one holds another connection, so:

a dependency that hangs
  → every request to it waits forever
  → the connection pool empties
  → requests that do not use that dependency now wait for a pool slot
  → the whole service is down because one thing was slow
The idea this week adds

A slow dependency is more dangerous than a broken one. A broken one fails fast and you handle it. A slow one holds your resources and takes everything else down with it.

So the rule is absolute. Every outbound call gets an explicit timeout, chosen by you, and shorter than the timeout of whoever calls you. Otherwise you are simply passing the hang upstream.

Attack 3 in full — the one that teaches most

// a dependency that accepts the connection and never replies
const blackhole = require('node:net').createServer(() => {});
blackhole.listen(9999);

// point the spine at it, then load-test a route that does NOT use it
autocannon -c 50 -d 30 http://localhost:3000/health

Predict the result before you run it. Most people expect /health to be unaffected, because it does not touch the dependency at all. Find out whether that is true for your service.

Why a hang is worse than a crash A crashed dependency gives you ECONNREFUSED in a millisecond. You catch it, return a 503, and the request slot is free again immediately. A hanging one gives you nothing — no error, no log line, no metric moving. The request is simply still open, and so is the next one, and the one after that. The service looks healthy right up to the moment it has no capacity left.

Timeouts have to be a budget, not a number

Say a client waits 10 seconds. Call three services with a 10-second timeout each and you can take 30 seconds to fail. The client left twenty seconds ago and you are still working for nobody.

So timeouts are chosen from the outside in:

LayerBudgetReason
Client10 sGiven. You do not control it.
Your handler, end to end8 sLeave room to send an answer.
One database query2 sFits with a retry inside the budget.
One upstream call3 sSame.

The numbers matter less than the direction. Each layer must be shorter than the one outside it. Otherwise a timeout deeper in the stack can never fire, because the outer one always wins first. You then lose the specific error that would have named the slow dependency.

Say it in the simplest words — the Feynman check

1 · In plain words Your service depends on other things. If one of them stops answering, and you never said how long you are willing to wait, you wait forever.

Then the next request waits too, and the next. Soon everything you have is tied up waiting for one thing, including work that had nothing to do with it.

2 · One analogy A restaurant with ten waiters. One table's order never comes back from the kitchen. If a waiter stands there waiting, that is nine waiters left. Ten stuck tables and the restaurant has stopped, even though nine kitchens are fine.

…and where the analogy breaks A waiter would notice after a few minutes and go and ask. Your code has no such instinct. It waits exactly as long as you told it to. If you told it nothing, that means until the network gives up, which can be many minutes.

The other break is worse. In a restaurant the stuck tables are visible, and somebody raises the alarm. Here there is no error and no log line. Nothing moves on your dashboard until capacity runs out completely. The failure is silent right up to the point where it is total.

3 · The sticking point People prepare for dependencies that break, and are surprised by ones that go slow. Breaking is the easy case. ECONNREFUSED arrives in a millisecond, you return a 503, and the slot is free.

Slow gives you nothing to catch. That is why “we handle errors from that service” is not the same as “we are protected from that service”. Only a timeout protects you, because only a timeout turns silence into an error you can handle.

4 · Now you explain it back

In two or three sentences, explain why a slow dependency is more dangerous than a broken one. Send it to me and I will tell you where the gap is. Do not skip this. Reading the block above feels like understanding. Saying it in your own words is the part that proves it.

Build it for real — the five attacks and the runbook

Requirements:

  1. An explicit timeout on every outbound call. Grep your own code and prove there are none left.
  2. A timeout budget written down: client, handler, each dependency. Each shorter than the one outside it.
  3. All five attacks run, with what happened recorded for each.
  4. The worst finding fixed. Only one — the others go in the runbook as known risks.
  5. A one-page RUNBOOK.md: what breaks, how you would know, what you do.
  6. A baseline p50, p95 and p99 for the whole spine, saved with today's date.

The runbook shape — three columns, nothing else

| What breaks          | How I would know          | What I do                    |
|----------------------|---------------------------|------------------------------|
| Database hangs       | pool wait time > 1s        | timeout fires, 503, alert     |
| Memory climbs        | RSS trend over 1h         | heap snapshot (week 32)       |
| Deploy drops traffic | 5xx spike during rollout  | check readiness delay (w27)   |
Requirement 4 says fix ONE thing, and that is deliberate You will find five problems. The instinct is to fix all five, and it is wrong at this stage. An unfixed risk you have written down is safer than an unknown one, and a service with one tested fix beats one with five untested ones. Deciding a known problem is not worth fixing yet is a real engineering judgement — and it is the one thing this course says should never be delegated.
What to hand in — and keep RUNBOOK.md, and your baseline numbers with today's date in the filename. Week 34 compares its memory against today. Week 50 compares NestJS against today's p99. If you do not save these numbers, both of those weeks lose their point.
Scenario

During an incident, a service returns 200 for its health check and times out on everything else. CPU is idle, memory is flat, and the database reports normal load with very few active queries.

Say what “very few active queries” tells you, name the most likely cause, and give the one measurement that would confirm it.

Say it like an interviewer just asked

What this lesson left out on purpose Circuit breakers are the right next step after timeouts, and this course only points at them. Bulkheads — separate connection pools per dependency, so one cannot starve the others — solve exactly the failure in the diagram and are worth reading about. Chaos engineering is this week's five attacks, done continuously and automatically. Alerting is a tooling subject; the runbook column “how I would know” is the part that matters.

Remember this · the 20-second recap
  • Every wait is unbounded until you bound it. Every outbound call needs an explicit timeout.
  • A slow dependency is more dangerous than a broken one. Broken fails in a millisecond; slow holds your resources.
  • One hanging dependency empties the pool, and then unrelated routes fail too.
  • Timeouts are a budget, chosen outside in. Each layer shorter than the one around it.
  • Run all five attacks. Predict each result first — the prediction is where the learning is.
  • Fix one thing. Write the rest down. A known risk is safer than an unknown one.
