Build — find and fix a real leak, and write it up

The write-up is the deliverable. An engineer who can explain a fix beats one who made it silently.

Your win today: a one-page report with real numbers, a hypothesis you got wrong, and how you ruled it out. That last part is the bit interviewers actually listen for.

The new idea is the write-up. Everything so far has been about finding things. This week is about making the finding useful to somebody else — including yourself in six months.

Start from the problem — a fix nobody understands is a fix nobody trusts

You find a leak and change one line. Three weeks later memory climbs again. Now what?

If all that exists is the commit, you start from zero. Now suppose a page exists. It says what the symptom was, what you measured, what you ruled out, and why the fix works. You know in two minutes whether this is the same bug.

Why the write-up is the deliverable

The fix helps once. The write-up helps every time it happens again, to anyone — including the you who has forgotten all of this.

And there is a second reason, which matters for the mission. Every interview asks some form of “tell me about a hard bug”. The people who answer that well are not the ones who fixed more bugs. They are the ones who wrote down how they found them.

The method — five steps, and the third is the one people skip

StepQuestionOutput
1 · SymptomWhat did you actually observe?A number over time. Not “it felt slow”.
2 · HypothesisWhat do you think it is, before looking?A written guess you can be wrong about.
3 · Rule outWhat would prove this hypothesis false?The test, and its result.
4 · EvidenceWhat actually shows the cause?A retainer path or a profile.
5 · ProofIs it fixed?The same measurement as step 1, after.
Step 3 is the whole difference between debugging and guessing Most people look for evidence that confirms their guess, and they usually find some — a chart that fits, a suspicious-looking function. Then they change it, memory looks better for an hour, and the bug comes back next week.

Ask the opposite question. “What would I see if I were wrong?” Then go and look for that. A hypothesis you cannot imagine disproving is not a hypothesis; it is a preference.

Measuring over an hour, not a minute

A leak that matters is slow. You cannot see it in a thirty-second load test. The measurement has to run long enough for the trend to separate from the noise.

// sample.js — attach this to the spine and leave it running
const v8 = require('node:v8');
const fs = require('node:fs');

setInterval(() => {
  const s = v8.getHeapStatistics();
  fs.appendFileSync('heap.csv',
    `${Date.now()},${s.used_heap_size},${s.heap_size_limit}\n`);
}, 10_000).unref();       // every 10s — unref so it never holds the process open

Run the spine under sustained load for an hour, then look at the shape of the line. There are only three shapes, and each means something different:

ShapeMeans
Sawtooth, flat averageHealthy. The collector is doing its job.
Rises then plateausHealthy. Caches and pools filling to their steady size.
Sawtooth with a rising floorA leak. Each collection frees less than the last.

The floor is the signal, not the peaks. The peaks move with traffic. The floor is what survived a collection, and a floor that climbs is memory you are never getting back.

Say it in the simplest words — the Feynman check

1 · In plain words Finding the bug is only half the job. The other half is being able to say how you found it. Then somebody else, or you later, can follow the same path.

The most valuable part of that story is not the answer. It is the wrong guess you had first, and how you found out it was wrong.

2 · One analogy A doctor's notes. They do not only write down the diagnosis. They write what the patient reported, what they suspected, and what they ruled out. The conclusion comes last.

…and where the analogy breaks A doctor has a patient who can say “it hurts here”. Your program cannot. Every symptom you get is one you decided to measure in advance.

So there is a step with no medical equivalent: choosing what to record before you know what is wrong. If you were not already logging used heap every ten seconds, the last hour's shape does not exist. It cannot be recovered. Half of debugging production is a decision you made weeks earlier about what to write down.

3 · The sticking point People look for evidence that their guess is right, and they nearly always find some. A chart that fits, a function that looks suspicious.

So you change something that was not the cause. Memory improves for an hour by coincidence. Next week the same bug returns, with your confidence intact and your notes empty. One question fixes this. “What would I see if I were wrong?” Then go and look for that.

4 · Now you explain it back

In two or three sentences, explain why a hypothesis you can disprove beats evidence that merely fits. Send it to me and I will tell you where the gap is. Do not skip this. Reading the block above feels like understanding. Saying it in your own words is the part that proves it.

Build it for real — an hour under load, then one page

Requirements:

  1. Run the spine under sustained load for at least one hour, sampling used heap every 10 seconds.
  2. Plot it. Decide from the floor whether it leaks. Say so either way.
  3. Write a hypothesis before taking any snapshot.
  4. Write the test that would prove your hypothesis false. Run it. Record the result honestly.
  5. Find the real cause from a snapshot comparison. Record the retainer path.
  6. Fix it. Re-run the full hour. Compare the two floors.
  7. Write REPORT.md — one page, the six headings below.
  8. Say the whole story out loud in three minutes, from the report only.

The report — one page, no more

# Memory investigation — 2026-XX-XX

## Symptom
Used heap floor rose from 48 MB to 91 MB over 60 minutes under 20 rps.
Peaks were normal. The floor was the signal.

## Hypothesis
The in-memory link store grows without limit. (WRONG — see below.)

## What ruled it out
Store size was logged alongside heap: it reached 4,200 entries
and stopped, while the heap kept climbing. Steady store, rising heap
means it is not the store.

## Evidence
Snapshot comparison, sorted by delta:
  Object +38,441   retainer path:
  Object → in listeners of EventEmitter → in server of Module
A SIGTERM listener was being added per request and never removed.

## Fix
Registered the shutdown handler once at start-up instead of
inside the request handler. One line moved.

## Proof
Same hour, same load. Floor: 48 MB → 49 MB. Flat.
Node's MaxListenersExceededWarning also stopped appearing.
If your service does not leak Good. Say that, with the graph, and then plant one deliberately and find it. The skill being practised is the method, not the bug. A report that says “no leak found, here is the evidence, and here is the same method applied to one I planted” is a completely valid deliverable.
Requirement 8 is not optional, and it is the one you will skip Saying it out loud in three minutes is a different skill from writing it down, and it is the one the interview actually tests. If you cannot tell the story without reading, the report is too long or you do not understand the fix. Both are worth finding out now rather than in the room.
Scenario

You present this report. A senior engineer says: “The floor rose 43 MB in an hour. You only proved the fix over one more hour. How do you know it is fixed and not just slower?”

Give the honest answer, then say what evidence would settle it — and what you would do if you could not get that evidence before shipping.

Say it like an interviewer just asked

What this lesson left out on purpose Continuous profiling in production — always-on, low-overhead sampling — is what mature teams use instead of ad-hoc investigations, and it is a tooling choice. Blameless post-mortems are this report's bigger sibling, for incidents rather than bugs. Automated memory regression tests in CI are possible and noisy; worth knowing they exist.

Remember this · the 20-second recap
  • The write-up is the deliverable. The fix helps once; the report helps every time it recurs.
  • Five steps: symptom, hypothesis, rule out, evidence, proof.
  • Step 3 is the difference between debugging and guessing: what would I see if I were wrong?
  • A real leak is slow. Measure for an hour, not thirty seconds.
  • Watch the floor, not the peaks. Peaks move with traffic; the floor is what survived a collection.
  • Report numbers, not adjectives. Before and after, same load, same duration.
  • Include the hypothesis you got wrong — it is the most convincing part of the story. Then tell it out loud in three minutes; if you cannot, you do not understand it yet.

If you remember one thing: look for what would prove you wrong. Evidence that fits your guess is always available.
