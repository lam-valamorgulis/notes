Signals and graceful shutdown

The line between a script and a service. A deploy must not drop a single request.

coreWeek 27 of 52. This lesson builds one domain-expert ability: debug a problem that has no ready answer online. Week 27 in the plan · Glossary

Your win today: the spine survives a deploy with zero failed requests. You will also find the reason most “graceful shutdown” code takes 3.5 seconds instead of 0.5 — and fix it in one line.
Where you are Weeks 21–25 made the spine work and made it fast. This module makes it survive. Every deploy, every scale-down and every crash is a shutdown, and most services do it badly without anyone noticing.

Start from the problem — somebody is about to stop your process

A deploy, an autoscaler, a docker stop. All of them do the same thing: send your process a signal, wait a bit, then kill it.

At that moment your server is in the middle of something. Maybe forty requests are in flight. What happens to them is entirely up to you, and doing nothing means dropping all forty.

Two signals, and only one of them is a conversation

SignalMeansCan you handle it?
SIGTERM“Please stop.” The polite one. What every orchestrator sends first.Yes. This is your whole opportunity.
SIGINTCtrl+C in a terminal.Yes. Handle it the same way.
SIGKILL“You are stopped.” The kernel ends you.No. Not ever.

That last row is not a convention you could argue with. Try to listen for it:

process.on('SIGKILL', () => {});
// throws: EINVAL

Verified on Node v24.14.0. The operating system will not deliver SIGKILL to your program at all, so there is nothing to hook.

The shape of every shutdown

An orchestrator sends SIGTERM, waits a grace period — 30 seconds by default in Kubernetes — and then sends SIGKILL. Everything you do happens inside that window, or it does not happen.

The four steps, in this order

  1. Stop accepting new work. server.close(). Existing connections are untouched.
  2. Tell the load balancer. Make the readiness check fail, so no more traffic is routed here.
  3. Let in-flight work finish. Wait, but not forever.
  4. Close what you own — database pool, queue connections — then exit.

The order matters. Close the database first and every in-flight request fails at the last moment, which is worse than never accepting them.

The three seconds nobody expects

Here is a shutdown that looks correct. A request takes 500 ms; we call close() at 50 ms and wait for its callback.

The real output (Node v24.14.0):

request finished at        533 ms
shutdown complete at      3540 ms   ← three seconds of nothing

The work was done at 533 ms. The process sat there for another three seconds. Nothing was happening. Why?

Because server.close() waits for every connection to end, not every request. HTTP keep-alive means the client holds its socket open in case it wants to send another request. That socket is idle, doing nothing, and it is holding your shutdown hostage until it times out.

server.keepAliveTimeout   // 5000 — five seconds, by default
The one-line fix server.closeIdleConnections() ends the sockets that have no request on them, leaving the busy ones alone. Call it repeatedly while draining: 
server.close(onDone);
const t = setInterval(() => server.closeIdleConnections(), 50);
t.unref();
Measured: 3540 ms becomes 538 ms. Same requests, same result, six times faster — and inside a 30-second grace period that difference is the margin you have when something goes wrong.
Why this reaches production and stays there Nothing fails. The deploy works, the requests complete, the logs are clean. You simply pay three seconds on every rolling restart of every pod, forever. It is invisible until you are deploying fifty pods and wondering why a rollout takes twenty minutes.

Liveness and readiness are different questions

CheckAsksIf it failsDuring shutdown
/health — livenessIs this process alive?You are killed and restarted.Keep returning 200. You are alive.
/ready — readinessShould traffic come here?You are removed from the pool.Return 503 immediately.
Getting these the wrong way round is a real outage Fail liveness during shutdown and the orchestrator concludes the process is broken, so it SIGKILLs you in the middle of draining — the exact thing you were trying to avoid.

And there is a delay you cannot skip. A load balancer notices a failed readiness check on its own schedule, often several seconds. So fail readiness first, then wait a few seconds before calling close(). Otherwise requests are still being routed to a server that has stopped accepting them.

Say it in the simplest words — the Feynman check

1 · In plain words Somebody taps you on the shoulder and says the building closes in thirty seconds. You do not drop what you are holding and run.

You stop letting new people in. You finish serving whoever is at your desk, lock your drawers, and then leave. If you take too long, you are carried out anyway.

2 · One analogy A shop at closing time. The door is locked so nobody new comes in, but the customers already inside are served properly. The alternative is turning off the lights with people still at the till.

…and where the analogy breaks In a shop you can see whether anyone is left. Your server cannot tell a customer still being served from one just standing near the door.

That is the keep-alive socket, and it is why a correct-looking shutdown took 3540 ms instead of 538 ms. Nobody was being served for three of those seconds. The server was waiting for someone who had finished but not walked out. The browser keeps the connection, in case it wants something else.

3 · The sticking point People think server.close() means “wait for the requests to finish”. It means “wait for the connections to finish”, and those are not the same thing.

A request lasts milliseconds. A connection lingers for keepAliveTimeout, five seconds by default, doing nothing at all. So the difference is invisible in any test with one request and no reuse. It costs three seconds on every restart of every instance in production.

4 · Now you explain it back

In two or three sentences, explain why server.close() can take seconds after the last request finished. Send it to me and I will tell you where the gap is. Do not skip this. Reading the block above feels like understanding. Saying it in your own words is the part that proves it.

Build it for real — shut the spine down under load

The shutdown, in full

let shuttingDown = false;

async function shutdown(signal) {
  if (shuttingDown) return;                // a second SIGTERM must not restart this
  shuttingDown = true;
  console.error(`${signal} received, draining`);

  ready = false;                           // 1 · /ready starts returning 503
  await sleep(5000);                       // 2 · let the load balancer notice

  server.close(() => console.error('http closed'));   // 3 · no new connections
  const idle = setInterval(() => server.closeIdleConnections(), 50);
  idle.unref();                            // the fix — do not wait on empty sockets

  const hard = setTimeout(() => {          // 4 · never hang forever
    console.error('drain timed out — forcing exit');
    process.exit(1);
  }, 20000);
  hard.unref();

  await once(server, 'close');
  await pool.end();                        // 5 · close what you own, LAST
  clearTimeout(hard);
}

for (const sig of ['SIGTERM', 'SIGINT']) process.on(sig, () => shutdown(sig));

Requirements:

  1. Separate /health (stays 200) and /ready (goes 503 first).
  2. The idle-connection sweep. Measure shutdown time with it and without.
  3. A hard timeout, so a stuck request cannot hold you past the grace period.
  4. Repeated signals are ignored. A second Ctrl+C must not restart the sequence.
  5. Zero failed requests when SIGTERM arrives mid-load.

autocannon -c 20 -d 20 http://localhost:3000/health &

Remember this · the 20-second recap
  • SIGTERM is a request you can handle. SIGKILL cannot be caught — process.on('SIGKILL') throws EINVAL.
  • Order: fail readiness, wait for the load balancer, close(), drain, then close what you own.
  • Liveness stays 200 during shutdown. Fail it and you are killed mid-drain.
  • server.close() waits for connections, not requests. Idle keep-alive sockets hold it open.
  • closeIdleConnections() on a timer fixes it. Measured: 3540 ms → 538 ms.
  • Always add a hard timeout. A stuck request must not hold you past the grace period.
  • Guard against a second signal, or Ctrl+C twice restarts the whole sequence.

If you remember one thing: close() waits for connections, not requests — and an idle socket is still a connection.
