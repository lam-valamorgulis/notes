Week 51 of 52 · Lesson 51 · The interview

System design, for Node specifically

General system design is everywhere. The Node judgement is rare, and it is what gets you hired.

coreWeek 51 of 52. This lesson builds one domain-expert ability: replace an adjective with a number, out loud, in front of someone. Week 51 in the plan · Glossary

Your win today: “a million notifications an hour” turns out to be 278 per second, which one Node process can do. Knowing that in the first minute changes the whole conversation.
Where you are Fifty weeks of building. This week is about saying it — designing a system you have not built, in 45 minutes, at a whiteboard, and defending every box.

Start from the problem — the first move is always arithmetic

Most candidates start drawing boxes. Boxes are cheap and they hide the thing that decides the design.

The first thing to say, every time

Convert the requirement into a rate. Everything after that is a consequence, and until you have done it you are guessing.

1,000,000 per hour ÷ 3,600 seconds = 278 per second

Say that number out loud. It reframes the entire problem, because 278 per second is not a lot. A single Node process serving a redirect did 51,797 per second in week 50.

Always ask for the peak Traffic is never flat. Assume 3x unless told otherwise, and say you are assuming it: 278 × 3 = 833 per second. Designing for the average is how systems fall over at 9am on a Monday, and naming the multiplier is what shows you know it.

How many workers? It depends on one number you must ask for

How long does one send take? Until you know, you cannot answer. Work it through:

Four hundred workers sounds like a serious system. It is also wrong, and this is the specifically-Node part of the answer.

That table assumes one send at a time. It is I/O. A send is waiting on somebody else's server. Everything since week 1 says one Node process handles hundreds of those at once. So the right question is not “how many workers” but “how many in flight per process”.

Fifty in flight per process turns 417 workers into two boxes and a spare. That is the answer, and it comes straight from the bounded-concurrency limiter you wrote in week 8.

And now name the limit's real purpose You are not choosing 50 to go fast. You are choosing it because it is bounded. Unbounded, a backlog of 100,000 jobs starts 100,000 requests at once and takes down the provider, your memory, or both. The number is a promise about your maximum load on somebody else.

Now find the constraint that is not yours

Your workers are two boxes. So what actually limits this system?

The provider's rate limit

provider allows      100/s = 360,000/hour
you need           1,000,000/hour
you are 2.8x over the limit

This is the design. Everything else is detail. You need a higher tier, several accounts, or a queue spreading the work over more than an hour. Which of those it is is a business question, so ask it.

Retries, which multiply the rate

Failure rate, 3 attemptsExtra sends/hourActual rate
1%20,000283/s (1.02x)

Retries are worst exactly when you can least afford them A 20% failure rate means the provider is already struggling — and your retries add 40% more load to the thing that is failing. That is a feedback loop, and it is how a small incident becomes an outage.

So a retry policy needs jittered backoff from week 8, and a maximum from week 42. Ideally also a circuit breaker that stops sending once the failure rate crosses a line.

Storage, which nobody costs

300 bytes per row × 1,000,000/hour
= 300 MB/hour = 7.2 GB/day = 2.63 TB/year

Two and a half terabytes a year for a notification log. Now the question is real: how long must you keep it, and why? That is a conversation with a number in it, which is a different conversation from “we will log the sends”.

Say where Node is the wrong choice — in your own design

This is the question that separates candidates, and most people dodge it. Do not. Point at your own diagram:

Part of the systemNode?Why
The API accepting notification requestsYesI/O bound. This is exactly what it is for.
The workers calling the providerYesHundreds of waits per process. Measured: 50 in flight → 1.1 processes.
Rendering 1M rich templates with imagesNoCPU bound. One thread. This blocks everything.
Aggregating a year of delivery dataNoThat is a query, not a service. Let the database do it.

Then say what you would actually do about it. It is not “rewrite in Go”. From week 23: the template rendering goes to a worker pool, or a separate service. You keep Node for the ninety percent that is waiting.

The sentence that lands “Node is right for this system and wrong for this box in it, so that box gets a worker pool and everything else stays.” Naming a weakness in your own design and then containing it is what judgement sounds like.
Say it in the simplest words — the Feynman check

1 · In plain words A big-sounding requirement usually is not big. Turn it into a rate before you draw anything: a million an hour is 278 a second.

Then the design almost writes itself, and the real bottleneck shows up immediately. Here it was not Node at all. The provider only allows 100 a second, so the system is 2.8 times over somebody else's limit.

2 · One analogy Working out how many tills a shop needs. You cannot answer from “a lot of customers”. You need customers per hour and minutes per customer, and then it is division.

…and where the analogy breaks A till serves one person at a time. A Node process does not. It is waiting on someone else's server, so it can hold hundreds of sends in flight.

That is the whole difference, and it changes the answer by two orders of magnitude. One send at a time gives 417 workers at peak. Fifty in flight per process gives 3.3. Size a Node system as if each process handled one thing at a time, and you overshoot badly. A hundred times too big, and you never notice.

3 · The sticking point People pick a concurrency limit to go fast. That is backwards. The limit exists because it is bounded: without it, a backlog of 100,000 jobs starts 100,000 requests at once.

The same thinking applies to retries, which most designs never cost. At a 20% failure rate, three attempts add 40% more load — to the provider that is already failing. Your resilience feature is a feedback loop, and it is loudest at the worst moment.

4 · Now you explain it back

In three sentences, explain why “a million an hour” needs two Node processes rather than four hundred. Send it to me and I will tell you where the gap is. Do not skip this. Reading the block above feels like understanding. Saying it in your own words is the part that proves it.

Build it for real — design it on paper, then defend it

Requirements:

  1. Do the arithmetic first, before any box. Rate, peak, time per send, in-flight per process, processes.
  2. Write down every number you had to assume, and mark it. In an interview, say each one out loud as you use it.
  3. Cost the retries and the storage. Both are usually missing, and both change the design.
  4. Find the constraint that is not yours — a provider limit, a quota, a contract. Name it as the real problem.
  5. Name every failure mode. The provider is down. The provider is slow. The queue backs up. A worker dies mid-send. The same job runs twice.
  6. For each one, say what the user sees. “It retries” is not an answer — say what happens on attempt four.
  7. Point at one box where Node is wrong, and say what you would do about it without rewriting the system.
  8. Then do it out loud in 45 minutes, recorded. Watch it back. Count the adjectives you used instead of numbers.
The drill — 45 minutes, whiteboard, out loud

“Design a service that sends 1 million notifications an hour.”

Spend the first five minutes on arithmetic and nothing else. Say the numbers as you compute them. If you have not said a number by minute three, you are drawing boxes and you have already lost the conversation.

Say it like an interviewer just asked

What this lesson left out on purpose General distributed systems design — sharding, consistency, leader election, CAP — is a large field with better books than a single lesson. Cost modelling in money, not just machines, is what senior conversations actually turn on. Multi-region changes almost every answer here. Scheduling and delivery windows matter for notifications specifically, since “within the hour” and “at 9am local time” are very different systems.

Remember this · the 20-second recap
  • Convert to a rate before drawing anything. A million an hour is 278/s, and 833/s at a 3x peak.
  • Sizing per worker gives 417 at peak. Sizing per in-flight request gives 3.3 — because a send is waiting, not computing.
  • The concurrency limit is for boundedness, not speed. Unbounded, a 100,000-job backlog starts 100,000 requests.
  • Find the constraint that is not yours: the provider allows 100/s, so you are 2.8x over. That is the design.
  • Cost the retries. At a 20% failure rate, three attempts add 40% more load to the thing already failing.
  • Cost the storage. 300 bytes × 1M/hour = 2.63 TB/year, which turns retention into a real question.
  • Name one box in your own design where Node is wrong. Then contain it with a worker pool, not a rewrite.

If you remember one thing: no number said by minute three means you are drawing boxes, not designing.
