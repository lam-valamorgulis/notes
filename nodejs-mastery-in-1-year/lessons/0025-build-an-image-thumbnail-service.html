This week assembles weeks 22 to 24 — and adds one idea Measuring the block from week 22. The pool from week 23. Every core from week 24.

The new idea is admission control: what a server should do when it is already full. Weeks 22–24 make work go faster. This week is about what to do when it cannot.

Start from the problem — a queue is not free

Your pool has 9 workers. Each resize takes 300 ms. A hundred requests arrive at once.

The first 9 start immediately. The other 91 wait. Work out what the last one experiences:

91 waiting ÷ 9 workers ≈ 10 rounds × 300 ms ≈ 3 seconds of queueing

Three seconds is longer than most clients will wait. So that request will time out — after your server has already spent 300 ms of CPU on it. You did the work and threw it away, and meanwhile everyone behind it waited too.

The idea this week adds

An unbounded queue does not protect you. It converts a throughput problem into a latency problem and hides it until everything times out at once.

So the pool needs a second limit: not just how many run at once, but how many may wait. Past that, refuse immediately with 503. A fast refusal is a better answer than a slow success nobody is still listening for.

Why the health check must not use the pool If /health queues behind image jobs, then under load it goes slow, your orchestrator decides the container is unhealthy, and it kills a server that was working perfectly. Then the traffic moves to the remaining ones, which are now more loaded, and they fail too.

A liveness check must answer from the main thread and touch nothing. That is the single most valuable line in this week's build, and it is one line.

Say it in the simplest words — the Feynman check

1 · In plain words A shop can serve nine customers at a time. A hundred arrive. You could let them all queue, and the last one waits three minutes and leaves before being served.

You did the work for people who had already gone, and everyone else waited longer because of it. Better to say “we are full, come back in a minute” at the door.

2 · One analogy A restaurant that stops taking bookings when the kitchen is full. It is not being unhelpful. Every extra booking makes the food later for people already sitting down.

…and where the analogy breaks A restaurant queue is visible. People see it and decide for themselves. Your queue is invisible — the client sees nothing at all, just a request that has not answered yet.

Worse, the client usually gives up on its own timer and retries. So a full server gets more traffic, not less, exactly when it can least take it. That is why the refusal must be explicit and fast, and why it should carry Retry-After. Silence is read as “try again”.

3 · The sticking point People believe a queue protects the server. It protects the workers from being overloaded. It does not protect the user, and it does not protect the machine.

An unbounded queue turns a throughput problem into a latency problem, and hides it. Everything looks fine, every worker is busy, no error appears. Then every request times out at once. The queue has to have a length, and hitting it has to mean something.

4 · Now you explain it back

In two or three sentences, explain why an unlimited queue makes an overloaded service worse, not better. Send it to me and I will tell you where the gap is. Do not skip this. Reading the block above feels like understanding. Saying it in your own words is the part that proves it.

Build it for real — thumbs

Upload an image, get three sizes back. Use sharp, which is genuinely CPU-heavy — that is the point.

Requirements:

  1. POST /thumbs takes an image and returns three sizes. Resizing runs in the week 23 pool.
  2. A 10 MB upload limit, refused with 413 before the bytes are held (week 20).
  3. A queue depth of 2 × pool size. Past that, 503 with a Retry-After header.
  4. GET /health answers from the main thread and never touches the pool.
  5. Clustered across all cores (week 24), with dead workers replaced.
  6. Transfer the image buffer to the worker rather than copying it. Measure whether it helped.

The measurement — this is the deliverable

Review this pull request

A teammate ships this. It passes review from two people and works perfectly in staging.

app.post('/thumbs', async (req, res) => {
  const buf = await readWholeBody(req);
  const sizes = await Promise.all([
    pool.run({ buf, w: 100 }),
    pool.run({ buf, w: 400 }),
    pool.run({ buf, w: 800 }),
  ]);
  res.json(sizes);
});
Name four things wrong with it, ordered by how much damage each does in production. Say them out loud before you read on. This is the week's real test of the fourth expert ability.
One hint, because the first fault is the least obvious Count how many times the image is copied. buf goes into three pool.run calls, and week 23 measured a copy at 28 ms per 50 MB. Then ask what the queue depth just became for a single user request.

Remember this · the 20-second recap
  • An unbounded queue turns a throughput problem into a latency problem, and hides it until everything times out at once.
  • Three limits: pool size (CPU), queue depth (wasted work), upload size (memory).
  • Past the queue depth, return 503 with Retry-After. Fast refusal beats slow success.
  • Silence is read as “retry”, so a full server gets more traffic. Refuse explicitly.
  • The health check must never queue. If it does, the orchestrator kills a healthy server.
  • Transfer the image buffer instead of copying it — 28 ms against 2 ms per 50 MB.
  • The four-row table is the artefact. /health is the column that matters.

If you remember one thing: a queue protects your workers, not your users. So it needs a length, and hitting it must mean something.
