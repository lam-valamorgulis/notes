The core — the 20% that carries the rest

Node is a big surface and most of it does not matter to a backend engineer. Twelve ideas do. Everything on this page is either one of those twelve, or it is depth — real, useful, and waiting until the twelve are solid. The rule is simple: no depth week starts while a core week is untaught.

12 of 12 core items fully taught · 48 of 52 weeks written · every lesson is written. The remaining four are review weeks, which carry no lesson by design.

  1. The event loop, and the one rule — one thread runs your JavaScript, and blocking it stops every other request. Weeks 1–4, 22 · taught
  2. The async model end to end — promises, async/await, and how an error travels through them. Weeks 5–6 · taught
  3. Bounded concurrency — N at a time, with a timeout and a retry. Unbounded Promise.all is how a service kills itself. Weeks 7–8 · taught
  4. The module system — CommonJS versus ESM, how Node resolves a path, and the process APIs a real tool needs. Weeks 9–12 · taught
  5. Streams and backpressure — moving data larger than memory without filling it. Weeks 14–17 · taught
  6. EventEmitter, and the server built on it — learn the emitter once and streams, sockets and http all stop being magic. Weeks 18, 20, 21 · taught
  7. Beyond one thread — worker threads for CPU work, cluster for every core. Weeks 22–25 · taught
  8. Process lifecycle — signals, graceful shutdown, and operational versus programmer errors. Weeks 27–30
  9. Measure before you fix — CPU profiles and heap snapshots. No claim without a number. Weeks 31, 32, 34 written · week 33 is depth
  10. Evidence — tests that do not flake, and input you hardened — node:test, mocking, coverage you can defend, and the boundary checks that stop a JSON body hurting you. Both halves answer how do you know? Weeks 35–38 · taught
  11. The data layer — a pool, a transaction, a cache, and a queue. Weeks 40–43 · taught
  12. TypeScript, then NestJS — the shape professional Node teams hire for. Weeks 44–50
Why 43 of the 52 weeks are core The 20% is twelve ideas, not twelve weeks. Node's full surface is far bigger than this course; the twelve above are the part a backend engineer touches again and again, and the year spends 43 of its 52 weeks going deep on just those twelve. That is the 80/20 rule working, not the 80/20 rule broken — a course that spent only a fifth of its weeks on the core would be the wide, shallow tour the rule exists to prevent. Every core week is listed against its item in MISSION.md and in the coverage table, so the claim can be checked instead of believed. Week 51 is the one exception: system design belongs to no single item, because it is where all twelve get used at once.
What is queued as depth Five weeks are tagged depth on purpose, and the plan says why on each one: week 11 publishing a package, week 19 raw TCP with net, week 29 observability and diagnostics_channel, week 33 micro-optimisation, and week 45 the TypeScript build setup. All five are real, and all five are now written. None of them is what a Node backend engineer touches weekly, so if you are short of time, do the core weeks first. The plan page has a "Show core only" button that hides them.

The big picture — how it all connects

Everything grows from one idea: Node.js is one thread that never waits. Two libraries make that true — V8 does the thinking, libuv does the waiting. Once you accept that, every other Node fact follows: why the event loop has phases, why a try/catch can miss an error, and why one slow for loop can take down a whole server.

You learn it by building, not reading. Every week ends in something that runs. Early weeks make small sharp tools — a server that proves blocking with numbers, an aggregator that survives a flaky network, a command-line tool you publish. Then, at week 21, the course changes shape: one API becomes the spine, and you keep the same service for the rest of the year. You give it a worker pool, spread it across every CPU core, make it survive a deploy without dropping a request, hunt a real memory leak in it, test and attack it, give it a real database, cache and queue, migrate it to TypeScript, and finally rebuild it properly in NestJS. Twelve toys teach less than one real system carried the whole way.

Four times a year — week 13, 26, 39 and 52 — there is no new material at all. You rebuild things from memory, answer questions shuffled across every earlier week, and sit a mock interview. That is not filler. Reading again feels like learning and mostly is not; pulling something back out of your own head is what makes it stay. The year ends when you can say all of it out loud, cold, with no notes — not when you have read all of it.

And if a real interview appears before the year is out, the 7-Day Node.js Interview Sprint is the short path. It is a separate sub-course: seven days, 21 lessons, four katas on a whiteboard, and a 50-question mock on the last day. It teaches nothing new — it turns what you already know into what you can say under pressure.
