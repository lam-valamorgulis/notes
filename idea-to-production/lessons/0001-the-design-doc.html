The design doc

The cheapest place to be wrong is a sentence. Be wrong there first.

Your win this week. A file called docs/0001-speck.md exists. It says what Speck is, what Speck refuses to do, and one design you rejected. One other human has argued with it. That file is the artefact for weeks 1–2, and half of Gate 1.

Domain expertise this moves forward: "Say why it was built this way, and what that choice gives up." A design doc is the only artefact whose whole job is to record the trade-off. Without it, in a year, you will remember the choice and not the reason. Tag: core — core item 1 of 12.

Why this has to be true

Start with something you already accept: changing code costs time.

Now ask a sharper question. Does every change cost the same? No. Renaming a variable costs a minute. Changing what a table means costs a month.

What makes the difference? Not the size of the edit. It is how much finished work depends on the thing you are changing.

A sentence in a document has nothing depending on it. Delete it, write a new one. A column in a live database has every query depending on it, plus every screen, plus every backup, plus the rows already sitting in it. You cannot delete it. You have to move an entire building.

So the cost of a wrong decision is not fixed. It grows as work piles on top of it. And work piles on top of it from the moment you start building.

The whole argument in one line

The cost of being wrong grows with time. So the cheapest moment to find out is the earliest moment.

But you cannot find out alone

Here is the step people skip. Being early is not enough. You also have to actually discover the mistake, and a mistake in your head does not announce itself. In your head, every plan is coherent. That is what a plan is — the version of the idea with the holes smoothed over.

A mistake becomes visible when the idea meets resistance. There are three kinds of resistance available, and they cost very different amounts:

ResistanceWhat it costsWhat it catches
Building the thingWeeksEverything, eventually, expensively
Another person's opinionAn hour of theirsWhat they happen to notice
Writing it in sentencesAn afternoonEvery part you were vague about

Writing is the cheapest resistance there is, and it works even with nobody else in the room. The reason is mechanical, not mystical. A vague thought can stay vague. A vague sentence looks vague on the page. When you write "the widget will handle spam somehow", you can see the word somehow.

So the derivation lands here:

  1. The cost of being wrong grows with the work built on top.
  2. Therefore find out early.
  3. You only find out when the idea meets resistance.
  4. Writing is the cheapest resistance, and a second reader is the next cheapest.
  5. Therefore: write the design down, in a form another person can attack, before you build it.

What must be in it, and why

Step 5 has a word doing all the work: attack. A document you cannot disagree with is not resistance. It is a to-do list with headings.

So the sections are not decoration. Each one exists to make a specific argument possible now instead of later.

SectionThe argument it lets someone have
Context and scope"You have misunderstood the situation."
Goals"That is not what success looks like."
Non-goals"You cannot leave that out." — or, more often, relief that you are leaving it out.
The design"That will not work, and here is where."
Alternatives considered"You rejected the right one."
Cross-cutting concerns"This leaks customer data."

Verified: the six section names and their purpose come from Design Docs at Google (Malte Ubl). The "argument it lets someone have" column is ours — the source lists the sections, it does not frame them this way.

Cover the table and answer Two of those six sections are the ones people leave out. Which two, and what argument becomes impossible when they are missing?

The parts that are convention, not logic

Be honest about which parts of this are derived and which are just what a big company settled on.

Convention, not law

The length. Google's guidance says roughly 1–3 pages for a small change and 10–20 for a large project. Those numbers are an observation about their teams, not a rule you can derive. Several blogs quote "3 to 20 pages" — that is a misquote, and it is recorded in RESOURCES.md.

The section names. A convention. Any set of headings works if it forces goals, non-goals and rejected options into the open.

Requiring approval before coding. That is a big-company process. Working alone, "approval" means one person read it and pushed back once.

When not to write one

This part matters as much as the rest, and the same source says it plainly. Skip the design doc when:

  • the solution is obvious and there are almost no trade-offs;
  • the document would only be an implementation manual — a list of what you will type, with nothing to disagree with;
  • you are prototyping fast, and the overhead would slow the loop that is currently teaching you the most.

Write one when the benefit — agreement now, and a record of the reason later — is worth the afternoon it costs. Not otherwise.

Say it out loud

Plain words

A design doc is you arguing with yourself on paper, before the argument gets expensive.

You write down what you are going to build, what you are deliberately not going to build, and the other ways you could have done it. Then you hand it to one person and ask them to find the hole. Finding the hole costs them an hour. Finding it after you built the thing costs you a month.

One analogy — and where it breaks

The analogy. It is the drawing an architect makes before the builders arrive. Moving a wall on the drawing costs a pencil. Moving it after the concrete is poured costs a jackhammer, a week, and a very unhappy conversation.

Where the analogy breaks An architect's drawing is binding. The builders must follow it, and the building is finished exactly as drawn. A design doc is not binding. Software design is partly discovered while building it, so a good doc is expected to be wrong in places by week three.

If you treat the doc as a contract, you get the worst of both: you move slowly and you follow a plan you already know is wrong. When reality disagrees with the doc, reality wins, and you write down what changed.

The sticking point

Almost everyone gets this one thing wrong, and it is why so many engineers say design docs are a waste of time.

They think the doc's job is to describe the plan. It is not. The doc's job is to expose the trade-off.

A document that only describes the plan cannot be argued with. A reader can only say "looks good". They are not being lazy — you gave them nothing to hold. So no hole gets found, the doc turns out to be useless, and the conclusion people draw is "design docs do not work".

The two sections that turn a description into an argument are non-goals and alternatives considered. A doc missing both is a to-do list wearing a suit.

Teach it back

In two or three sentences, in your own words: why is a design doc that lists no rejected option almost worthless?

Write it before you open the box below. Then send your version to me, and I will tell you which part of the idea is not yet solid. Reading a good answer feels like understanding. It is not the same thing.

Show a model answer — only after you have written yours

A design doc exists so someone can find the mistake while it is still cheap to fix. To find a mistake, they need something they can disagree with. If the doc only says what you will build, the only possible response is "fine" — there is no choice on the page to attack.

The rejected options are what turn it into a real decision. They show the reader what you weighed, so the reader can tell you that you weighed it wrong. They also survive: in a year, the doc answers "why not the simpler way?" and saves you from redoing the same thinking.

Words that mean you recited the book If your answer contained "alignment", "stakeholders", "single source of truth" or "best practice", you copied a shape instead of an idea. Say it again with none of those four words. If it gets harder, that is the signal.

How this fails in production

Five ways this goes wrong. The first four are the reason people distrust design docs. The fifth is the reason they distrust the people who like them.

The to-do list in a suit The doc lists what will be built. No non-goals, no alternatives. Everyone replies "looks good". Six weeks later two people disagree about whether Speck accepts image attachments — and both of them think the doc agreed with them.

The tell: nobody left a single comment that changed anything. The fix: before you send it, find the sentence a reasonable engineer would fight you about. If there is not one, you have not made a decision yet.
The doc nobody read — fails quietly Fourteen pages, a link in a message, two thumbs-up reactions. This one is dangerous precisely because it feels successful. You now believe the design was reviewed. It was not. You will build on that false confidence for weeks.

The fix: never ask "any comments?" Ask one specific question that requires having read it. "Which of my three non-goals do you think I will break first?" An unread doc cannot answer that.
Written after the code — costs real money Very common, and completely pointless. Once the code exists, the doc can only describe it. It cannot change anything, because changing it now means throwing work away — which is exactly the cost the doc was invented to avoid.

You pay the full price of writing it and get none of the benefit. If a doc is genuinely needed after the fact, write a short record of the decision instead, and be honest that it is a record, not a design.
The doc that will not die Week 1 says Speck stores notes in one table. Week 3 proves that is wrong. The doc still says week 1, and now people argue from the doc against what they can see with their own eyes.

The fix: a doc has a date and a status. When reality disagrees, reality wins. Mark it superseded and say what changed. A doc pretending to be current is worse than one openly marked stale.
A doc for everything — kills the practice Someone reads about design docs and requires one for a two-line config change. The team learns that design docs are paperwork, and quietly stops writing the ones that mattered.

This failure is named by the source itself: skip the doc when the solution is obvious and the trade-offs are small. The fix: the test is "is there a real choice here?" No choice, no doc.
The pattern under all five Four of the five come from the same mistake: treating the doc as a deliverable instead of a tool for finding mistakes early. Judge any doc by one question — did it change what we build? If not, it did not work, no matter how neat it looks.

Do it on Speck

This is the artefact for weeks 1–2. Speck is the invented product this whole course builds: a hosted feedback widget. A site owner pastes one <script> tag. Visitors leave a short note. The owner reads the notes in a dashboard.

The task a git repoa text editorone human
  1. Create a repository called speck. Create the file docs/0001-speck.md.
  2. Fill in the six sections below. Keep the whole thing under three pages. Speck is a small product; a 15-page doc for it would be a different failure.
  3. Write at least three non-goals. Each one must be something a reasonable person would expect Speck to do. "Speck will not send SMS" is not a non-goal — nobody expected that. "Speck will not let owners reply to a visitor" is a real one.
  4. Write at least two alternatives considered, with why you rejected each. One of them must be an option you genuinely find tempting.
  5. Write one paragraph each for security, privacy, and how you would know Speck is working.
  6. Send it to one human. Ask exactly one question: "Which of my non-goals do you think I will break first, and why?"

Proof it worked: three things are true. The file exists. You can argue out loud for the option you rejected — properly, not as a straw man. And one other person has written a comment that made you change a sentence. If nothing changed, the doc did not do its job.

The template

# Speck — design doc
Status: draft · Author: you · Date: 2026-08-29

## Context and scope
What exists today. Who has this problem. What this document covers,
and what it does not touch.

## Goals
What is true when this works. Each one must be checkable.

## Non-goals
Things Speck could reasonably do, and will not, on purpose.
At least three. Say why for each.

## The design
How it works. Start with a rough picture, then the detail.
Name the parts: the widget, the write endpoint, the store, the dashboard.

## Alternatives considered
At least two. For each: what it was, and why not.
One must be an option you actually like.

## Cross-cutting concerns
Security. Privacy. How you would know it is working.
A rule of ours, not the source's If you cannot argue for the rejected option, you did not consider it. Writing "we rejected building our own database because that would be silly" is not a rejected alternative. It is a joke about one. This test is this course's, not Malte Ubl's.

Remember this

  • The cost of a wrong decision grows with the finished work standing on it. So find out early.
  • You only find out when the idea meets resistance. Writing is the cheapest resistance, and a second reader is the next cheapest.
  • Six sections: context and scope, goals, non-goals, the design, alternatives considered, cross-cutting concerns.
  • The doc's job is to expose the trade-off, not to describe the plan. No rejected option means nothing to argue with.
  • Length is convention, not law: about 1–3 pages small, 10–20 large. The "3 to 20" you will see quoted online is a misquote.
  • Skip it when the answer is obvious and the trade-offs are small. Requiring one for everything is how teams learn to hate them.
  • It is not binding. When reality disagrees with the doc, reality wins — and you write down what changed.

If you remember one thing: a design doc nobody can disagree with has already failed.
