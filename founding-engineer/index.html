Founding Engineer in 1 Year

How a real software product gets made inside a startup — from no idea at all, to strangers paying for it. Twelve months, because that is how long the money lasts.

The goal. Any early startup can hand you a vague idea, and you can take it all the way to a live product with paying users — alone if you have to — and say why each decision was right. Read MISSION.md for the full version, and The 12-Month Plan for the whole year on one page.

The big picture

Start with what a startup is. Steve Blank's definition does the work: a startup is a temporary organisation searching for a repeatable business model, while a company is a permanent one executing a model it already has.1 Search and execute are opposite jobs. Almost every mistake in this course comes from doing the second one while you are still in the first.

Searching costs money, and the money runs out. That is the whole shape of the problem: a fixed timer, and a guess you have to test before it stops. So the person who turns the guess into something real is not a code monkey. They are the constraint. That is the founding engineer's job, and it is where this course starts.

The timer is also the syllabus. A seed startup with $600k and $50k a month of burn has twelve months. So this course runs for twelve months, and month 1 of the course is month 1 of the runway. One month is the unit of work: one core idea, three Lessons that make it real, one artefact that exists at the end, and one gate that has to close before the next month starts. That mapping is ours, not Blank's — but the arithmetic under it is his framing plus division.

From there the course follows the order the work actually happens in, as three Tracks of four months each.

Track 1 — Find (months 1–4). You cannot build the right thing before you know what hurts. So first you learn to run a conversation without poisoning it: the Mom Test — talk about their life, not your idea, because any question about the future comes back as a polite guess. Then choosing which problem is worth a year of your life, using frequency, pain, who holds the budget, and whether you can find these people at all. Then the part nobody teaches: the kill rule, written down before you start, so you know in advance what evidence would make you stop. Without it, the runway makes the decision for you.

Track 2 — Build (months 5–8). Now the engineering, but with the objective function changed. You stop asking "how long will this take?" and start asking "how much time is this worth?" — appetite instead of estimate, because a fixed-scope overrun has no limit and a fixed-time one does. You learn cutting to the MVP, and to say out loud what you left out on purpose. You learn why the stack should be boring: you get about three novel choices, and a database is a terrible place to spend one. And you build a walking skeleton — thin, ugly, deployed on day one — then go fast everywhere except the places where a failure loses data or loses trust. That line is the quality boundary, and knowing where it sits is most of the craft.

Track 3 — Live (months 9–12). A product nobody uses has taught you nothing. You get the first ten users by hand, one at a time, because every normal channel multiplies a stock you do not have yet. You set the first price, because charging is a test and not a reward. You pick the one metric that matters for the stage you are in and ignore the other thirty. And you learn keeping it alive alone: backups you have actually restored, errors that reach your phone, a deploy you are not afraid of on a Friday.

Twelve core ideas, one per month. That is the 20%, and every month is built around exactly one of them. The other three Lessons in a month are the parts that turn the idea into work you can show someone.

1 Sourced: Steve Blank, "What's A Startup? First Principles." and "Search versus Execute". Checked 2026-08-23.

The year, month by month

Every month has one core Lesson, three support Lessons, one artefact and one gate. The gate is a sentence that must be true before the next month starts. The full version of each month — the question it answers, the artefact, the gate — is on The 12-Month Plan.

Phase 1 · Months 1–4 · Track 1 — Find

Four months and not one line of product code. You still ship every month — a memo, a landing page, a fake door — but never the product.

Month 1 core Lesson live

The seat and the timer

What the job is, how long you have, and what you are actually being paid in.

  • F1 The job is not writing code — the runway is a timer, so a month buys an answer, not a feature. core
  • Runway, burn, and the cost of a month — reading a real set of numbers, and what "we have some time" hides.
  • Why startups die, and which causes you control — the honest list, split into what an engineer can move and what they cannot.
  • The offer: equity, vesting, cliff, dilution — read this before you sign. The usual ranges are convention, not law.

Gate: you can say the company's runway in months, out loud, without looking it up.

Month 2 core Lesson live

Talking to people without poisoning it

The hardest skill in the course, and the one everyone thinks they already have.

  • F2 The Mom Test — talk about their life, not your idea. Ask about the last time it happened, never about the future. core
  • Finding twenty strangers to talk to — where they are, the first message, and why a warm intro is worse than it looks.
  • Notes that survive the week — recording, tagging and storing a conversation so month 3 can use it.
  • Reading the transcript — separating a fact from a compliment, and the enthusiastic person who never buys.

Gate: you can point at three conversations where you learned something that surprised you. Nothing surprising means you were pitching.

Month 3 core Lesson live

Choosing the problem

Twenty conversations produce more than one problem. This month picks one and kills the rest.

  • F3 Choosing which problem to build — frequency, pain, who signs, can you reach them. Score 0 to 5 and multiply, so one 0 takes the whole score to zero. core
  • The one-page problem statement — written so the person with the problem reads it and says "yes, that is it".
  • How small is too small — counting the buyers from the bottom up. No market-sizing slides.
  • Competitors, and why "nobody does this" is bad news — an empty space usually means people tried and it did not work.

Gate: a person with the problem read your page and did not correct you.

Month 4 core Lesson live

Deciding to commit, or not

The month that makes the difference between a decision and a drift.

  • F4 The kill rule — the number, the date and the action, written while you are still calm about the idea. Three paths out, not two. core
  • The riskiest assumption, and the cheapest test — the one belief that, if false, makes everything else pointless.
  • Fake doors, landing pages and concierge tests — shipping a test that is not the product, and the honesty line you do not cross.
  • The go / no-go memo, and saying no out loud — the sentences to use when the answer is no and the founder is in the room.

Gate: the kill rule has a number and a date on it. "It feels wrong" is not a kill rule.

Phase 2 · Months 5–8 · Track 2 — Build

Now you build, with the objective function changed. The month-8 deploy is the point of the whole phase.

Month 5 core Lesson live

Time as the fixed thing

The one habit that stops a six-week feature becoming a six-month feature.

  • B1 Appetite, not estimate — fix the time, vary the scope, because a fixed-scope overrun has no limit. core
  • Shaping, before anyone bets on it — rough sketches at the right altitude: concrete enough to build, loose enough to cut.
  • The scope hammer and the circuit breaker — cutting inside the cycle, and why an unfinished bet does not get an extension.
  • The cycle when the team is one person — six weeks with no designer, no QA and nobody to hand off to.

Gate: the pitch has an appetite in weeks and a written list of what is out. A pitch with no "no" in it has not been shaped.

Month 6 core Lesson live

Cutting

The smallest thing that still answers the question, and saying out loud what is missing.

  • B2 Cutting to the MVP — the removal test and the answer test. A human doing it by hand is a valid implementation. core
  • Buy, glue, or build — the make-or-buy call, and the three questions that settle it in ten minutes.
  • Design and brand with no designer — enough taste to not look untrustworthy, using other people's decisions.
  • The three-minute demo — the script, and why the demo gets written before the software.

Gate: you can give the three-minute demo of a product that does not exist yet, and someone follows it.

Month 7 core Lesson live

The stack

Choosing the tools, and spending a small budget of new things where it actually wins.

  • B3 Boring stack and innovation tokens — about three novel choices. The real argument is failure modes you already understand. core
  • One box, and what it can carry — the honest capacity of a single server and a single database, with numbers.
  • The tables you will regret — the few data-model decisions that are expensive to reverse, and the many that are not.
  • AI features: the product, or a button — when a model is the thing being sold, and when it is a feature that must fail quietly.

Gate: you spent three tokens or fewer, and you can name what each one buys.

Month 8 core Lesson live

Shipping something real

The biggest month in the course. At the end of it there is a URL a stranger can open.

  • B4 The walking skeleton and the quality boundary — deploy end to end on day one, then go fast on anything you could fix in an afternoon. The axis is reversibility. core
  • Auth, payments and email — the three you do not write yourself, and what you still have to understand about each.
  • One database, many customers — tenant isolation done simply, and the query that leaks everyone's data.
  • Shipping alone, continuously — environments, migrations with no downtime, and a flag you can turn off from your phone.

Gate: someone who is not you completed the whole journey without being told what to click.

Phase 3 · Months 9–12 · Track 3 — Live

A product nobody uses has taught you nothing. Users, money, one number, and the work that stops you losing what you built.

Month 9 core Lesson live

The first users

One at a time, by hand, watching them fail in front of you.

  • L1 The first ten users, by hand — every channel multiplies a stock you do not have yet, so at zero they all return zero. Log the refusals. core
  • Onboarding one person at a time — sitting with them, saying nothing, and writing down every place they stopped.
  • Support that does not eat your week — the inbox, the canned answers, and turning a repeated question into a fix.
  • The weekly learning review — one hour, one page: what did this week's users teach us, and what changes because of it.

Gate: at least one user came back a second week without being asked.

Month 10 core Lesson live

Money

Charging is a test, not a reward. This is the month the test runs.

  • L2 The first price — never ask "would you pay?". Say "it is $X, shall I send the link?" and then be quiet. core
  • Taking the money — payments, invoices, tax, refunds and failed cards. The boring half of revenue.
  • Pricing v2: tiers, packaging, expansion — what to change once the first price has been tested, and what not to touch.
  • The first churn, and the exit interview — the most useful conversation in the course, and the one everyone avoids.

Gate: you said a price out loud to a stranger's face and did not apologise for it.

Month 11 core Lesson live

The number

One number for this stage. The other thirty are noise until this one moves.

  • L3 The one metric that matters — one number per stage: empathy, stickiness, virality, revenue, scale. Growth before stickiness fills a leaking bucket at full price. core
  • Instrumentation you will not regret — the few events worth naming carefully, funnels, and cohorts that do not lie.
  • Growth channels after hand-recruiting — picking one channel, testing it properly, and killing it on a date.
  • A/B testing once you finally have traffic — the traffic a test needs before it means anything, and what to do below it.

Gate: you can say why this number and not the obvious one, and what would make you switch.

Month 12 core Lesson live

Staying alive

The smallest set of operations work that stops you losing data or losing trust — plus the honest list of what you skip.

  • L4 Keeping it alive alone — a backup you have restored, errors that reach your phone, a deploy you are not scared of. Plus the list you skip on purpose. core
  • The first security review — SOC 2, GDPR and the questionnaire, from the point of view of one engineer with no compliance team.
  • Scaling past one box — queues, caching and read replicas, and why this is a problem you have to earn.
  • Handing it over: engineers 2 to 5 — the month your habits become the company's habits, whether you wrote them down or not.

Gate: you restored a backup into a clean environment and the product worked. Not "we have backups". You restored one.
