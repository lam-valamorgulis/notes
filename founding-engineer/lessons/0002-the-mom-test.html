Why the normal way of asking fails

Start with what you actually want to know.

You want to know whether people will use your product, and will pay. That is a fact about the future. Now the problem: the future cannot be observed. So you have to get it some other way.

Step 1 — The only thing you can observe is the past

You can observe what someone did. What they bought. What they built by hand to work around the problem. How much time it took them last Tuesday. All of that is real and checkable.

You cannot observe what they will do. Neither can they.

Step 2 — Asking about the future returns a guess

"Would you use this?" asks a person to predict their own behaviour. People are bad at that. Not lying — bad at it. Predicting your own future action is a hard task, and the answer comes out as a hopeful guess.

Step 3 — Politeness makes the guess worse

Now add the second force. The person in front of you can see that you care about the answer. Most people will not hurt you for no reason. So the hopeful guess bends further toward yes.

This is where the name comes from. Ask your mother if your business idea is good and she says yes. Not because it is good. Because she loves you.1

Step 4 — Therefore: never ask about the future

If future questions return bent guesses, stop asking them. Ask only about the past and the present. Those answers are facts, and facts do not bend to be polite.

Instead of this (a guess)Ask this (a fact)
"Would you use a tool that did X?" "When did you last have this problem? Walk me through that day."
"Would you pay $20 a month for it?" "What do you spend on this today? Tools, hours, anyone's salary?"
"Do you think this is a big problem?" "What have you already tried? Why did you stop using it?"
"Would this fit your workflow?" "Show me how you do it now." Then stay quiet and watch.

Step 5 — Therefore: do not mention your idea first

This one surprises people. The moment you describe your idea, the person stops being a witness to their own life. They become a critic of your idea. Those are two different jobs, and only the first one is useful.

So the idea comes last, or not at all in the first conversation.

Step 6 — The three rules, now derived rather than memorised

Rob Fitzpatrick's three rules fall straight out of the steps above.1

The ruleWhich step it comes from
Talk about their life, not your ideaStep 5
Ask about specifics in the past, not the futureSteps 1 to 4
Talk less, listen moreSteps 3 and 5

Step 7 — So what counts as evidence?

Not all answers weigh the same. Rank them by one question: what did it cost the person to give me this? A compliment is free, so it is worth nothing. A credit card number is expensive, so it is worth a lot.

Cover the screen Say the chain: I need a fact about the future → the future cannot be observed → only the past can → future questions return polite guesses → so ask only about the past. Then say the three rules without looking.

Step 8 — Where this stops being true

Two honest limits.

First, the Mom Test finds out whether a problem is real. It does not tell you whether your solution is good. That answer only comes from people using the thing, which is Track 3.

Second, at some point you do have to show your idea and ask for money. The rule is not "never talk about it". The rule is "not first, and not instead of listening".

Where it goes wrong

You pitched, and called it research You spoke for most of the conversation. You explained the idea, handled two objections, and left feeling good. You now know their reaction to your idea. You know nothing about their work.

The tell: your notes contain your own sentences.

You collected compliments Six conversations, all positive, and you cannot name a single number, date, or tool from any of them. Encouragement feels like progress. It is the cheapest thing a person can give you.

The tell: no numbers, no dates, no product names anywhere in your notes.

You asked about the future — fails quietly "Would you pay for this?" Eleven people said yes. Two paid. Nobody lied to you. You asked them all to guess, and their guesses were optimistic. This one is dangerous because it produces confident, well-documented, wrong conclusions.

The tell: the word would appears in your evidence.

Right problem, wrong person Enthusiastic conversations with someone who feels the pain but controls no money and no decision. Months later you learn the buyer is a different person with different priorities, and you built for the wrong one.

The tell: they said "we" a lot, and you never asked who "we" is when something gets bought.

Say it out loud

In plain words

People are kind. If they can see that you want a yes, most of them will give you one. So do not ask questions where kindness can change the answer. Ask about things that already happened. What they did, what it cost, what they tried before. Those answers are facts. Facts are not polite.

One analogy, and where it breaks

You are a doctor. A doctor never asks "do you think you have the flu?" They ask when it started, what you ate, what you already took, whether it hurts when they press here. Then they decide what is wrong.

Where the analogy breaks. A doctor can order a blood test. You cannot. There is no test that shows a person's future buying behaviour. Their history is the only instrument you have. That is why the history-taking is not the warm-up — it is the whole examination.

The sticking point

Two wrong readings, both common.

The first is thinking this is about being polite or being sneaky. It is neither. It is about not changing the answers you came to collect. You are the biggest source of bias in the room, so you keep yourself out of them.

The second is thinking it means you can never talk about your product. You can. Later, and to ask for money, not to ask for approval.

Words that mean you recited a book customer discovery, problem interview, validate the problem, signal, ICP. The plain version: "I asked eight people to walk me through the last time it happened. Six of them had built a spreadsheet to cope. Two were paying someone to do it by hand."
Teach it back

Explain in two or three sentences why "would you use this?" is a bad question — without using the words "bias" or "validate".

Say it out loud, then send it to me. I am checking for one thing: whether you explain why the answer bends, not just that it does.

Reveal a model answer — only after you have tried

"It asks the person to predict their own future, and people are bad at that even when they are trying hard. Then, because they can see I want a yes, the guess moves further toward yes to be kind. So the answer tells me about their manners, not about their behaviour. If I ask what they did the last time the problem happened, kindness cannot change that answer."

Check yourself

Do it this week

Task 1 — three real conversations ~4 hrs
  1. Pick any group of people whose work you find interesting. You do not need a product idea yet. That is the point of doing this now.
  2. Find three of them. Use Indie Hackers, a subreddit for their job, LinkedIn, or people you already know through work.
  3. Ask for fifteen minutes to learn about how they work. Do not mention any idea. You do not have one.
  4. In each call, use only past-tense questions. Take notes in three columns: what they did · what it cost them (hours or money) · what they already pay for · the problem they named. The last column feeds F3.

Proof: three pages of notes in which the word would appears zero times. Every page must contain at least one number and one real tool name. Send them to me and I will mark each line as a fact or as applause — applause meaning a compliment with no fact inside it.

Task 2 — grade your own notes ~30 min
  1. Take the evidence ladder from Block 01.
  2. Go through your three pages line by line. Write the ladder rung next to every line.
  3. Count how many lines sit in the top three rungs: a workaround, a referral, money. That number is your interview quality score for this week.

Proof: a count. If more than half your lines are compliments and guesses about the future, do one more call before moving to F3. This is the one Lesson worth repeating until the number is good.

Remember this

  • You need a fact about the future. The future cannot be observed. Only the past can.
  • Future questions return a guess, and politeness bends the guess toward yes.
  • Three rules: their life not your idea · specifics in the past not the future · talk less.
  • Do not describe your idea first. It turns a witness into a critic.
  • Rank evidence by what it cost them to say. Compliments are free, so they prove nothing.
  • Find out who holds the money before you build for the person who feels the pain.
  • This finds out if the problem is real. It cannot tell you if your solution is good.

If you remember one thing: if the word "would" is in your evidence, you do not have evidence.

Send me your three pages of notes. I will mark every line as fact or applause, and show you the questions you nearly asked well. The best question is the one you think is too basic to ask.
