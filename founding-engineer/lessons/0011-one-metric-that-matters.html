The one metric that matters

One number per stage. Watching thirty numbers is the same as watching none.

Moves forward: judge someone else's work and say what is wrong and why. Naming the wrong headline metric is the fastest diagnosis you can make.  ·  Tag: core item 11 of 12.

The one win. You can look at any startup's dashboard and say, in one sentence, which number should be the headline and which one they are fooling themselves with.

Why one number, and not a dashboard

Start with the problem. You have a dashboard with thirty charts on it. It is Monday morning. Which one do you work on?

Nothing on the screen answers that. That is the problem this Lesson solves.

Step 1 — A metric is only useful if it changes a decision

Start here, because it eliminates most metrics immediately. If the number goes up and you do nothing differently, and it goes down and you do nothing differently, then measuring it was a hobby.

Step 2 — Attention is the scarce resource in a small team

With three people, the limit is not data. It is how many things can genuinely be worked on at once. That number is about one.

That last number is a judgement, not a measurement. If you think your team can hold two, the rest of this Lesson still works. It just gets weaker as the number grows.

Step 3 — Therefore thirty numbers means zero numbers

A dashboard with thirty charts cannot tell you what to do, because it never says "this one, now". Every chart is a request for attention, and you have one unit of it. So the choosing is the work. Croll and Yoskovitz's name for the result is the One Metric That Matters: pick the single number that matters most right now, rally around it, and ignore the rest until it is solved.1

Step 4 — Which number depends on the open question

And the open question changes as the company grows. That is why the OMTM is not one number forever. Lean Analytics gives five stages, each with its own question.1

StageThe open questionWhat the number looks like
Empathy Is this problem real? Interviews per week, against ten by Friday. Not a product metric at all.
Stickiness Do people come back? Stickiness is the stage name; the number under it is retention. Of the 15 who started in week one, how many used it in week two?
Virality Do users bring users? Invites sent per active user, and how many are accepted.
Revenue Does the money work? Revenue per customer against what it costs to get one.
Scale Can we grow this repeatably? Growth rate from one channel you can spend into.

Step 5 — Therefore a later-stage metric is a trap when used early

This is the mistake worth learning properly. Chasing growth before stickiness means pouring users into a container that leaks. You spend money to bring people in and they leave through the holes you have not fixed. The growth number even goes up for a while, which is what makes it a trap.

Cover the screen Name the five stages in order. Then say why a growing intake number can hide a failing product.

Step 6 — Why cumulative totals cannot be your metric

Same point as the kill rule in F4, and it is worth repeating because it catches everyone.

A cumulative total — total sign-ups, total downloads, total revenue ever — only goes up. It hides a fall: the useful part is its slope, and reading a slope by eye is slow and easy to fool yourself about.

So measure the slope directly. That is a rate. A number chosen because it always rises is a vanity metric: pleasant, and decoration.

Use a rate (per week, per user) or a cohort (this group, tracked over time). A cohort is the strongest early tool you have. It stops a flood of new sign-ups hiding the fact that everyone who joined in March has gone.

A count with a target and a date behaves like a rate, because it can fall behind. "Ten interviews by Friday" is usable. "Interviews done" is not.

Step 7 — The squeeze-toy effect

A useful consequence from the same source. When you push hard on one metric, you usually stress another one — improve sign-up conversion and support load rises, improve retention and growth slows. That is not failure. The metric that got worse is telling you what the next OMTM is.1

Step 8 — Where this reasoning stops

One limit, and it is a real one.

Small numbers are noisy. With eleven users, a change from four to six is not a trend, and treating it as one will make you act on noise. Below about a few hundred users, the honest instrument is still conversations — the metric confirms what the conversations told you, rather than replacing them.

1 Sourced: Alistair Croll & Benjamin Yoskovitz, Lean Analytics (O'Reilly, 2013) — the One Metric That Matters, the five stages, and the squeeze-toy effect. See also Ash Maurya's write-up of the same ideas. Checked 2026-08-23.

Where it goes wrong

The dashboard of thirty — fails quietly Everything is measured. Nothing is decided. The dashboard becomes a thing people look at on Monday and feel informed by. It never says "this one, now", so it never changes any behaviour.

The tell: nobody can say what the team would do differently if any single chart moved.

Cumulative sign-ups as the headline "We are at 4,000 users." Of those, 60 opened it twice. The number went up every week, so it never triggered a decision, and it was quoted in every investor update as evidence.

The tell: your headline number has never gone down.

Chasing revenue at the empathy stage Sales targets set before anyone knows whether the product works. The team optimises closing, gets a few customers who then leave, and the churn is blamed on onboarding rather than on the stage being wrong.

The tell: you have a sales target and no retention number.

A metric nobody can move "Brand awareness." "Market share." Real business ideas, but no one on a three-person team can change either of them this week. A metric you cannot act on is a mood.

The tell: you cannot name one thing you would build to move it.

Say it out loud

In plain words

A small team can only really work on one thing at a time. So watching thirty numbers is the same as watching none of them — nothing ever says "do this next". Pick the one number that answers the question you are actually stuck on right now, put it on the wall, and ignore the others until it moves. When the company changes, the question changes, so pick again.

One analogy, and where it breaks

A doctor in an emergency watches one thing. If the patient is bleeding, they watch blood pressure, not cholesterol. Cholesterol matters — later, and not tonight.

Where the analogy breaks. A human body has a fixed set of vital signs that never changes. A product's key number changes every time the company enters a new stage, and nothing tells you it has changed. So you have to re-pick on purpose, which is a step the doctor never has to take.

The sticking point

People think "one metric" means being under-informed, so they compromise: they pick one headline and keep the other twenty-nine on the same screen. That defeats it, because the twenty-nine still compete for the one unit of attention.

The rule is about action, not about data collection. Record everything. Act on one. If your headline metric shares a screen with twelve others, you have a dashboard with a bigger font.

Words that mean you recited a book north star metric, KPI, OKR, data-driven, actionable insights, engagement. Plain version: "of the fifteen people who started in week one, six used it again in week two. That six is the number I am trying to make ten, and nothing else is on the wall."
Teach it back

Explain in three sentences why spending money on growth before fixing retention is a mistake — without using the words "funnel" or "churn".

Say it, then send it. I am looking for the multiplication idea: growth multiplies whatever stays. If your answer is just "retention is important", the mechanism is missing.

Reveal a model answer — only after you have tried

"Growth brings people in, and how many are still there next month decides how many of them are worth anything. So growth multiplies whatever stays — and if almost nobody stays, I am multiplying by nearly zero and paying full price for it. The dangerous part is that the incoming number still goes up, so it looks like it is working while I am spending money to fill a container with holes in it."

Drill the spoken version

Check yourself

Do it this week

Task 1 — pick your stage, then your number ~1 hr
  1. Name your stage honestly from the table in Block 01. Most people building a first product are at empathy or stickiness, not revenue.
  2. Write four lines: the OMTM, its value today, the target, and the date.
  3. Check it can go down. If it cannot, it is a vanity metric — pick again.
  4. Write the five numbers you are choosing not to watch, by name. This part matters as much as the first.

Proof: the four lines, on the wall or at the top of your README, plus the ignore list. Send it to me. The two common faults are a cumulative number and a stage that flatters you — I will say which one you did.

Task 2 — build one cohort table ~90 min
  1. Take everyone who first used your product in a given week.
  2. Count how many of that same group used it in week two, week three, week four. Rows are the starting week, columns are the weeks after.
  3. Ten users is enough. A spreadsheet is enough. Counting by hand is enough.

Proof: a small table with real names or IDs in it. If the second column is mostly empty, you have found the most important fact about your product, and you found it before spending money on growth.

Remember this

  • A metric is only useful if it changes a decision. Most metrics fail this immediately.
  • Attention is the scarce resource. Thirty numbers means zero numbers.
  • The right number depends on the open question: empathy, stickiness, virality, revenue, scale.
  • Growth before stickiness is a leaking bucket — and the intake number still goes up.
  • A cumulative total hides a fall. The news is in its slope, so measure the slope.
  • Use a rate or a cohort. A cohort stops new sign-ups hiding old users leaving.
  • Record everything, act on one. And below a few hundred users, conversations are still the real instrument.

If you remember one thing: if your headline number can never go down, it can never ask you to change anything.
