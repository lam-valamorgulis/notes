The kill rule

Decide how you will stop, before you start. Written while you are still calm about the idea.

Moves forward: predict how it behaves in a case you have never seen. A kill rule is a prediction you write down, then check.  ·  Tag: core item 4 of 12.  ·  Month: 4 of 12 — the plan.

The one win. You can write a stopping condition in one sentence that a stranger could check without asking you anything. And you can say why writing it early is not pessimism.

Why the decision must be made in advance

Start with the failure this prevents. A team spends nine months on a product that six people use. Everyone can see it is not working. Nobody stops. The money runs out, and the money makes the decision.

That is not a courage problem. It is a timing problem, and it has a mechanism.

Step 1 — Building creates attachment

When you have spent four months on something, your judgement about it changes. The effort already spent starts to feel like a reason to continue. This is the sunk cost fallacy. It is standard behavioural economics, with a long research record.1

Knowing about it helps less than you hope. Training can reduce the effect. It does not remove it, and it is weakest on your own project.1

Step 2 — Therefore a decision made after building is a biased decision

A decision made before building is free of that one bias. It has other biases. It does not have this one.

Same person, same brain, different amount of sunk cost in the room. So move the decision earlier in time.

Step 3 — Therefore write the stopping condition first

Before the build starts, you write what result would make you stop. That written sentence is your kill rule. Your calm self is making a decision on behalf of your attached future self.

Step 4 — A rule that cannot be checked is not a rule

"If it is not working, we stop" is a wish. To be a rule it needs three parts:

PartWhy it is requiredBadGood
A number So two people cannot disagree about whether it happened "decent usage" "8 of 15 come back in week two"
A date So the decision happens instead of drifting "once we have data" "on 14 October"
An action So the outcome is not just a feeling "reconsider" "pivot to the second problem on the list"

Annie Duke calls this idea kill criteria, and her pattern is "states and dates": if by this date I have not reached this state, I quit.3 Fill this template and you have one:

By <date>, if <measurable thing> is not at least <number>,
we <pivot to X | kill it>.

Step 5 — The date is not optional, and it is the part people skip

Without a scheduled decision date, the decision never gets made on purpose. It gets made by exhaustion or by an empty bank account. Eric Ries's version of this is a recurring pivot-or-persevere meeting, held on a fixed schedule, where the team looks at the numbers and picks one of the paths.2

Step 6 — There are three outcomes, not two

This is where most people get it wrong. They think a kill rule means "continue or quit". There is a third path, and it is the one people leave out.

Cover the screen Say the three parts a kill rule must have, and the three paths out of the decision. Then say why the decision has to be scheduled rather than triggered.

Step 7 — What to measure: the trap of the growing number

Choose the measured thing carefully, or the rule is decoration.

Any cumulative total — total sign-ups, total downloads, total visits — only ever goes up. It hides a fall: the useful part is its slope, and reading a slope by eye is slow and easy to fool yourself about.

So measure the slope directly. That is a rate.

Use a rate or a cohort instead. Of the fifteen people who started in week one, how many were still there in week two? That number can fall, so it can speak. Lesson L3 goes deep on this.

Step 8 — Where this reasoning stops being true

Two honest limits.

First, a kill rule protects you from continuing too long. It does not protect you from quitting too early. If you set the bar at a level nothing new could reach in the time given, you set the rule so it will always fire. The number has to be the smallest result that would still be encouraging.

Second, the evidence base here is thinner than elsewhere in this course. Most published writing on quitting versus pivoting is opinion and hindsight, not data. Ries's pivot-or-persevere meeting is the strongest named practice, and it is still a practice, not a finding.

Where it goes wrong

No date, so it drifts The rule says "if usage does not pick up we will rethink". There is no day on which anyone is required to look. Six months pass. Everyone privately knows and nobody has to say it.

The tell: the rule contains no calendar date.

A number that can only go up — fails quietly The gate is "500 sign-ups". You reach it, so you persevere. Nobody notices that of the 500, eleven came back a second time. The rule passed and told you nothing, which is worse than having no rule, because now you have false comfort.

The tell: the measured thing is a cumulative total.

The gate moved after the fact You needed 8 of 15 returning. You got 4. So the story becomes "the 4 are really engaged, and the onboarding was broken, so it does not count". Every part of that may be true. It is also exactly what the bias predicted you would say.

The tell: the reason for continuing was invented after seeing the result.

Killed the company when only the guess was wrong The rule fired, and the team shut everything down. But they had a group of users who trusted them and a real understanding of an industry. Both were thrown away with the product. That is the pivot path, missed.

The tell: your rule had two outcomes written in it instead of three.

Say it out loud

In plain words

Once you have worked on something for months, you cannot judge it fairly. The time you already spent starts to feel like a reason to keep going. So make the decision early, while you can still think clearly: write down the result that would make you stop, put a date on it, and say what you will do instead.

One analogy, and where it breaks

It is a stop-loss order. A trader decides the exit price before buying, because nobody thinks clearly while watching their own money fall.

Where the analogy breaks. A share price is one clean public number that updates every second. A startup's signal is messy, slow and partly your own choice of what to measure. So a kill rule needs something a stop-loss does not: you must also name which number and how it is measured, or you will argue about that instead of about the decision.

The sticking point

People think writing a kill rule shows you do not believe in the idea. It is the exact opposite, and this is worth saying out loud in an interview.

A kill rule is what lets you commit completely. You can work at full speed for twelve weeks without hedging. You already know what happens at the end of them.

Without a rule you would be checking your own doubt every week. With one, you are not.

Words that mean you recited a book fail fast, de-risk, validated learning, stage gate, north star. Plain version: "we agreed on 1 August that if fewer than eight of the first fifteen users came back in their second week, we would switch to the warehouse problem instead."
Teach it back

Explain in two or three sentences why a kill rule has to be written before the work starts, and why that is a sign of commitment rather than doubt.

Say it out loud, then send it. I am checking whether you explain the mechanism — why your judgement changes — not just that it does.

Reveal a model answer — only after you have tried

"After a few months of work, the effort I already spent starts to feel like a reason to continue, and knowing about that does not stop it happening. So the version of me that can judge this fairly is the one who has not started yet — I write the stopping condition then, and my later self just checks it. It is commitment, not doubt: I can push hard for twelve weeks without hedging, because the date and the number are already agreed."

Drill the spoken version

Check yourself

Do it this week

Task 1 — write your kill rule ~1 hr
  1. Take the winning problem from F3.
  2. Fill the template: "By [date], if [measurable thing] is not at least [number], we [pivot to X | kill it]."
  3. Check the measured thing is a rate or a cohort, never a cumulative total.
  4. Name the pivot destination now, by name. "We would think again" is not a destination.
  5. Sanity-check the number both ways. Is it low enough that a real early product could reach it? Is it high enough that reaching it means something?

Proof: one sentence a stranger could check without asking you a single question. Send it to me. The most common fault is a number that can only go up — I will catch that immediately.

Task 2 — grade someone else's ~45 min
  1. Take one of the three startups you researched in F1.
  2. Write the kill rule you think they should have, from the outside.
  3. Then write what you think they are actually using instead, and which of the four scars in Block 02 it matches.

Proof: two written rules and a named scar. This is interview practice as much as anything — "what would make you stop?" is a question a good founder will ask you back.

Remember this

  • Effort already spent starts to feel like a reason to continue. Knowing that does not stop it.
  • So the fair decision is the one made before the build. Write the rule then.
  • A rule needs three parts: a number, a date, an action.
  • Schedule the decision. Without a date it never gets made on purpose — the bank account makes it.
  • Three paths out, not two: persevere, pivot, kill. The middle one is the one people forget.
  • Never gate on a cumulative total. A number that only rises can never say stop.
  • A kill rule is what lets you commit hard, because you already know how it ends.

If you remember one thing: pick the number and the date while you still do not care very much.
