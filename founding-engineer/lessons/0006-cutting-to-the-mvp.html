Why cutting is the main skill, not a compromise

Start with the failure this prevents. A team writes down twenty features. They build fifteen of them. They launch after five months. Nobody uses it.

Then the painful part. Eleven of those fifteen were never the reason anyone would have used it. That is not a discipline problem — the team worked hard. It is a filter problem, and the filter is easy to miss.

So let us build the filter. Go back to F1: the output of a month is an answer. Apply that to a feature list and the rest follows.

Step 1 — Every feature costs runway

Not just build time. Also design decisions, bugs, support questions, and code you keep working around forever. A feature costs you again every month, not once.

Step 2 — And every feature is a bet on an unsettled guess

You do not yet know if anyone wants the product. So every feature is built on top of an assumption that has not been tested. If the assumption is wrong, all of it was waste — not just the feature, the whole month.

Step 3 — Therefore each feature has to earn its place with an answer

The question for each item is not "is this useful?" Almost everything is useful. The question is: does this change what we learn? If the answer arrives the same either way, the feature is not part of the first version.

Step 4 — So define MVP properly, because the usual definition is wrong

The definition that works:

Definition

The smallest version that is still worth using once, for real, by someone who is not your friend.

Read the three constraints in that sentence. Worth using, so not a demo. Once, so it does not have to be a habit yet. Not your friend, so politeness cannot carry it.

Step 5 — Two tests you can apply to any item on the list

TestHow to run itIf it fails
The removal test Take the feature out. Does the user still get the benefit, once? Keep it. The benefit depends on it.
The answer test Does having this change what we learn from the first ten users? Cut it. It costs runway and buys nothing.

Note what happens when both are applied. Most items fail the second test even though they pass a "would users like this?" test. That gap is where months of a startup's life go.

Convention, not logic The "three items or fewer" target in Block 04 is this course's number. The two tests decide what survives, and they might leave two items or four. Three is a signal that you ran the tests strictly. It is not a law, and no source gives a number here.

Step 6 — A human behind the screen is a valid implementation

This is the step that surprises engineers, and it is the most useful one.

Your guess is about desire, not about automation. Say ten users request something and you do it by hand each night. You have learned exactly the same thing as if a job queue did it, and you learned it three weeks earlier. Lesson L1 applies the same argument to finding the users.

So for the first version: doing it by hand is not cheating. It is the correct engineering decision, because it answers the same question for less runway. You automate when the manual work stops fitting in your evening, and by then you know it is worth automating.

Step 7 — Deep satisfaction beats broad reach

YC's version of this is blunt and worth keeping: a small group of customers who love you beats a large group who kind of like you. Michael Seibel puts a number on it — ten customers with a burning problem beat a thousand with a passing annoyance.1

The reason is mechanical, not sentimental. Only people who love it give you the detailed, angry, specific feedback that tells you what to build next. People who are fine with it say nothing and leave quietly.

Cover the screen Say the definition of MVP with all three of its constraints. Then say the two tests, and which one most features fail.

Step 8 — Say what you cut, out loud

Silence about a cut reads as "this is everything we thought of". Then, when the missing thing comes up, it looks like an oversight instead of a decision.

So the first version ships with a written list: not in this version, and one reason each. That list is a professional signal. It shows the gaps are chosen.

Step 9 — Where this stops being true

Cutting has a floor. Some things cannot be partly built without the product being a lie: taking money, storing personal data, anything where a failure cannot be undone.

Those sit on the careful side of the quality boundary in B4. You do not cut them. You cut around them, or you do not launch that part at all.

Where it goes wrong

Built the admin panel first It felt necessary — how else will you manage the data? Three weeks went into screens that only you will ever see. With ten users, the admin panel is a database client and a script.

The tell: more than a day of the first month went into a screen no customer will open.

Built for the objection, not the user — fails quietly Someone in a meeting asked "but what about permissions?" and permissions went into version one. Nobody who actually has the problem asked for it. Objections in meetings are cheap and they expand scope for free.

The tell: you can name the meeting a feature came from, but not the user.

An MVP that is a demo It looks right and it cannot be used for real work. Fake data, no saving, one happy path. You will get compliments and no answers, because nobody depended on it for anything.

The tell: nobody could get a real task finished with it today.

Automated what a human could do for ten users A job queue, retries and a dead-letter table, to handle four requests a day. Two weeks of runway spent so that you did not have to spend twenty minutes an evening. The queue was the right build — eight months too early.

The tell: your infrastructure could handle a hundred times your current users.

Watch this next — the primary source "How to plan an MVP" — Michael Seibel, YC Startup Library. Short, and the clearest statement of the "do not fall in love with your MVP" point: if you love it, you will not let it change, and it has to change.

Say it out loud

In plain words

Every feature costs you time now and forever, and you still do not know if anyone wants the product. So only build the parts that change what you learn. Everything else waits. If a person can do a step by hand for the first ten customers, let a person do it. And write down what you left out, so people can see you chose it.

One analogy, and where it breaks

Open a food stall before you open a restaurant. One dish, one queue, real money from strangers. You find out today whether people like the food.

Where the analogy breaks. A food stall cannot become a restaurant by adding tables — it is a different business with different equipment. Software mostly can grow that way. So the real risk is not that your first version is too small. The risk is confusing small with fake: a stall still sells real food to real people who really pay.

The sticking point

People think MVP means low quality. It does not. It means narrow.

The first version does one job, and it does that job properly — it saves, it does not lose data, it works twice. Narrow and solid is an MVP. Broad and flaky is a demo, and a demo teaches you nothing because nobody can depend on it.

Words that mean you recited a book MVP, lovable, thin slice, v0, concierge, wizard of oz. Plain version: "version one does one thing: it takes their weekly file and gives back the report. I do the checking by hand every evening until it is too much work."
Teach it back

Reveal a model answer — only after you have tried

"What I am trying to find out is whether people want this, and a human doing the step answers that question exactly as well as code doing it. So the automated version costs more runway and buys the same answer, which makes it the worse engineering choice right now. I automate when the manual work stops fitting in my evening — and by then I know it is worth building, because people are using it enough to make it painful."

Drill the spoken version

Check yourself

Do it this week

Task 1 — the cut list ~2 hrs
  1. For the problem from F3, write every feature you think the product needs. Aim for at least fifteen. Do not filter while listing.
  2. Run the removal test on each. Then the answer test.
  3. Make two columns: Keep and Not in version one. Every row in the second column gets a one-line reason.
  4. For every kept row, write who does it: code or me, by hand.

Proof: the Keep column has three items or fewer — this course's target, not a rule — and at least one of them says "me, by hand". Send it to me. If Keep has eight rows, you ran a usefulness test instead of the answer test, and I will show you which rows gave it away.

Task 2 — defend a cut ~30 min
  1. Pick the cut that would be hardest to defend to the person who asked for it.
  2. Record yourself defending it in under sixty seconds. No apology, no "eventually we will".
  3. Then record the version where they push back once.

Proof: two recordings. Check that you named what the cut buys — shipping two weeks earlier, learning sooner — rather than only what it saves.

Remember this

  • Every feature is a permanent bill and a bet on a guess you have not settled yet.
  • The question is not "is it useful?" — it is "does this change what we learn?"
  • MVP = smallest version still worth using once, by someone who is not your friend.
  • Two tests: removal (does the benefit survive?) and answer (does learning change?).
  • A human doing it by hand is a valid implementation. It buys the same answer for less runway.
  • Narrow and solid is an MVP. Broad and flaky is a demo, and a demo teaches nothing.
  • Write the "not in this version" list. Silence about a cut reads as an oversight.

If you remember one thing: cut until it is embarrassing, then check it still does one real job properly.
