What a first build has to prove, and where care belongs

Part one — why the first build is thin and end to end

Step 1 — Software nobody can reach teaches nothing

From F1: the output is answers, and answers come from strangers touching the thing. Code on your laptop is worth zero answers. So being deployed is not a milestone at the end — it is a precondition for learning anything.

Step 2 — The riskiest part of a new system is the joins

Think about where new projects actually get stuck. Rarely in the business logic. Almost always in the seams: the deploy pipeline, environment variables, the database connection from the real host, the auth callback URL, the email provider refusing to send from a new domain.

Each piece works alone. The system fails between the pieces.

Step 3 — Therefore build the thinnest complete path first

Alistair Cockburn's name for this is a walking skeleton: a tiny implementation of the system that performs one small end-to-end function, linking together the real main components.1 Then the architecture and the features grow together, along a path that already works.

For a web product, the first day looks like this:

a browser → one real route → one real write → the real database ↓ a visible result on a real URL ← one real read deployed. on a domain. by a script you ran, not by hand.

It does nothing useful. That is fine. Every seam in the system has now been crossed once, while the cost of fixing a seam is at its lowest.

Step 4 — A skeleton is not a prototype

Important distinction, and people get it wrong constantly. A prototype is built to be thrown away. A skeleton is the first version of the real thing: real components, real deploy, real database. Thin, not fake.

Part two — so where does care belong?

Now the harder half. "Go fast" cannot mean "never be careful", or you lose customer data in month two. So we need a rule that says where. Let us derive one instead of using taste.

Step 5 — Two questions decide it

For any possible mistake, ask two things:

  1. How bad is it if this goes wrong?
  2. Can it be undone?

The second question is the one that does the work, and it is the one people forget. A bad-but-reversible mistake costs you time. A small-but-permanent one can cost you the customer who was going to give you the answer.

Step 6 — Therefore reversibility decides first, and cost decides second

Cover the screen Say the two questions that place a mistake on the grid, and which one people forget. Then name three things that live on the careful side for a product with ten users.

Step 6b — Two things this rule does not cover

Be honest about the limits, or the rule turns into a slogan.

It does not cover how likely the mistake is. A cheap, reversible bug that hits every user every day still deserves a test.

It does not cover how expensive the undo is. Reversible in an afternoon and reversible in a week are not the same thing. A bad migration with a good backup is reversible and still costs you a day of downtime.

So reversibility gets you the first answer, not the whole answer. Ask "can it be undone?" first, then "what would undoing it cost?"

Step 7 — What this rule actually decides for you

DecisionThe rule's answer
Should I write tests for the payment code? Yes. Wrong money movement is not undoable. This is the clearest yes in the whole product.
Should I write tests for the settings page layout? No. Wrong layout is an afternoon. Ship it and look at it.
Should I add a backup before launch? Yes, and restore it once. Lost data is the definition of irreversible. See L4.
Should the import be idempotent — safe to run twice? Yes, if running it twice would double or corrupt data. That is the same question in different clothes.
Should I refactor this ugly module? Not yet. Ugly is reversible and cheap. Do it when it starts causing the irreversible kind of bug.

Step 8 — What "go fast" gives up, honestly

The reversible side really does get worse. Your code will be ugly, your naming will be inconsistent, and you will have three slightly different ways of doing the same thing. That is a genuine cost, paid later, in slower development.

You accept it because the alternative — being careful everywhere — costs you the runway, and runway is the thing you cannot buy back. Say this out loud rather than pretending the trade is free.

Step 9 — Where this reasoning stops

The boundary moves as the company changes. When the business model is found and customers depend on you daily, more things become irreversible: an outage is now a lost contract, a bad release now reaches ten thousand people. The rule is the same; the answers change. That is the flip from search to execute in F1, and this is exactly where founding engineers get caught out.

Where it goes wrong

Careful in the reversible half Ninety-five percent test coverage on the interface. No database backup. The care went where mistakes are cheap and skipped where they are permanent. This is the single most common misread of "move fast", and it looks like discipline from the outside.

The tell: you can quote your coverage number but not the last time a backup was restored.

A skeleton that never left the laptop — fails quietly It works end to end locally, so it feels done. The real deploy is scheduled for "when there is something to show". Then week six discovers the auth callback, the environment variables and the email domain all at once, under pressure.

The tell: your skeleton has no public URL.

Careful everywhere, so nothing shipped Full test suite, clean architecture, three abstraction layers, and no users. The work is genuinely good. It bought zero answers, and the runway does not care how clean it was.

The tell: month two, and no stranger has used it.

Money code written at product speed The charge runs twice on a retry. A refund lands on the wrong customer. Both fixable in code, neither undoable in the customer's bank account or in their opinion of you.

The tell: nothing that touches money is safe to run twice.

Say it out loud

In plain words

On day one, build the thinnest thing that goes all the way through — a real page, a real save, a real address on the internet. It does not have to be useful. It has to be real, because that is where the hidden problems live. After that, work fast on anything you could fix in an afternoon, and be careful only where a mistake cannot be taken back.

One analogy, and where it breaks

Before you build the bridge, you string a single rope across the river and walk it. Now you know how wide the river is, where the far bank is soft, and that the rope holds.

Where the analogy breaks. A rope and a real bridge have nothing in common structurally — the rope teaches you about the river, not about the bridge. A walking skeleton is the opposite: it uses the real components. That is the entire point. It teaches you about the bridge, because it is the first inch of the bridge.

The sticking point

People place mistakes on the grid by how bad it feels instead of whether it can be undone.

That is why a slow page gets a week of work. A missing backup gets none. The slow page is visible and embarrassing every day. The missing backup is invisible until it is fatal.

Read the two low-drama corners of the grid again. A lost sign-up email is small and permanent. That person is gone and you will never know their name. It belongs on the careful side.

A crashing dashboard is loud and reversible, so it does not — unless it crashes in front of the person deciding whether to trust you. Then it has moved to the other side.

Words that mean you recited a book technical debt, tech debt is fine, move fast and break things, MVP quality, good enough. Plain version: "the layout is bad and I will fix it next week. The import cannot run twice, and I wrote a test for that, because running it twice would double every customer's data."
Teach it back

Explain in three sentences how you decide whether a piece of code in an early startup deserves a test — without using the words "important" or "critical".

Say it, then send it. I am looking for undone or reversible in your answer. If the answer is about how central the feature is, the rule has not landed.

Reveal a model answer — only after you have tried

"I ask what happens if this is wrong, and then whether I could take it back. If the worst case is an ugly screen or a missing feature, I can fix that in an afternoon, so a test costs more than the mistake would. If the worst case is deleted data, double-charged money, or a leaked email address, then no amount of fixing afterwards undoes it — so that is where the test goes. The size of the feature does not come into it; only whether the damage is permanent."

Drill the spoken version

Check yourself

Do it this week

Task 1 — deploy a walking skeleton today ~3 hrs
  1. Using the stack you settled in B3, build one route that accepts one input.
  2. Write it to a real database — not a file, not memory.
  3. Read it back and show it on the page.
  4. Deploy it to a real URL, using a command or a push. Not by dragging files.
  5. Open it on your phone, on mobile data, and add one row.

Proof: the URL, and a screenshot from your phone showing the row you added. Send me the link. If any step took more than an hour, that step was a seam — write down what it was, because that is what this task exists to find.

Task 2 — write your quality boundary ~1 hr
  1. Two lists for your product. Fast: at least five things you will do quickly and fix later. Careful: exactly three things you will not.
  2. Beside every item on the Careful list, write the sentence "if this goes wrong, ______ cannot be undone".
  3. Pin it where you will see it. This list is the thing you will be tempted to break at 11pm.

Proof: the two lists. If your Careful list has seven items, you have written a wish, not a boundary — three is the number that survives contact with a real week.

Remember this

  • Undeployed software buys zero answers. Being live is a precondition, not a milestone.
  • New systems fail between the pieces. So cross every seam once, on day one.
  • A walking skeleton is thin but real: real components, real database, real URL.
  • A skeleton is not a prototype. A prototype is thrown away; a skeleton is version one.
  • The quality boundary is set first by reversibility, then by what the undo costs.
  • Careful side: lost data, wrong money, leaked personal data, the trust of your first ten users.
  • The boundary moves as the company grows. More things become irreversible, and nobody announces it.

If you remember one thing: go fast on anything you could fix in an afternoon, and only there.
