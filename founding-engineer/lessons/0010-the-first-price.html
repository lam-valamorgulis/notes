The first price

Charging is a test, not a reward for finishing. Free usage proves less than you think.

Moves forward: explain it from first principles. Why money is evidence and usage is not, derived rather than asserted.  ·  Tag: core item 10 of 12.

The one win. You can set a first price with a written reason, and ask a real person for money out loud without apologising for it.
Weaker evidence than the rest of this course Be warned: this is the thinnest-sourced Lesson here. Almost everything published on first pricing is experience and opinion, not measurement. The reasoning below is derived from first principles where it can be, and where it rests on practitioner advice it says so. Treat the numbers as starting points, never as findings.

Why money is evidence and usage is not

Start with the problem. You built something. Fifty people signed up and use it for free. Do you have a business?

You cannot tell. This Lesson is about why, and what to do instead.

Step 1 — What makes a signal strong

Same rule as F2, applied to a product instead of a conversation. A signal is strong in proportion to what it cost the person to send it.

Step 2 — Therefore free usage is a weak signal

Signing up for something free costs a minute of curiosity. It measures interest, and interest is abundant. It does not measure whether the problem is worth solving, because the person risked nothing by finding out.

Step 3 — Money is the cheapest strong signal available

There is one thing stronger than money: coming back for months. But that takes months. Money takes an afternoon and it costs the sender something real.

So charging is not the final step after everything works. It is the fastest available test of whether anything works at all.

The reframe

Charging is not a reward for having finished. It is the cheapest strong test you can run this week.

Step 4 — Therefore a low price is not the safe choice

This step feels backwards, and it has two parts.

First, a very low price hides the answer. If someone pays $3 a month, you have not learned whether the problem matters — only that it is not worth arguing about. The test loses its power exactly because it is cheap.

Second, cheap customers cost more, not less. One practitioner counted his own support load by tier: his $9 customers filed about seven times as many requests as his $199 customers.1

That is one product, not a study. Treat it as a warning, not a law. The related finding is better attested: paying customers ask for far less support than free users.1

Step 5 — What to price on: the value metric

Pick the thing that grows as the customer gets more value. That is the value metric, and getting it right matters more than getting the number right.1

You charge per…Good whenBad when
Seat / user Value grows with team size Teams share one login to avoid the cost — you taught them to cheat
Usage (rows, orders, jobs) Value grows with volume The bill is unpredictable and finance blocks it
Flat fee You want a decision with no arithmetic Your biggest customers pay the same as your smallest

Step 6 — Therefore: derive the first number from what they already pay

You do not need a pricing model. You need one number, and you already collected the input for it in F2: what does this problem cost them today?

Cover the screen Say why free usage is weak evidence, and why a very low price is not the safe option. Then say where the first number comes from.

Step 7 — Ask for the money, do not ask about it

"Would you pay for this?" is an F2 violation — a question about the future, answered by politeness. There is only one version of this question that returns a fact:

The only pricing question that returns data

"It is $40 a month. Shall I send you the link?"

Then be quiet. What happens next is your data: they pay, they negotiate, they ask a question, or they go quiet. All four are informative. A "yes, sounds reasonable" with no payment is not.

Step 8 — The first price is a hypothesis with a date on it

Write it down the same way you wrote your kill rule in F4: "$40/month, reviewed on 1 November 2026." A price you never revisit becomes permanent by accident, and the first price is almost always wrong.

Step 9 — Where this reasoning stops

Three limits, stated plainly.

Not every product can charge on day one. Products whose value depends on other people being there — marketplaces, social products — genuinely need free users first. Then usage really is your only early signal, and it is weak, and you have to live with that.

Discounts to the first customer set your reference price. Common advice is to price fairly so price is not the blocker for the reference account.1

SaaStr says the opposite about the cost: it thinks a first discount stops mattering once you have hundreds of customers, so give it and raise the price for the next ones. We think it sets the reference price. That disagreement is our opinion, not a finding.

The evidence here is practitioner experience. Nobody has run a controlled trial on your first price. Hold these rules more loosely than the ones in Track 2.

Where it goes wrong

Free forever, to "get traction" — fails quietly Four hundred sign-ups, no revenue, and a founder who believes the product is working. Then charging starts and 396 of them leave. The number was never evidence; it was curiosity, counted.

The tell: your best metric is a number of free accounts.

Priced on your cost, not their value Server bills plus a margin. That number has nothing to do with what the problem is worth to the customer, and it is usually far too low — software costs almost nothing to run and can save someone a day a week.

The tell: you can explain your price using your own expenses.

Asked instead of charged "Would you pay $50 for this?" Eleven yeses, one payment. You ran a politeness survey. The fix is one sentence: name the price and offer the link.

The tell: your pricing evidence contains the word "would" — again.

The friendly discount became the price You gave your first customer 70% off because they took a chance on you. Fair. Then they recommended you to two others and quoted the discounted number, and now that is what the market expects.

The tell: nobody has ever paid your list price.

Say it out loud

In plain words

People will happily use something free, because it costs them nothing to try. That tells you they were curious. It does not tell you the problem matters. Money costs them something, so it is the first honest answer you can get. Ask for it early, and ask by naming a price — not by asking whether they would pay one.

One analogy, and where it breaks

You are busking. A crowd stops and claps. That feels great and means little. The coins in the hat are the measurement, because a coin costs the giver something and a clap does not.

Where the analogy breaks. A busker cannot ask the person who walked past why they did not pay. You can, and you must. Your refusals are half your data — the exact words of a "no thanks" usually name the reason, which is something no busker ever gets to hear.

The sticking point

Engineers feel that charging before the product is good is dishonest. That instinct is decent and it is aimed at the wrong thing.

Charging for something that does not work is dishonest. Charging for something small that does work is normal. A small shop can sell one product and still be a real business.

The honest version has three parts. Say plainly what it does and does not do. Name a price. Refund anyone unhappy without arguing. That is not a trick.

Words that mean you recited a book monetise, willingness to pay, value-based pricing, freemium, conversion rate. Plain version: "it does the Monday report and nothing else yet. It is $40 a month. Their operations person spends about four hours on it now, so it pays for itself in the first week."
Teach it back

Explain in three sentences why a very low first price can be worse than a higher one.

Say it, then send it. I want both halves: what it does to the signal, and what it does to who shows up. Most people only get the second.

Reveal a model answer — only after you have tried

"The whole reason to charge is that paying costs the person something, so their yes means something. At $3 a month it costs them almost nothing, so the yes goes back to being nearly free and I have not learned whether the problem matters. And the cheapest price attracts the people who want the most help for the least commitment, so I get a weaker signal and a heavier support load at the same time."

Drill the spoken version

Check yourself

Do it this week

Task 1 — derive the number ~90 min
  1. Open your F2 notes. For each person, write what the problem costs them today, in money or in hours times a plausible rate.
  2. Decide which branch of the diagram you are on: their hours, another tool, or nothing.
  3. Write one price, one value metric, and one sentence of justification.
  4. Add the review date: "reviewed on ____".

Proof: four lines — price, value metric, justification, review date. Send them to me. If your justification mentions your server bill, I will send it straight back.

Task 2 — ask three people for money ~2 hrs
  1. Take three of the people from L1 who are actually using it.
  2. Say the sentence: "It is $X a month. Shall I send you the link?" Then stop talking.
  3. Write down what happened, word for word. Especially the refusals and the negotiations.

Proof: three verbatim responses. This is the hardest task in the course so far and it is the one with the highest information per minute. A refusal here is worth ten compliments.

Remember this

  • A signal is strong in proportion to what it cost the sender. Free usage costs a minute.
  • Money is the cheapest strong signal. Only long-term return is stronger, and that takes months.
  • Charging is a test, not a reward for finishing.
  • A very low price weakens the signal and attracts the heaviest support load.
  • The value metric — what grows as their value grows — matters more than the number.
  • Derive the first number from what the problem costs them today.
  • Never ask "would you pay?". Say "it is $X, shall I send the link?" and then be quiet.
  • All seven of these are practitioner experience, not measurement. Hold them loosely.

If you remember one thing: a yes with no payment attached is not a yes.
