Where this role comes from

Start with the problem, not the job title.

A founder has an idea. Nobody knows if the idea is true. Talking about it does not settle anything. Somebody has to make it real enough for a stranger to use it, or refuse it. That is the problem this role was invented for. Now let us derive the rest.

Step 1 — What a startup is (accept this)

Steve Blank's definition does most of the work. A startup is a temporary organisation that searches for a repeatable, scalable business model. A company is a permanent organisation that executes a model it already has.1

Search and execute are opposite jobs. Remember that. Most mistakes in this course come from doing the second one while still in the first.

Step 2 — The money is a timer (accept this)

Searching costs money. The money is finite. Two numbers give you the timer:

runway (months) = cash in the bank / money burned per month

That is arithmetic, not strategy. A startup with $600k and $50k a month has twelve months. Not "about a year". Twelve.

Step 3 — So what does a month produce?

Here is the first real step. If the business model is unknown, then the useful output of a month is not features. It is answers.

The company spends money and gets answers back. Did anyone want it? Will they pay? Do they come back? Every month you either bought an answer, or you did not.

Step 4 — Therefore the measure changes

If answers are the output, then the number that matters is answers per month. Not features per month. Not lines of code.

Two engineers can ship at exactly the same speed and buy a very different number of answers. One builds four small things that each settle a question. The other builds one large thing that settles nothing until month fourteen.

Step 5 — Therefore somebody has to make things real

An answer about your solution cannot come from a meeting. A stranger has to touch the thing and react. So the search needs a person who turns a vague idea into a real, usable thing, fast.

Answers about the problem can come from conversations. That is Lesson F2, and it needs no product at all.

The person who makes things real is the founding engineer. This course's one-line version: the first person accountable for the company being able to act.2

Cover the screen Say the chain out loud, from the start: startup is search → search costs money → money is a timer → so a month must produce an answer → so someone must make things real. If a link is missing, read Step 1 to Step 5 again before you go on.

Step 6 — Five consequences that follow, not opinions

Each of these follows from "the output is answers per month". None of them is a taste preference.

Because… Therefore
Nobody has written the requirements yet You sit in the customer calls yourself. Second-hand requests lose the why, and the why is the part you build from.
Time spent learning a tool buys no answers You pick tools you can already operate. Choosing a boring tool is a speed decision. Lesson B3.
Software nobody can reach teaches nothing You deploy something end to end on day one, however thin. Lesson B4.
Every line of code is written before you know the guess is true You write less than you want. Cutting scope is the main skill. Lesson B2.
Some failures cannot be undone You stay careful in a few places. Losing a customer's data removes the person who was going to give you the answer.

Read that last row again. It is the line between fast and reckless. Lesson B4 calls it the quality boundary.

Step 7 — Where this reasoning stops being true

Name the limit, or the rule becomes a habit nobody can explain.

All of the above holds while the business model is unknown. The day the model is found, the job flips from search to execute. Then the same habits turn into debt. Thin code becomes an outage. "Deploy anything" becomes an incident review.

This is why some founding engineers struggle two years in. The job changed under them, and nobody announced it. Knowing that in advance is most of the value of this Lesson.

Bare facts, not derivations Two things here are convention, not logic. First, the title. "Founding engineer" is not a legal or standard role. It means different things at different companies.

Second, the equity. The two sources do not agree, and neither gives a single range. Underdog.io reports 0.1% to 2%, with a median near 0.33%. FCTO quotes 1%–3% pre-seed, 0.5%–1.5% at seed, and 0.25%–0.75% at Series A. The stage moves the number more than your negotiation does.

Where it goes wrong

Four ways this goes wrong in real life. Each one has a tell you can spot in your first week.

Execute-mode process in a search-mode company The team runs quarterly roadmaps, ticket estimates and velocity charts. Output looks excellent. Answers per month: zero. Everyone is busy delivering a plan that was written before anyone knew anything.

The tell: ask "what did we learn last month?" and get a list of shipped features as the answer.

Second-hand requirements — fails quietly The engineer never joins a customer call. The founder relays what was said. Each relay drops the why, so the engineer builds the literal request. This one is dangerous because nobody is unhappy until launch day.

The tell: you can describe what to build but not who asked for it or what they do today instead.

Innovation tokens spent on plumbing Week one: a database nobody on the team has run, a framework released this year, and a deploy tool with three blog posts about it. Three weeks of runway went into learning tools. Zero answers bought. Lesson B3 covers this.

The tell: your last three days of work show up in your terminal history, not in the product.

Careful in the reversible half Ninety-five percent test coverage on the interface. No database backup. Care was spent where mistakes are cheap, and skipped where they are permanent. This is the most common misread of "move fast".

The tell: you can name your test coverage number but not the last time a backup was restored.

Say it out loud

In plain words

A startup is a guess about what people want. The money in the bank is a timer. Your job is to turn the guess into something real, fast, so the answer arrives before the timer stops. Writing code is how you do the job. It is not the job.

One analogy, and where it breaks

You are the first cook in a new restaurant that has no menu yet. You do not plan forty dishes. You cook one small dish, put it in front of a customer, and watch their face. Their face is the answer. Then you cook the next one.

Where the analogy breaks. A cook can throw the dish in the bin and the kitchen is unchanged. Software you ship stays. It keeps running, keeps costing money, keeps needing care. So "just try it" is cheaper for a cook than for you. That is why small matters more than fast.

The sticking point

Almost everyone hears "move fast" and thinks "be sloppy". That is the wrong reading, and it is the one that gets people fired.

Speed comes from cutting scope. It does not come from cutting care. Cutting scope is reversible — you can add the feature next month. Losing a customer's data is not reversible. Same word, "fast", two very different decisions.

Words that mean you recited a book If you use these in an interview without the plain version behind them, you sound like you read about the job. lean, iterate, MVP, product-market fit, validate, de-risk. Say the plain thing instead: "we built the smallest version to find out if anyone would use it."
Teach it back

Explain in two or three sentences. Why does a startup need a founding engineer at all? And why is that different from a normal engineering job?

Say it out loud first, then write it, then send it to me. I am looking for one specific thing: whether the timer appears in your answer. If it does not, the reasoning has not landed yet.

Reveal a model answer — only after you have tried

"A startup does not know yet whether anyone wants its product. And it has a fixed amount of money to find out."

"So what a month has to produce is an answer, not a feature. A founding engineer makes the idea real enough for a stranger to use it — fast enough that the answer arrives while there is still money left."

"A normal engineering job optimises for correctness over years. This one optimises for learning speed. That is why the same person can be right in one job and wrong in the other."

Three sentences. No jargon. The timer is in it.

Drill the spoken version

These are the questions you will be asked, in an interview and on the job. Say each answer out loud before you reveal it. Reading a good answer feels like knowing it, which is the exact illusion to avoid.

Check yourself

Do it this week

Task 1 — find the guess ~90 min
  1. Find three startups that are hiring a founding engineer or a first engineer right now. Use Work at a Startup, Hacker News "Who is hiring", or Indie Hackers.
  2. For each one, write the guess the whole company depends on. Not the product. The guess. Use this shape: "We believe [who] will change [what they do today] to get [what benefit]."
  3. For each one, write the smallest thing that would test that guess, and how many weeks it would take.
  4. For each one, write the single question you would ask the founder in an interview, aimed straight at the guess.

Proof: one page. Three startups, twelve lines. Send it to me. I will tell you which guesses you wrote as products instead of guesses — that is the most common miss, and seeing it once fixes it.

Task 2 — sixty seconds, out loud ~30 min
  1. Record a voice memo on your phone. Sixty seconds, one take.
  2. Answer: "What does a founding engineer do, and why does the role exist?"
  3. Listen back. Mark every jargon word you used from the list above.
  4. Record it again with those words removed. Keep the second take.

Proof: two recordings, and the second one is shorter than the first. If it got longer, the jargon was hiding a gap in the reasoning.

Remember this

  • A startup searches for a business model. A company executes one it already has. Opposite jobs.
  • runway = cash / monthly burn. The money is a timer, and the timer sets every decision.
  • While the model is unknown, a month's real output is an answer, not a feature.
  • So the measure is answers per month. Four small builds beat one big build at identical speed.
  • A founding engineer is the first person accountable for the company being able to act.
  • Fast means small scope, never less care. Cut features, not backups.
  • When the model is found, the job flips to execute, and these habits become debt. Nobody will tell you the day it happens.

If you remember one thing: the money is a timer, so your job is to buy answers with it — not features.
