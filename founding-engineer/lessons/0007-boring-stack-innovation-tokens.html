Boring stack and innovation tokens

You get about three novel choices. A database is a terrible place to spend one.

Moves forward: say why it was built this way, and what that choice gives up. This is the clearest trade in the course: you buy one thing by giving up another.  ·  Tag: core item 7 of 12.

The one win. You can defend a boring stack to an excited team without sounding old or lazy, using one question that ends the argument.

Why "boring" is a speed argument

Here is the problem. This week you have to pick a language, a database, a host, a deploy method and five other things. You have no product, no users, and money running out.

Every one of those choices is made with the least information you will ever have. And you will live with them for years.

People think the answer is about taste, or about being cautious. It is not. It is an argument about time.

Step 1 — Every tool has two costs, not one

CostWhen you pay itCan you see it when choosing?
Learning it The first week Yes. You can feel this one, so you plan for it.
Debugging it when it breaks strangely A random Tuesday, later No. Invisible at choosing time, and much larger.

Step 2 — The second cost is the one that matters

Learning a tool has a floor and a ceiling. You can guess it: days for a library, weeks for a database or a deploy system.

Debugging an unfamiliar tool at the worst moment has no ceiling. You do not know how long it will take, because you do not know what is wrong. And you have no instinct to guide you.

Step 3 — What "boring" actually means

Dan McKinley's argument, and the clearest statement of this I have found: the point of boring technology is that its failure modes are well understood.1 Not that it is old. Not that it is dull. When it breaks, the shape of the breakage is already known — and reading about a failure is not the same as having fixed one.

Step 4 — Therefore novelty is a risk budget

McKinley's name for it: innovation tokens. You get about three. Every unfamiliar tool spends one.1 Spend them and you have no capacity left for surprises — and the product itself is going to be one big surprise.

Step 5 — Before you have a product, the budget is one, not three

McKinley's three is for a company that already has a business model. Before you have one, this course sets the budget at one. That number is ours, not his.

The reason: a pre-product startup has no spare capacity at all, and the product itself is guaranteed to be a surprise.

Step 6 — The number that matters is per team, not per tool

This step is ours, not McKinley's. He defines boring by maturity and wide use, and he lists Postgres as boring with no qualification. Here is the extension.

"Boring" is not only a property of the tool. It is also a property of the tool plus you.

Postgres is boring to most of the industry. If you have never run Postgres in production, it is not boring to you — it costs you a token. Meanwhile a framework the internet calls risky might be free for you, because you have shipped four products with it.

So the honest question is not "is this boring?" It is "have I run this in production before?"

Cover the screen Say the two costs of any tool, and which one is invisible when you choose. Then say what "boring" really means, in five words or fewer.

Step 7 — The question to ask out loud

Say this in the meeting

"What does this give us this month that the boring option does not?"

The words "this month" do the work. Almost every argument for an exciting tool is about a future state: when we have scale, when we have a team, when the data model grows. Those are real arguments for a company that has found its business model. You have not found yours yet.

Step 8 — What the boring choice gives up

Be honest about the cost, or you are just being conservative and calling it reasoning. Boring choices give up three things:

  • Some real capability. Sometimes the new tool genuinely does something the old one cannot. When a customer can feel that difference, spend the token.
  • Some hiring appeal. A few engineers only want to work on new technology. You will not attract those people. Decide if you mind.
  • Some future rework. The boring choice may need replacing at a scale you may never reach. That is a good trade: a problem you have to earn is better than one you invented.

Step 9 — Where this reasoning stops

Two limits.

First, if the novel thing is the product — you are building a company around a new model, a new protocol, a new device — then it is not a token, it is the business. The rule applies to everything around it, not to it.

Second, "boring" can be an excuse for a tool that genuinely cannot do the job. If you are fighting your database every week to make it do something normal, the cost has moved to the other side, and you should move with it.

Where it goes wrong

All three tokens on plumbing, week one A database nobody has run, a framework released this year, a deploy tool with three blog posts about it. Three weeks of runway went into learning tools and zero answers were bought. When the real surprise arrives, there is no capacity left for it.

The tell: your last three days show up in your terminal history, not in the product.

"We will need it at scale" — fails quietly A choice justified by a load you do not have and may never have. It looks responsible, so nobody challenges it. The cost lands months later as slow development on every ordinary feature.

The tell: the justification is a sentence about the future with no date in it.

Boring by reputation, new to you You picked Postgres and Kubernetes because everyone calls them standard. You have never operated either. Postgres is on McKinley's boring list; Kubernetes is not, and being widely used is not the same thing. Either way, a tool you have never run costs you a token.

The tell: you cannot say what you would check first if it went down tonight.

The choice was about you, not the product It is the tool you want on your CV, or the one you have been curious about. That is a real and normal motivation, and it is not the company's money's motivation.

The tell: you cannot finish the sentence "the customer gets ___ from this choice".

Say it out loud

In plain words

Every tool costs you twice: once to learn it, and once when it breaks in a strange way at a bad moment. The second cost is the big one and you cannot see it when you choose. So use tools you have used before, where you already know what breaking looks like. Keep your risk for the product, because the product is the thing that is really going to surprise you.

One analogy, and where it breaks

You are driving in a foreign city at night, in the rain. You want the boring rental car — the one with the pedals where you expect them. The city is already the hard part; you do not need the car to be interesting too.

Where the analogy breaks. A rental car is a one-day commitment and you can swap it in an hour. A database is a multi-year commitment and swapping it is a project. So the real decision is heavier than the analogy suggests: choosing a boring car is cheap to undo, and choosing a boring database is a choice you are mostly stuck with.

The sticking point

Everyone hears "boring" as "old" or "worse". Both are wrong, and mixing them up loses the argument in the meeting.

Boring means the failure modes are known. Hearing it as "old" loses the argument in the meeting, because "old" is easy to argue against and "known" is not.

Words that mean you recited a book innovation tokens, battle-tested, bleeding edge, future-proof, best-in-class. Plain version: "I have run Postgres in production three times and I know what to look at when it slows down. I have never run the other one, so if it breaks on a Sunday I will be reading documentation instead of fixing it."
Teach it back

Explain in three sentences why the same tool can be a boring choice for one team and a risky choice for another.

Say it, then send it. I want to see the words failure modes or your own plain version of them. Without that, the explanation collapses into "use old things", which is not the argument.

Reveal a model answer — only after you have tried

"Boring is not about the age of the tool, it is about whether you already know what it looks like when it breaks. A team that has run this database for years has instincts — they know which three things to check first — so an outage costs them an hour. A team that has never run it has no instincts, so the same outage costs them a day of reading. Same tool, different risk, because the knowledge is in the team and not in the tool."

Drill the spoken version

Check yourself

Do it this week

Task 1 — count your tokens ~1 hr
  1. Write the stack for the product from B2. Every real choice: language, framework, database, hosting, deploy, auth, payments, background jobs, email, error tracking.
  2. Three columns: choice · have I run this in production? · token spent (Y/N).
  3. Add the tokens up.
  4. If the total is more than one, replace choices until it is one or zero. Write what you replaced and why.

Proof: the table, with a total of 0 or 1. Then one sentence about the token you kept. That sentence must say what the customer gets from it. Send it to me — most people undercount by two, because "industry standard" got counted as free.

Task 2 — the 3am test ~45 min
  1. For each row in your table, write the first thing you would check if it broke tonight and you were alone.
  2. Any row where you cannot write a real answer is a hidden token. Mark it and re-total.

Proof: one line per row. Blank lines are the finding — they are the tools you think you know and do not.

Remember this

  • Every tool costs twice: learning it, and debugging it later. The second cost is invisible and bigger.
  • Boring means the failure modes are known. Not old, not dull, not worse.
  • Novelty is a risk budget. About three tokens, and every unfamiliar tool spends one.
  • Boringness is a property of tool plus you. Ask "have I run this in production?"
  • The question that ends the argument: "what does this give us this month?"
  • Boring gives up some capability, some hiring appeal, and some future rework. Say so.
  • If the novel thing is the product, it is not a token — it is the business.

If you remember one thing: keep your risk for the product, because the product is the surprise you cannot avoid.
