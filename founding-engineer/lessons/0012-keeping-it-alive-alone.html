Deriving the shortest honest list

Step 1 — Every hour on operations is an hour not buying answers

You are one person. There is no separate infrastructure budget and no separate week. So the goal is not reliability. Reliability is a means.

Step 2 — The goal is: prevent the losses you cannot undo

Straight from the quality boundary in B4. Reversible failures cost you an afternoon. Irreversible ones can remove the customer who was going to give you the answer.

Step 3 — So list the failures you cannot undo. There are three

Irreversible lossWhat it actually looks like
You lose the data A bad migration, a wrong DELETE, a dropped table, a deleted account. The customer's work is gone and no code fixes that.
You lose money, quietly Charges that ran twice. Payments that never ran. Both fixable in code, neither undoable in someone's bank account or in their opinion of you.
You lose trust, silently It has been broken for nine days and nobody told you. You kept selling. That is the one that ends relationships.

Step 4 — Each of those maps to exactly one piece of work

Step 5 — Why the word is "restored", not "backed up"

An untested backup is a belief, not a backup. This failure is common, and it is usually found at the worst moment. The file exists, and it cannot be put back: wrong version, missing encryption key, missing extension, a schema that no longer matches.

The standard practice is a restore drill: actually recover into a scratch environment and confirm the data is complete and usable. Guidance recommends doing it on a schedule.1

Writing down the failed attempts too is our own addition, and it is the part that pays. A failed restore found during a drill is a task. The same failure found during an incident is a crisis.

Two numbers name what "restored" has to mean, and they are worth knowing by their real names:

NameThe question it answersA sane first answer
RPO
recovery point objective		 How much recent work may we lose? "Up to 24 hours" — so a nightly backup is enough.
RTO
recovery time objective		 How long may we be down while restoring? "Up to 4 hours" — so a manual restore is enough.

The wider rule of thumb is 3-2-1: three copies, on two kinds of storage, with one off-site. A modern version adds one immutable copy and zero errors on verified restores.1 For a one-person product, your managed database's automatic backup plus one copy you can download is usually enough — if you have restored it.

Step 6 — Why errors must reach a person

A silent failure is worse than an outage. During an outage nobody can use it and everybody knows. During a silent failure you keep selling something broken, and your customer finds out before you do.

The first action, and the one with the biggest effect, is error tracking.2 Install the SDK, deploy, and you have it.

Then send the alerts somewhere that interrupts you. The phone rule is ours, not the source's — that post suggests email or Slack. We think an inbox is where alerts go to die.

Then add uptime checks on the production URL and the one or two endpoints that matter.2 Then structured logs on the paths where silence hurts most: payments, sign-up, third-party integrations.2

Step 7 — Why a fearless deploy is operations work

This one looks like developer comfort and is not.

If deploying is scary, you deploy less. If you deploy less, each deploy contains more changes. Smaller changes have a lower failure rate, which is the best-established finding in this whole Lesson.3

So fear of deploying causes outages, by a mechanism, one step removed.

The bar is low: one command or one push, works on a Friday, and you can get back to the previous version. That is enough.

Cover the screen Name the three irreversible losses and the one piece of work that stops each. Then say why "restored" is the word that does the work in item one.

Step 8 — Write the panic note

One paragraph, saved somewhere you can find on your phone: how to check if it is up, where the logs are, how to roll back, how to restore. Its purpose is not completeness. Its purpose is to slow you down for thirty seconds so you think before typing under pressure.2

Step 9 — Where this reasoning stops

The moment there is a second engineer, or customers who lose money when you are down, the skipped column starts to apply. Not all at once — but the boundary moves, exactly as it did in B4. The list here is correct for one person with early users, and it is wrong for a company with a contract.

Where it goes wrong

The backup that exists and cannot be restored Nightly backups ran for eight months. On the day it mattered, the restore failed: the dump was made with a newer version of the database than the one available, and one extension was missing. The file was real. The recovery was not.

The tell: you cannot say how many minutes a restore takes, because you have never done one.

Alerts to an inbox nobody reads — fails quietly Error emails go to a shared address. There are two hundred of them. The one that said payments had been failing since Tuesday is between two hundred that do not matter.

The tell: your error emails are in a folder with a filter on it.

Alert fatigue, then silence You alerted on everything, got woken up for nothing three times, and muted the channel. Now you have the cost of monitoring and none of the benefit. Two loud alerts beat forty quiet ones.

The tell: you have muted a channel that was meant to wake you.

Friday fear, then the big release Nobody deploys on Friday, so Friday's work waits. Then Monday's release carries four days of changes, and when it breaks you cannot tell which change did it. The caution caused the incident.

The tell: your average release contains more than a day of work.

The most useful thing is not reading: run a restore drill this week and write down the number of minutes. That number is worth more than any article on this subject, including this one.

Say it out loud

In plain words

You are one person, so you cannot look after everything. Look after the things that cannot be fixed later. Data that is gone is gone. Money taken wrongly is already in someone's account. A product broken for a week without you knowing is already remembered. So: a backup you have actually put back, errors that reach your phone, and a deploy you are not scared of. Everything else can wait until there are more of you.

One analogy, and where it breaks

A house needs a smoke alarm and a way out. It does not need a fire brigade living in the spare room. You need to know quickly, and you need to get out.

Where the analogy breaks. A smoke alarm has a test button, and pressing it proves it works. Your backup has no test button. Nothing tells you it is broken until you need it. So you have to schedule the test yourself, and that is the whole reason "restored" is in the name of item one.

The sticking point

People treat the skipped list as a backlog they are behind on. It is not a backlog. It is a set of decisions.

An on-call rotation with one person is not a rotation. An error budget over eleven users is a number worked out from too few users to mean anything. Multi-region failover for a product that might be dead in six months spends the runway on a future you have not earned.

Being able to say that out loud, calmly, in an interview, is a stronger signal than having built any of it.

Words that mean you recited a book observability, SLO, error budget, five nines, blameless post-mortem, toil. Plain version, with your own numbers: "I restored the database into a scratch copy last Tuesday and timed it. Errors go to my phone. I can deploy with one command and roll back with another."
Teach it back

Explain in three sentences why a backup you have never restored does not count — and why being scared to deploy actually causes outages.

Say it, then send it. The second half is the one people miss. I want the mechanism: fear, batching, then blame.

Reveal a model answer — only after you have tried

"A backup file existing is not the same as being able to put it back — the version can be wrong, a key can be missing, the schema can have moved — and you find all of that out on the worst day unless you try it on a calm one. Being scared to deploy works the same way, one step removed: if deploying is frightening I do it less often, so each release carries more changes, and when something breaks I cannot tell which change did it. So the caution is what produced the outage."

Drill the spoken version

Check yourself

Do it this week

Task 1 — the restore drill ~2 hrs
  1. Take your production database backup and restore it into a scratch database. A local one is fine.
  2. Time it. Start a timer when you begin and stop it when you can read real data.
  3. Write down everything you needed that you did not have to hand: the version, the extensions, the key, the connection string, the roles.
  4. Write your RPO and RTO in one line each, as sentences a customer could read.

Proof: a number of minutes, and the list of things you were missing. Send me both. Almost nobody's first drill is clean, and the missing list is the actual output of this task.

Task 2 — make an error reach your phone ~1 hr
  1. Add error tracking to the deployed product from B4. A free tier is enough.
  2. Route alerts to something that interrupts you, not to email.
  3. Throw a real error on purpose, in production, and confirm the alert arrives.
  4. Then write the panic note: one paragraph — how to check it is up, where the logs are, how to roll back, how to restore.

Proof: a screenshot of the test alert on your phone, and the panic note. If the alert took more than five minutes to arrive, say so — that delay is a real property of your setup and you should know it.

Remember this

  • The goal is not reliability. It is preventing the losses you cannot undo.
  • Three irreversible losses: the data, money quietly, and trust silently.
  • Three answers, in order: a restored backup · errors that reach a person · a deploy you are not afraid of.
  • An untested backup is a belief. Run a drill, time it, write down what was missing.
  • RPO = how much work may be lost. RTO = how long you may be down. Write both.
  • Fear of deploying causes outages: fear → bigger batches → you cannot tell what broke it.
  • On-call, error budgets, multi-region and uptime targets are skipped on purpose. Say why.

If you remember one thing: you do not have a backup until you have put one back.
