Choosing which problem to build

Four cheap filters that kill most ideas in ten minutes, so you spend your runway on the one that survives.

Moves forward: judge someone else's work and say what is wrong and why. After this you can look at any startup and name the guess it never tested.  ·  Tag: core item 3 of 12.

The one win. Given five candidate problems, you can rank them in ten minutes and say out loud why the loser lost. No spreadsheet, no market research report.

Why a filter is needed at all

You have one runway and it can only test one problem properly. So the choice has to be made before the expensive part. That means the filters must be cheap: things you can check with the interview notes from F2 and nothing else.

Step 1 — What has to be true for anyone to buy anything

Someone only buys when they change their behaviour. They stop doing it the old way and start doing it your way. That is the whole event you are betting on.

Step 2 — Changing behaviour is not free

Switching costs them something: learning time, risk of it going wrong, explaining it to a colleague, admitting the old way was bad. Call that the switching cost.

So the condition is simple, and it is the whole Lesson in one line:

pain of the problem  >  cost of switching

Both sides are real. Most failed products had real pain on the left and never measured the right.

Keep that in mind as you read the four filters. Three of them measure the left side. The right side needs its own estimate: ask what the person would have to stop doing, learn, or explain to a colleague.

Step 3 — Therefore: frequency matters

A problem that happens once a year cannot build a habit. Even if it hurts a lot, the person forgets you between events. Each time, they start again from nothing.

A problem that happens weekly gives you many chances to be useful. And the switching cost is paid only once.

Step 4 — Therefore: the size of the pain matters

Measure pain in the only two units that are checkable: hours and money. "It is annoying" is not a unit. "It takes her four hours every Monday" is.

The best sign here is a workaround. If someone built a spreadsheet, hired a person, or wrote a script to cope, they already paid for a solution. That is proof of pain, not a report of it.

Step 5 — Therefore: someone must hold money

Pain with no budget produces no sale. Ask two separate questions and do not confuse them. Who feels the problem? Who signs for the fix? When those are two different people, your product has two customers and you must satisfy both.

Step 6 — The filter everyone forgets: can you reach them?

A group you cannot find is the same as no group. Before you commit, name the actual place: this subreddit, this conference, this trade body's member list, these forty companies on this page.

If you cannot name a place, the problem is not selectable yet, however real it is.

Step 7 — Multiply, do not add

Here is the important bit of arithmetic. Score each filter 0 to 5, where 0 means it fails outright. Then multiply the four scores.

A zero anywhere makes the whole score zero. Adding cannot do that. That is the only reason to multiply.

Cover the screen Name the four filters in order. Then say which of them measure the pain, and which measure whether the sale can happen at all. If you cannot say pain > switching cost without looking, read Step 2 again.

Step 8 — Three signs that look bad and are good

These three come from YC's talk on evaluating ideas. They look wrong at first, which is exactly why they are worth memorising.1

Looks badActually good, because
It looks like a lot of boring set-up work The boring work is what keeps competitors away. Most people see the work and stop, so nobody has done it properly.
The whole space is boring Boring spaces get less attention from founders. So the easy wins are still there, unlike in the exciting spaces.
Competitors already exist Somebody proved people pay for this. An empty market usually means no market, not an untapped one.

One more question from the same talk, which is not on that list but belongs here: do you have the problem yourself? If yes, you get free access to a real user. Be careful anyway — your own pain still has to pass all four filters.1

Step 9 — Where this reasoning stops

These filters rank problems. They cannot tell you whether you are the right person to solve one. That question is about unfair advantage — what you know, who you know, what you can build faster than others — and it is a separate judgement the filters do not make.

Where it goes wrong

Real pain, once a year Tax filing, annual audits, moving house. The pain is genuine and large, so it passes the pain filter easily. Then you discover you get one shot per customer per year, so learning is twelve months slow and habit never forms.

The tell: you can only describe the problem using the word "annual".

The market is "everyone" Small businesses. Developers. Teams. None of these is a place. You cannot name where to find them, so every user costs a new invention to reach.

The tell: you cannot finish the sentence "I will find the next ten of them at ______".

The passionate user with no budget A junior employee suffers most and cares most, so your interviews are warm and useful. They have no purchasing power. The person who signs has a different problem, and you never asked them.

The tell: your best three interviews are all with the same job title, and it is not a manager.

The engineer's trap — fails quietly You chose the problem you know how to build rather than the problem that hurts most. It feels responsible, because you are being realistic about your skills. It is backwards, and it stays hidden because the build goes well.

The tell: you decided the technology before you finished the interviews.

Say it out loud

In plain words

People only buy when they change how they work. Changing is uncomfortable, so the problem has to hurt more than the change does. That means you want problems that happen often, cost real hours or real money, belong to someone who can pay, and involve people you can actually find. Four cheap questions. Most ideas fail one of them, and one failure is enough.

One analogy, and where it breaks

You are choosing where to dig a well. You do not dig where the ground is softest. You dig where the water is.

Where the analogy breaks. With ground water you can survey the land before you dig. With a market you cannot. The only survey available is watching who is already carrying buckets by hand. That is why a workaround is the strongest signal in this Lesson: it is a person carrying a bucket.

The sticking point

Almost everyone treats the four filters as a checklist to add up. They are a product, not a sum.

Here is why that matters. An idea scoring 5, 5, 5, 1 sums to 16 out of 20 and looks strong. Multiplied it is 125 against a maximum of 625, and the 1 is the reason. That 1 might be "I cannot find these people". No amount of pain fixes that.

Words that mean you recited a book TAM, market opportunity, pain point, underserved segment, white space. Plain version: "Six of the eight people I spoke to were doing this by hand every Monday, it took about three hours, and their operations manager already pays for two other tools."
Teach it back

Explain in three sentences why a problem that hurts a lot can still be a bad problem to build for.

Say it, then send it. I am looking for whether you name a specific filter as the failure, not just "it depends".

Reveal a model answer — only after you have tried

"Pain is only half the condition. The other half is what it costs someone to change how they work, and how often they get the chance. A problem that hurts badly once a year gives me one shot per customer per year, so I learn slowly and they never build a habit. And if the person who feels it cannot sign for the fix, the pain never turns into money."

Drill the spoken version

Check yourself

Do it this week

Task 1 — score five problems ~2 hrs
  1. From your F2 notes, list up to five problems you heard. Real ones people described, never ones you invented. Three real problems beats five with two inventions in them.
  2. Build a table: one row per problem, four score columns, one product column.
  3. Score each filter 0 to 5, where 0 means it fails outright. Beside every score write the evidence from your notes. A score with no evidence is a guess, so mark it as one.
  4. Multiply. Write one sentence for the winner and one for the loser saying which filter decided it.

Proof: the table, plus this sentence completed for the winner: "I will find the next ten of these people at ______." If you cannot finish that sentence, the winner has a 1 in the reach column and you scored it wrong.

Task 2 — hunt for the workaround ~90 min
  1. Take the winning problem.
  2. Go to the place you named and find three examples of people doing it by hand: a shared spreadsheet, a forum post asking how, a job advert for someone to do exactly this, a freelancer selling it as a service.
  3. Screenshot each one.

Proof: three screenshots of people already paying in time or money. If you cannot find any in ninety minutes, that is a real result: go back to your second-place problem and say why.

Remember this

  • Nobody buys unless they change behaviour, and changing costs them something.
  • The condition is pain > switching cost. Most failures never measured the right side.
  • Four cheap filters: frequency, pain in hours or money, who signs, can you reach them.
  • Multiply the scores, 0 to 5. A single 0 is fatal, and adding hides it.
  • A workaround someone built is the strongest signal available. It is proof of pain, not a report of it.
  • Three signs that look bad and are good: boring set-up work, a boring space, existing competitors.
  • These filters rank problems. They cannot tell you whether you are the right person to solve one.

If you remember one thing: you must be able to name the place. If you cannot, you have not chosen a problem yet.
