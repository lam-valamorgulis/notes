Why nobody arrives on their own

Here is the day this Lesson is about. The product works. It is deployed. You have a URL. Zero people are using it.

Most engineers respond by building: a landing page, a blog, a launch plan. Let us derive why that is the wrong instinct.

Step 1 — Every way people normally find products needs a stock you do not have

How people normally arriveWhat it needs firstDo you have it?
SearchPages, links, and months of ageNo
Word of mouthExisting happy usersNo
ReputationA track recordNo
Paid adsMoney, and a page that convertsNo, and unproven

Three of these four multiply something you do not have. Zero times anything is zero.

Ads are the exception: they turn money into visitors. But they need a page that converts, and yours is untested. So the result is unknown, not zero — and unknown is not an answer you can spend.

Step 2 — Therefore the first users must be fetched, not attracted

You go and get them, individually, by hand. Paul Graham's essay is the canonical statement: the most common unscalable thing founders have to do at the start is recruit users manually, and nearly all startups have to.1

Step 3 — "It does not scale" is not an objection

This is where engineers get stuck, so let us be exact about it.

You are not trying to build a user acquisition system. You are trying to buy an answer.

Ten users bought by hand answer the question "does anyone want this at all?" They cannot tell you a rate — what share of people stay, or how often something breaks. That is fine. The rate question is not the one you are stuck on this month.

So the unscalable work is not a temporary compromise. It is research. Graham's own words for it are "a necessary part of the feedback loop that makes the product good".1 The words "research" and "sample" are ours.

Step 4 — Therefore over-serve them, deliberately

Now a second consequence, and it is the one that wins. With ten users you can do things no large company can afford to do:

  • Set the product up for them, in a call, while they watch.
  • Reply in minutes, not days.
  • Fix their bug the same afternoon and tell them it is fixed.
  • Ask them what happened, every week, by name.

YC has a name for the extreme version, from Stripe's founders: the "Collison installation" — when someone said they would try it, the founders offered to set it up on their laptop right then.1

Step 5 — Ten who love it beats a thousand who shrug

Mechanical reason, not a sentimental one. People who love your product complain in detail. They tell you exactly what is broken and what they wish it did, because they now depend on it. People who are mildly fine with it say nothing and quietly stop. Silence contains no information.

Cover the screen Say why every normal acquisition channel produces nothing at zero users. Then say why "it does not scale" is not an objection to doing this.

Step 6 — Where to find the ten

You already did this work. In F3 you had to finish the sentence "I will find the next ten of these people at ______". That named place is your list. And the people you interviewed in F2 are the warmest ten names you will ever have — they already told you the problem is real.

Step 7 — Where this reasoning stops

Two limits, and the first is the important one.

Hand-recruiting stops working as a growth strategy at some point. No source names the number, and it depends on your price and your market. You will feel it when your week is all messages and no product.

At that point you do need a repeatable channel, and picking one is a real skill. It is on the depth queue for exactly that reason: it is a good problem that you have to earn.

Second, over-serving distorts what you learn. If you set the product up for every user in person, you cannot tell whether the sign-up flow works. That is an acceptable trade at ten users — desire first, self-service later — but know that you are making it.

1 Sourced: Paul Graham, "Do Things that Don't Scale" (2013) — manual recruiting and the Collison installation. Graham calls the manual work "a necessary part of the feedback loop that makes the product good". The words research, sample and instrument are this course's, not his. Links checked 2026-08-23.

Where it goes wrong

Built a landing page and waited — fails quietly A good page, a waiting list form, a launch tweet. Fourteen emails collected over three weeks. It feels like marketing, so nobody calls it avoidance. It is avoidance: writing a page is comfortable and asking a named person is not.

The tell: you have a landing page and you have not sent ten personal messages.

Burned the big list too early You had one good channel — a mailing list, a community you belong to, a conference talk — and you used it on a version that was not ready. Two hundred people came once. Now the channel is spent and the people remember it as bad.

The tell: your biggest launch happened before ten people had used it twice.

Automated onboarding at five users A welcome email sequence, an in-app tour, a help centre. All of it built to avoid the conversations that were the actual point. At five users the tour replaced the only research instrument you had.

The tell: you know your activation rate and you have not spoken to a user this week.

"We need marketing" Said in month two, with nine users, none of whom were asked what they thought. Sometimes true later. At this stage it is almost always a way of saying that asking people is uncomfortable.

The tell: you cannot name your last five users.

Say it out loud

In plain words

At the start, nobody knows you exist and nothing brings people to you. Search, word of mouth and reputation all need something you already have. Ads need a page you have not tested. So you go and find people one at a time and ask them. That does not work at a big scale, and it does not need to — you only need ten, because ten people using it for real tells you what you need to know.

One analogy, and where it breaks

It is the first day of a market stall. You hand the food to one person and watch them eat it. Their face tells you more than a survey of a thousand people.

Where the analogy breaks. At a market, strangers walk past you for free — the crowd is provided. Online, nobody walks past you at all. There is no footfall. So you have to do a step the stall holder never does: find each person and bring them to the stall yourself.

The sticking point

Engineers hear "does not scale" as "wrong". It is a habit from building systems, where a solution that only handles ten cases is a bad solution.

Flip the frame. You are not building a system here, you are running an experiment. A small study answers a small question. That is not a fault in the study — it is what it was built for.

The ten are your instrument for answering one question. They are also your first ten real customers. You stop using the method later, not the people.

Words that mean you recited a book growth, funnel, acquisition channel, go to market, early adopters, product-led growth. Plain version: "I wrote to twenty people I found in that forum, one message each, no template. Nine replied, four are using it, and two of those I set up myself on a call."
Teach it back

Explain in three sentences why building a landing page is the wrong first move when you have zero users.

Say it, then send it. I am looking for the multiplication idea — that a channel needs a stock you do not have. If your answer is only "talking to people is better", the reasoning is not there yet.

Reveal a model answer — only after you have tried

"A landing page is a machine for converting visitors, and I have no visitors. Every normal way of getting them — search, word of mouth, reputation — multiplies something I already have, and right now that is zero, so the answer stays zero however good the page is. The only source of users that does not need an existing stock is me writing to a named person and asking, which is why that is the first move and the page can wait."

Drill the spoken version

Check yourself

Do it this week

Task 1 — twenty names, ten messages ~4 hrs
  1. Open the place you named in F3. Write down twenty real names: people or companies who have the problem. Real names, not categories.
  2. Write to ten of them. One at a time. No template — reference something specific and true about them in the first sentence.
  3. Ask for one thing only: fifteen minutes, or permission to set it up for them.
  4. Log every reply verbatim in a file, including the refusals and the silence.

Proof: a list of twenty names, ten sent messages, and a reply log. Send me the log. The refusals are the part I want to read — the exact words of a "no" usually contain the reason your positioning is wrong.

Task 2 — set one up by hand ~2 hrs
  1. Get one person on a call and set the product up for them while they watch.
  2. Do not explain what they should do. Stay quiet and watch where they hesitate.
  3. Write down every place they paused or asked "what does this mean?"

Proof: a list of the hesitations. That list is your next week of work, in priority order, and you did not have to guess it.

Remember this

  • Every acquisition channel multiplies a stock you already have. At zero, they all return zero.
  • So the first users are fetched, not attracted. One at a time, by hand, by name.
  • "It does not scale" is not an objection. The unscalable work is research.
  • The ten are your instrument for one question, and your first real customers. Drop the method, not the people.
  • Over-serve them: set it up for them, reply in minutes, fix it the same day.
  • Ten who love it beat a thousand who shrug, because only love produces detailed complaints.
  • Log the refusals. The exact words of a "no" usually name the real problem.

If you remember one thing: if you cannot name your last five users, you do not have a marketing problem.
