Appetite, not estimate

Stop asking how long it will take. Ask how much time it is worth, then design something that fits.

Month 5 of 12 — Time as the fixed thing

The question this month answers: How much time is this problem worth, before we know how long it takes?

This is the month’s core Lesson. Three more Lessons finish the month — they are named here so you can see what is still missing:

  • Shaping, before anyone bets on it — rough sketches at the right altitude: concrete enough to build, loose enough to cut.
  • The scope hammer and the circuit breaker — cutting inside the cycle, and why an unfinished bet does not get an extension.
  • The cycle when the team is one person — six weeks with no designer, no QA and nobody to hand off to.

The gate: The pitch has an appetite in weeks and a written list of what is out. A pitch with no “no” in it has not been shaped.

Why estimates fail here specifically

Estimates are not evil. They work fine on work that is understood. The problem is that startup work is not understood yet, and there is a structural reason.

Step 1 — Scope is discovered while doing the work

When you start a new feature you do not know the edge cases, the third-party surprises, or the two extra screens the flow will turn out to need. You find them by building. That is not bad planning; it is the nature of new work.

Step 2 — So an estimate is a guess about an unknown quantity

You are asked "how long?" while the "what" is still moving. So the number you give is a guess about something not yet defined.

Step 3 — Two variables, and you only get to fix one

Here is the step that makes the whole Lesson click. Every piece of work has scope and time. You can fix one of them. The other must be allowed to move.

You fix…Then this moves…How bad can it get?
Scope (an estimate) Time Unbounded. Six weeks becomes six months. There is no natural stop.
Time (an appetite) Scope Bounded. Worst case you ship a smaller thing on the day you said.

Fix the scope, and the risk sits on the variable you cannot control. Fix the time, and the risk sits on the one you can. Cutting scope is a decision, not an accident.

Step 4 — Therefore: fix the time first

That fixed time is the appetite: how much time this problem is worth. It comes from the value of solving it, not from the design of the solution. Basecamp's Shape Up is where this is named and worked out properly.1

Two sentences that show the difference:

Estimate

"This will take about six weeks."

Appetite

"This is worth two weeks to us. What can we build in two weeks that solves most of it?"

Step 5 — Therefore the design comes after the number

This feels backwards the first time. You set the budget, then you design to fit it. In Shape Up that design step is called shaping: roughing out a solution at the level of "these three screens and this rule", not at the level of a specification.1

Cover the screen Say why fixing scope produces an unbounded overrun, and fixing time does not. Then say where the appetite number comes from. If you said "from the design", read Step 4 again.

Step 6 — Appetite without permission is just a deadline

This is the part most people skip, and it is the part that decides whether the idea helps you or hurts you.

If the time is fixed and the scope is fixed, you have not got an appetite. You have a fixed deadline with a nicer name. So when you accept an appetite, you must say the trade out loud, in the same conversation:

Say this when you accept an appetite

"Two weeks works. To hold that date I will be cutting things as I go, and I will tell you what I cut. If everything on this list has to ship, then it is not a two-week job and we should talk about the value instead."

Step 7 — Name the rabbit holes before you start

A rabbit hole is a part of the work that could quietly use up the whole budget: a data migration, a third-party integration with bad documentation, an edge case in permissions. Shape Up puts these in the pitch on purpose, so nobody discovers one in week five.1

Write them down before you start. A named risk is a decision. An unnamed one is a surprise you cannot plan for.

Step 8 — What is convention, and where this stops

Two honest notes.

Convention, not logic: Basecamp's six-week cycle. Six is their company's choice. The reasoning behind fixing the time is general; the specific length is not. Two weeks, three weeks and six weeks are all fine appetites.

Where it stops: appetite does not work when the scope genuinely cannot move. A tax rule, a payment integration that must be complete or not exist, a legal deadline. There, the honest answer is an estimate with a wide range, and you say which parts are the unknowns.

1 Sourced: Ryan Singer, Shape Up (free online) — appetite, shaping, the pitch, rabbit holes. The sentence to say in Step 6 is this course's own: Shape Up assumes the team already has permission to cut. Links checked 2026-08-23.

Where it goes wrong

Appetite without the authority to cut The team is given six weeks and the full feature list. Now both variables are fixed, so the only thing left to move is quality and people's evenings. This is the most common way the idea gets misused, and it is worse than plain estimating.

The tell: nobody can name a single thing that is allowed to be cut.

"Six weeks" heard as a promise — fails quietly You said two weeks meaning an appetite. The founder wrote it in a customer email meaning a delivery date for everything discussed. Nobody lied. The word did two jobs. It stays hidden until the demo.

The tell: you never said the sentence in Step 6 out loud.

No rabbit holes named, so week five finds one Everything went well until the import needed to handle their old system's date format, and that turned out to be four days of work. It was foreseeable in week one by anyone who asked "what part of this scares me?"

The tell: your plan has no section listing what could use up the budget.

Appetite set by excitement, not by value You gave it six weeks because it is the interesting problem, not because solving it is worth six weeks of the company's runway. The number has to come from the value side of the table.

The tell: you cannot say what the company gets for the six weeks in one sentence.

Skip the parts about Basecamp's own team setup — about fifty people, with project teams of one designer and one or two programmers. The reasoning transfers; their team shape does not.

Say it out loud

In plain words

Nobody can say how long new work will take, because you find out what the work is by doing it. So stop trying. Instead decide how much time the problem is worth, and then build the best thing that fits in that time. If it does not all fit, you cut, and you say what you cut.

One analogy, and where it breaks

It is packing a suitcase. The suitcase is a fixed size. You do not measure your clothes and then buy a bag; you take the bag and choose what goes in. Choosing is the work.

Where the analogy breaks. Two ways, and both matter. You can buy a bigger suitcase; you cannot buy more runway. And you can see all your clothes laid out on the bed before you pack — you cannot see all the work before you start it. So real packing is easier than this. Leave room.

The sticking point

People think cutting scope means cutting quality. It does not.

You cut whole features. You do not cut the care inside the features you keep. Half a feature built well is a feature. A whole feature built badly is a bug report.

That distinction is what makes an appetite survivable. If holding the date meant shipping something broken, nobody would agree to one.

Words that mean you recited a book timeboxing, right-sizing, scope creep, circuit breaker, story points. Plain version: "this is worth two weeks to us. Here is what I think fits in two weeks, and here are the three things I have left out on purpose."
Teach it back

Explain in three sentences why fixing the time is safer than fixing the scope — without using the words "timebox" or "agile".

Say it out loud, then send it. I am looking for the word unbounded or its plain equivalent, because that is the actual argument.

Reveal a model answer — only after you have tried

"You always find out what the work really is while you are doing it, so one of scope and time has to be allowed to move. If I fix the scope, the time moves and there is no limit on how far — nothing in the plan tells me when to stop. If I fix the time, the scope moves instead, and the worst case is that I ship something smaller on the day I promised, which is a decision I made rather than a surprise."

Drill the spoken version

Check yourself

Do it this week

Task 1 — write a one-page pitch ~2 hrs
  1. Take the winning problem from F3 and pick one slice of it.
  2. Write a single page with exactly these five headings: Problem · Appetite · Solution sketch · Rabbit holes · Not doing.
  3. Set the appetite first, before you write the solution. Justify the number from value, in one sentence.
  4. The solution sketch stays rough on purpose: boxes and arrows, or three sentences per screen. No field lists.

Proof: a page a non-engineer could read in three minutes and say yes or no to. Send it to me. The most common fault is a solution sketch that has quietly become a specification — I will point at where it happened.

Task 2 — say the trade out loud ~20 min
  1. Record yourself saying the Step 6 sentence, in your own words, as if a founder just gave you a two-week appetite and a nine-item list.
  2. Then record the harder version: the founder pushes back and says all nine items are required.

Proof: two recordings. In the second one, check that you moved the conversation to value rather than to hours. That is the move that keeps the room.

Remember this

  • You discover the real scope while doing the work. So one of scope and time must move.
  • Fix scope → time overruns are unbounded. Fix time → the worst case is a smaller ship.
  • Appetite = how much time this is worth, decided from value, before any design.
  • Shaping comes after the number: rough boxes and rules, not a specification.
  • Appetite without permission to cut is a deadline. Say the trade out loud when you accept it.
  • Name the rabbit holes in week one. A named risk is a decision; an unnamed one is an ambush.
  • Six weeks is Basecamp's convention, not a law. And appetite does not work when scope truly cannot move.

If you remember one thing: never answer "how long will it take?" — answer "how much is it worth?" instead.
