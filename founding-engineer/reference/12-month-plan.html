The 12-Month Plan

Twelve months, twelve core ideas, forty-eight Lessons. One month is the unit of work, and every month has to end in something a stranger can look at.

Why the course is a year. It is not a round number. A seed startup with $600k in the bank and $50k a month of burn has twelve months of runway. That is arithmetic, and it is the same arithmetic taught in F1. So the course runs on the clock it teaches: month 1 of the course is month 1 of the runway. By month 12 you either have a product strangers pay for, or you killed it on purpose and can say exactly why.
Whose idea this shape is The twelve-month shape, the month titles, and the gates on this page are ours. No source says "do discovery for four months". The runway arithmetic is Blank's search-versus-execute framing plus simple division; the individual ideas inside each month are sourced on their own Lesson pages and in RESOURCES.md.

How a month works

Every month has the same four parts. Nothing else.

PartWhat it is
One core LessonThe idea the month is built on. Twelve core ideas, one per month. These are the 20% — see MISSION.md.
Three support LessonsThe parts that make the core idea real work instead of a nice sentence.
One artefactA thing that exists at the end of the month. A memo, a page, a deploy, a payment. Not a feeling of progress.
One gateA sentence that must be true before month N+1 starts. If it is not true, you repeat the month or you use the kill rule.

At 20 hours a week, a month is about 80 hours. Most of that is the artefact, not the reading. Four Lessons cost maybe eight hours to read. The other seventy-two are talking to people, cutting scope, and shipping.

Reading order is the month order. The Lessons are chained: month 3 uses the interview notes from month 2, month 6 uses the problem from month 3, month 12 needs the product from month 8.

Phase 1 · Months 1–4 · Track 1 — Find

Four months and not one line of product code. You are buying one answer: is there a real problem here, and is it worth the other eight months? You still ship every month — a memo, a landing page, a fake door — but never the product.

Month 1 1 of 4 live

The seat and the timer

Before anything else: what the job is, how long you have, and what you are actually being paid in.

The question: how many months are there, and what does a month have to buy?

  • F1 · The job is not writing code — the runway is a timer, so the output of a month is an answer. live
  • Runway, burn, and the cost of a month — reading a real set of numbers, and what "we have some time" hides.
  • Why startups die, and which causes you control — the honest list, split into the ones an engineer can move and the ones they cannot.
  • The offer: equity, vesting, cliff, dilution — read this before you sign. The usual ranges are convention, not law.

Artefact: one page — cash, burn, months left, and the three questions the company most needs answered.
Gate: you can say the company's runway in months, out loud, without looking it up.

Month 2 1 of 4 live

Talking to people without poisoning it

The hardest skill in the course, and the one everyone thinks they already have.

The question: what actually hurts, in words the person used first?

  • F2 · The Mom Test — talk about their life, not your idea. Ask about the last time, never about the future. live
  • Finding twenty strangers to talk to — where they are, the first message, and why a warm intro is worse than it looks.
  • Notes that survive the week — recording, tagging and storing a conversation so month 3 can use it.
  • Reading the transcript — separating a fact from a compliment, and what to do with the enthusiastic person who never buys.

Artefact: twenty conversations, written up, with the exact words people used for the pain.
Gate: you can point at three conversations where you learned something that surprised you. Nothing surprising means you were pitching.

Month 3 1 of 4 live

Choosing the problem

Twenty conversations produce more than one problem. This month picks one and kills the rest.

The question: which of these problems is worth a year?

  • F3 · Choosing which problem to build — frequency, pain, who signs, can you reach them. Score 0 to 5 and multiply. live
  • The one-page problem statement — writing it so the person with the problem reads it and says "yes, that is it".
  • How small is too small — counting the buyers from the bottom up. No market-sizing slides.
  • Competitors, and why "nobody does this" is bad news — an empty space usually means people tried and it did not work.

Artefact: one problem statement, one page, plus the scoring table showing the problems you rejected and why.
Gate: a person with the problem read your page and did not correct you.

Month 4 1 of 4 live

Deciding to commit, or not

The month that makes the difference between a decision and a drift.

The question: what evidence would make us stop, and have we tested the riskiest guess yet?

  • F4 · The kill rule — the number, the date and the action, written while you are still calm. live
  • The riskiest assumption, and the cheapest test — finding the one belief that, if false, makes everything else pointless.
  • Fake doors, landing pages and concierge tests — shipping a test that is not the product, and the honesty line you do not cross.
  • The go / no-go memo, and saying no out loud — the sentences to use when the answer is no and the founder is in the room.

Artefact: a signed memo — the guess, the test, the result, the decision, the kill rule, and the date it gets checked.
Gate: the kill rule has a number and a date on it. "It feels wrong" is not a kill rule.

Phase 2 · Months 5–8 · Track 2 — Build

Now you build, with the objective function changed. You stop asking "how long will this take?" and start asking "how much time is this worth?" The month-8 deploy is the point of the whole phase.

Month 5 1 of 4 live

Time as the fixed thing

The one habit that stops a six-week feature becoming a six-month feature.

The question: how much time is this problem worth, before we know how long it takes?

  • B1 · Appetite, not estimate — fix the time, vary the scope. A fixed-scope overrun has no limit. live
  • Shaping, before anyone bets on it — rough sketches at the right altitude: concrete enough to build, loose enough to cut.
  • The scope hammer and the circuit breaker — cutting inside the cycle, and the rule that an unfinished bet does not get an extension.
  • The cycle when the team is one person — what six weeks looks like with no designer, no QA and no one to hand off to.

Artefact: one shaped pitch — the problem, the appetite, a rough solution, the rabbit holes, and the things explicitly not doing.
Gate: the pitch has an appetite in weeks and a written list of what is out. A pitch with no "no" in it has not been shaped.

Month 6 1 of 4 live

Cutting

Deciding the smallest thing that still answers the question, and saying out loud what is missing.

The question: what is the least we can build and still learn the thing?

  • B2 · Cutting to the MVP — the removal test and the answer test. A human doing it by hand is a valid implementation. live
  • Buy, glue, or build — the make-or-buy call, and the three questions that settle it in ten minutes.
  • Design and brand with no designer — enough taste to not look untrustworthy, using other people's decisions.
  • The three-minute demo — the script, and why the demo gets written before the software.

Artefact: two lists on one page — in, and deliberately out. The "out" list should be the longer one.
Gate: you can give the three-minute demo of a product that does not exist yet, and someone follows it.

Month 7 1 of 4 live

The stack

Choosing the tools, and spending your small budget of new things where it actually wins.

The question: which parts of this are we allowed to find interesting?

  • B3 · Boring stack and innovation tokens — about three novel choices, and a database is a terrible place to spend one. live
  • One box, and what it can carry — the honest capacity of a single server and a single database, with numbers.
  • The tables you will regret — the few data-model decisions that are expensive to reverse, and the many that are not.
  • AI features: the product, or a button — when a model is the thing being sold, and when it is a feature that must fail quietly.

Artefact: a one-page stack decision — every choice, and for each one the sentence "I have run this in production" or "this is a token I am spending".
Gate: you spent three tokens or fewer, and you can name what each one buys.

Month 8 1 of 4 live

Shipping something real

The biggest month in the course. At the end of it there is a URL a stranger can open.

The question: does the whole path work end to end, for one real case?

  • B4 · The walking skeleton and the quality boundary — deploy on day one, then go fast everywhere except where a failure loses data or trust. live
  • Auth, payments and email — the three you do not write yourself, and what you still have to understand about each.
  • One database, many customers — tenant isolation done simply, and the query that leaks everyone's data.
  • Shipping alone, continuously — environments, migrations with no downtime, and a flag you can turn off from your phone.

Artefact: a live URL. One real user journey, start to finish, on the internet, with a real domain.
Gate: someone who is not you completed the whole journey without being told what to click.

Phase 3 · Months 9–12 · Track 3 — Live

A product nobody uses has taught you nothing. Four months of users, money, one number, and the operations work that stops you losing what you built.

Month 9 1 of 4 live

The first users

One at a time, by hand, watching them fail in front of you.

The question: will a stranger get through it without you in the room?

  • L1 · The first ten users, by hand — every channel multiplies a stock you do not have, so at zero they all return zero. live
  • Onboarding one person at a time — sitting with them, saying nothing, and writing down every place they stopped.
  • Support that does not eat your week — the inbox, the canned answers, and turning a repeated question into a fix.
  • The weekly learning review — one hour, one page: what did this week's users teach us, and what changes because of it.

Artefact: ten real users, plus a refusal log — everyone who said no, and the exact reason.
Gate: at least one user came back a second week without being asked.

Month 10 1 of 4 live

Money

Charging is a test, not a reward. This is the month the test runs.

The question: will anyone move money for this?

  • L2 · The first price — never ask "would you pay?". Say "it is $X, shall I send the link?" and then be quiet. live
  • Taking the money — payments, invoices, tax, refunds, and failed cards. The boring half of revenue.
  • Pricing v2: tiers, packaging, expansion — what to change once the first price has been tested, and what not to touch.
  • The first churn, and the exit interview — the most useful conversation in the course, and the one everyone avoids.

Artefact: a payment in the account from someone who is not a friend — or, if nobody paid, the written record of every refusal.
Gate: you said a price out loud to a stranger's face and did not apologise for it.

Month 11 1 of 4 live

The number

One number for this stage. The other thirty are noise until this one moves.

The question: which single number tells us whether this is working?

  • L3 · The one metric that matters — one number per stage: empathy, stickiness, virality, revenue, scale. live
  • Instrumentation you will not regret — the few events worth naming carefully, funnels, and cohorts that do not lie.
  • Growth channels after hand-recruiting — picking one channel, testing it properly, and killing it on a date.
  • A/B testing once you finally have traffic — the traffic you need before a test means anything, and what to do below it.

Artefact: one number on a dashboard, measured every week for four weeks, with the change you made to move it.
Gate: you can say why this number and not the obvious one, and what would make you switch.

Month 12 1 of 4 live

Staying alive

The smallest set of operations work that stops you losing data or losing trust — plus the honest list of what you skip.

The question: can this survive a bad week without you awake for it?

  • L4 · Keeping it alive alone — a backup you have restored, errors that reach your phone, a deploy you are not scared of. live
  • The first security review — SOC 2, GDPR and the questionnaire, from the point of view of one engineer with no compliance team.
  • Scaling past one box — queues, caching and read replicas, and why this is a problem you have to earn.
  • Handing it over: engineers 2 to 5 — the month your habits become the company's habits, whether you wrote them down or not.

Artefact: a restore you actually performed, dated. An error that woke your phone. A deploy done on a Friday afternoon on purpose.
Gate: you restored a backup into a clean environment and the product worked. Not "we have backups". You restored one.

What is not in the year

Two items from the depth queue are deliberately outside the twelve months, because raising money is not the goal of this course:

  • Fundraising: what a seed deck has to prove. Useful as a skill, wrong as a purpose.
  • Investor and board reporting. The same reason.

If you want either one early, say so. The Lesson will name which month it is jumping over.

If a month fails

A gate that does not close is information, not shame. Three legal moves, and you must pick one out loud:

MoveWhen it is the right one
Repeat the monthThe work was thin. You read four Lessons and shipped nothing. Do the artefact this time.
Go back a monthThe gate failed because the month before it was wrong. Ten users refuse to come back because the problem in month 3 was not real.
Use the kill ruleThe evidence you wrote down in month 4 has arrived. Stop, and write what you learned.

The one move that is not legal is carrying on with the gate unclosed and hoping month N+1 fixes it. That is how twelve months become one long unanswered question.

Reading order is the month order. If you are not sure where you are, you are in the earliest month whose gate has not closed.
