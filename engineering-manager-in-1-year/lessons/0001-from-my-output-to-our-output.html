From "My Output" to "Our Output"

The one idea the whole year rests on. Get this, and every other manager skill has a reason to exist.

Your win today: you'll be able to say, in one sentence, how a manager's job is different from a senior engineer's — and why the habits that made you great so far can quietly work against you now.

The one shift

As a senior engineer, your value is simple to see: what you build. Good code, solved problems, shipped features. You are measured by your own output, and you have spent years making that output excellent.

A manager is measured by something different: what the team builds. Andrew Grove, who ran Intel, put it as close to a formula as management gets:

Andrew Grove · High Output Management

"A manager's output = the output of their organization + the output of the neighboring organizations under their influence."

Notice what is not in that formula: your own code. Your personal work still exists, but it is now a small part of the total. Your real job is to raise the output of everyone around you. This is called leverage: one hour you spend can now change many hours of other people's work — for better or worse.1

Example

Priya manages six engineers on a billing team. Every morning two or three of them lost an hour to a test suite that failed at random. She stopped her own coding for two days, split the flaky tests out of the main run, and gave each one a named owner. Her commits that week were close to zero — and the team got about ten hours a week back.

What "a good day" looks like now

Because the measure changed, the feeling of a good day changes too. This table is the whole transition in miniature — read it slowly.

As a senior engineer…As a manager…
A good day = you shipped something.A good day = you removed a blocker so they could ship.
You go deep and uninterrupted.Interruptions are the work — you are the unblocker.
You are the expert with answers.You ask questions so others build the answer.
Success is visible in your commits.Success is mostly invisible and shows up weeks later.
You optimize your own focus time.You optimize the team's focus time.
Example

Marco runs a four-person platform team. On Thursday he wrote no code — he settled a spec argument with the data team, chased a blocked API key, and spent thirty minutes with a new hire on a review comment she did not understand. It felt like a day of pure interruption. Three weeks later that work shipped on time, and none of it showed up in his commit history.

Reframe You are not giving up building. You are changing what you build — from software to a team that builds software well. That is a real craft with its own deep skill.

Why this is genuinely hard

Two forces make this shift uncomfortable, and knowing them helps you push through:

  • You lose your fastest feedback loop. Code gives a dopamine hit: tests pass, the feature works, today. Management feedback is slow and fuzzy — a person grows over months. Your brain will crave the quick win and pull you back to coding.2
  • Your identity is on the line. "Best engineer in the room" was your identity for years. Now the best thing you can do is not be that person — and let someone slower learn by doing. That feels like going backwards. It isn't.

Camille Fournier calls the early management job a series of these deliberate letting-gos. The engineers who struggle most are the ones who try to stay the top IC and manage — they end up as a bottleneck for both. (We'll name that trap directly in Lesson 3.)3

Practice: read the situation, pick the move

Management is judgment under real conditions. So every lesson ends with a drill: a true-to-life situation, then a choice. Read it, decide out loud before you look, then check.

Scenario

It's your first week as manager of your old team. A ticket you'd normally grab yourself — a tricky bug you could fix in an hour — lands in the backlog. A mid-level engineer picks it up and estimates two days. The sprint is tight.

Remember this
  • Your measure of success moves from your output to the team's output.
  • Grove: a manager's output = the output of their team + the teams they influence.
  • Leverage means one of your hours can change many of theirs — so spend it on them.
  • A good day is now removing a blocker, not shipping a feature yourself.
  • It feels hard because you lose the fast dopamine of code and part of your "best engineer" identity.
  • Trying to stay the top IC and manage makes you a bottleneck for both.

If you remember one thing: you now build the team that builds the software.
