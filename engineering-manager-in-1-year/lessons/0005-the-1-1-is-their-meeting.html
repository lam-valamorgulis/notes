The 1:1 Is Their Meeting

One recurring 30–60 minutes is the highest-leverage habit in management. Most new managers waste it by turning it into a status update.

Your win today: you'll know exactly who owns the 1:1, what belongs in it, and the small set of mechanics — shared agenda, visible notes, actions reviewed — that turn it from a chat into the meeting that surfaces problems early.

Why this one meeting beats the rest

Month 1 taught the shift: your output is now the team's output, and your tool is leverage. The 1:1 is the clearest example of leverage in the whole job. Andy Grove did the arithmetic:

Andrew Grove · High Output Management

"Ninety minutes of your time can enhance the quality of your subordinate's work for two weeks, or for some eighty-plus hours."

That is the trade: about 1.5 hours of your attention against 80 hours of someone's work. Nothing else on your calendar has that ratio. The research points the same way — Gallup found that employees whose managers hold regular meetings with them are almost three times as likely to be engaged as employees whose managers do not.1

Whose meeting is it?

Theirs. This is the single idea that decides whether the habit works. Grove wrote that a 1:1 "should be regarded as the subordinate's meeting, with its agenda and tone set by him," and sent ahead of time so the manager can prepare.2 James Stanier puts a number on it: the report should drive about 70% of the conversation.3

So your job in the room is not to fill the time. It is to listen, ask, and remove things that are in their way. If you did most of the talking, the meeting failed — even if it felt productive to you.

What a 1:1 is not

Michael Lopp (Rands) is blunt about it: "A 1:1 is not a project meeting. A 1:1 is not a status report."4 Status can be read in a ticket board. What cannot be read anywhere is how the person is doing, what they are worried about, and the problem they have not told anyone yet.

The mechanics that make it work

  • Same time, every week. A fixed slot removes the weekly decision of whether to have it. Weekly is the sane default for a direct report; adjust later (Month 5 covers matching cadence to the person).5
  • Long enough to get past the easy layer. 30 minutes is the floor; 45–60 is better. Grove argued for an hour minimum, because anything shorter "tends to make the subordinate confine himself to simple things that can be handled quickly."2
  • One shared document per person, not private notes. Both of you add items before the meeting. You write notes during it, in the open. Bold text means an action.3
  • Close with the actions out loud. "So: I'll talk to product about the deadline, you'll write the ADR by Thursday." Then open next week with those two lines.
  • Private. A room with a door, or a call with your camera on. Not a shared desk, not a corridor.
Move it, don't cancel it Rands's rule: cancelling a 1:1 tells the person "you don't matter."4 When your day explodes, offer a new slot in the same week instead. The cost of moving is ten seconds; the cost of cancelling is a small withdrawal from trust — and you may need that trust in three weeks.
Watch out The Bottleneck trap loves this meeting. A report brings a problem; you feel the pull to solve it in the room because you can, and fast. Solving it yourself every week teaches them to bring you problems rather than answers. Lesson 3 of this month is about picking the right mode instead.

Do it now, with no reports

You do not need a direct report to practise this. Two moves, this week:

  • Give your mentee a real 1:1. A fixed 30 minutes, a shared doc, their agenda first. It is the same skill at a smaller size — the rep from Lesson 4.
  • Fix the 1:1 you already have. Start a shared doc with your own manager and put your items in it before Friday. You will feel, from the report's seat, how much better a prepared 1:1 is.

Remember this
  • The 1:1 is the highest-leverage meeting you have: ~90 minutes of yours can shape 80+ hours of theirs (Grove).
  • It is their meeting. They set the agenda ahead of time; they talk about 70% of the time.
  • It is not a status report and not a project meeting. Read status beforehand.
  • Mechanics: same weekly slot · 30 min floor, 45–60 better · one shared doc · notes in the open · actions read out loud.
  • Open next week with last week's actions — follow-through is what proves the meeting is real.
  • Move it, never cancel it. Cancelling says "you don't matter."

If you remember one thing: you own the slot, they own the agenda.
