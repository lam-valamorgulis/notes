The Transition Traps

Almost every new manager falls into one of four traps. You can't avoid what you can't see — so let's name them, and the way out of each.

Your win today: you'll recognize the four classic new-manager traps in the wild, know which one you are most likely to fall into, and have a one-line antidote for each.

Two ways to get it wrong

Most management mistakes sit on a single line: how much you insert yourself. Too little, and the team drifts and problems fester. Too much, and you smother the team and become the limit on everything. The skill is finding the supportive middle — and it moves depending on the person and the task.1

The four traps

1. The Bottleneck too hands-on

You keep the hardest, most interesting work for yourself, or every decision has to pass through you. It feels responsible. But the team stalls whenever you're busy, nobody grows, and you drown. This is the trap for ex-star-engineers.

Antidote Delegate real ownership, not just tasks. Ask "who could grow by doing this?" before you grab anything. Your calendar being the critical path is a bug, not a badge.

2. The Micromanager too hands-on

You assign the work but then control how it's done — reviewing every line, redoing others' work, needing constant updates. People stop thinking for themselves because you'll override them anyway.

Antidote Delegate the outcome, not the steps. Agree on what "done" looks like, then let them choose the path. Match how closely you steer to their experience with that specific task (we'll formalize this as "task-relevant maturity" in Month 5).2

3. The Pushover too hands-off

You were their peer last month, so you avoid anything that risks the friendship: hard feedback, saying no, addressing a slacker. Being liked feels like success. But the strong people quietly lose respect for a manager who won't hold a line.

Antidote Remember "clear is kind." Caring about someone includes telling them the hard truth. Avoiding it isn't nice — it robs them of the chance to improve. (This is the whole point of Month 3's feedback module.)3

4. The Hero too hands-on

Every crisis, you swoop in and personally save the day — the late-night fix, the weekend rescue. It feels heroic and gets praise. But the team learns to wait for you to rescue them, the same fires keep starting, and you burn out.

Antidote Fix the system, not just the fire. After each rescue, ask "why did this happen, and what would stop it next time without me?" Coach someone else through the next one.

The meta-trap All four share one root: doing what feels good short-term instead of what builds the team long-term. Grabbing the fix, keeping the peace, saving the day — each gives you a quick hit and quietly weakens the team. Noticing the quick-hit urge is your early-warning system.

Practice: spot the trap

Scenario

A report ships a feature. It works, but you'd have structured it differently. It's not wrong — just not how you'd do it. Nobody else is blocked by it. You feel a strong urge to ask them to redo it your way before it merges.

What trap is that urge, and what's the better move?

Remember this
  • Most management mistakes are too hands-off or too hands-on; the healthy middle is narrow and moves.
  • Bottleneck: you keep the work / all decisions → delegate real ownership.
  • Micromanager: you control the how → delegate the outcome, not the steps.
  • Pushover: you avoid hard talks to stay liked → "clear is kind."
  • Hero: you personally save every fire → fix the system, coach the next rescue.
  • Ex-strong engineers most often fall into Bottleneck or Micromanager.

If you remember one thing: the move that feels good right now is often the one that weakens the team.
