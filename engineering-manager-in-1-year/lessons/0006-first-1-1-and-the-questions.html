The First 1:1 and the Questions That Open People Up

Lesson 1 gave you the slot and the rules. Now: what do you actually say for 45 minutes — and how do you get past "yeah, all good"?

Your win today: you'll have a real question bank — the first-1:1 questions that learn how a person works, the one opener that does the most work, and the difference between a question that opens someone up and one that closes them down.

The first 1:1 has one job

Not to set goals. Not to review the roadmap. The first 1:1 collects the person's operating manual: how they like feedback, what makes them grumpy, what they want next, what they need from you. Lara Hogan's advice on why to ask early is the whole point in one line: "Have the answers to these questions WAY before you need them."1

You will need them on a bad day. The day you must give hard feedback is a terrible day to discover that this person hates being corrected in the moment and would much rather hear it in writing, an hour later.

Hogan even gives you the opening line so it does not feel strange: "I'd like to ask you some cheesy questions to help me better-support you."1

Ask about…The questionsWhat you do with the answer
Grumpiness What makes you grumpy? How will I know when you're grumpy? How can I help when you're grumpy? You stop guessing. Their quiet Tuesday now has a meaning you can check.
Feedback How do you prefer to get feedback — in person, in writing, in chat? During the 1:1, or as it happens? Month 3 (feedback) becomes far easier, because delivery is already agreed.
Recognition How do you like to be recognised — publicly or privately? Stops you praising a private person in a big channel and making them shrink.
Goals & support What are your goals this year, and in the next 3 months? What do you need from your manager? From your team? From peers outside the team? The raw material for a growth plan (Month 4) — in their words, not yours.
The meeting itself What makes 1:1s the most valuable for you? You are handing them the agenda, out loud, in meeting one.
Keep a preferences page Write the answers at the top of that person's shared doc and leave them there. Hogan keeps a document of each person's preferences for exactly this reason.1 Re-reading three lines before a hard conversation is the cheapest preparation in management.

The opener that does the most work

After the first meeting, you need a way in every week. Rands opens with the plainest possible question — "How are you?" — and then listens to which kind of answer comes back. He names three:2

The answerWhat it sounds likeYour move
The UpdateCalm facts. "Fine — the API work is nearly done."Pleasant, and not enough. Go looking: "what's the most annoying part of that?"
The VentHeat, but focused. "Honestly? Platform ignored us again."Let it run. Do not interrupt, do not fix. Being heard is the work.
The DisasterEverything at once, or a flat "I'm done."Stay calm and unshaken. Slow down, ask, take notes, plan nothing today.

The Update is the trap, because it feels like a successful meeting. Nothing was wrong, everyone was polite, you got your time back. But you learned nothing you could not have read on the board.

Grove's reason for a long meeting was exactly this shape: anything shorter, he wrote, "tends to make the subordinate confine himself to simple things that can be handled quickly."3 Layer 3 does not appear in minute four.

Open questions, then silence

A closed question gets you a word. An open question gets you the thing you needed to know. The difference is small on paper and huge in the room.

Closed — gets "yes"Open — gets the truth
"Is the migration on track?""What worries you most about the migration?"
"Are you happy with the team?""What's one thing you'd change about how the team works?"
"Do you need help?""What's slowing you down that I could take off your plate?"
"Was the review OK?""How did that review land for you?"
"Any feedback for me?""What's one thing I should do differently next month?"

Then the hard part: stop talking. Count five seconds in your head. Most people start telling you the real answer at second four, right after the polite one. If you fill that gap, you get the polite one forever.

Watch out Do not fire all the first-1:1 questions in one 30-minute meeting. Twelve questions in a row is an interview, not a conversation — and it puts the person on the defensive. Ask three or four, note the answers, and spread the rest over the first month.

Notes you actually act on

A 1:1 note that nobody reads again is theatre. Keep it tiny and mechanical:

  • One running doc per person, newest at the top, visible to both of you.4
  • Bold = an action, with a name on it. Yours count as much as theirs.
  • Open next week by reading last week's bold lines. Two minutes. This is the habit that makes a report believe the meeting is real.
  • Write down exact words for anything emotional ("I feel invisible on this project"). Their words are evidence; your paraphrase is a guess.

Do it now, with no reports

Run the first-1:1 questions with your mentee this week — all of them apply outside a manager relationship. Then flip seats: send two of the answers to your own manager unprompted ("here's how I like feedback, and what makes me grumpy"). You will learn how it feels to be asked, and your manager gets a better manual for you.

Practice: the vent

Scenario

Twelve minutes into the 1:1, Duc drops the polite tone: "Honestly, the platform team ignored our ticket for three weeks and then told us to read the docs. This happens every time." He is angry, specific, and still talking. You happen to know the platform lead well and could fix this with one message.

Remember this
  • The first 1:1 collects the person's operating manual: grumpiness, feedback style, recognition style, goals, what they need.
  • Ask early — you need those answers before the bad day, not during it.
  • Open with "How are you?" and listen for which answer you got: Update (dig deeper), Vent (let it run), Disaster (stay calm, plan nothing today).
  • Three layers: status → problems → the person. Short meetings never leave layer 1.
  • Open questions, then five seconds of silence. The real answer follows the polite one.
  • Notes: one running doc, bold = action, and next week starts by reading them.

If you remember one thing: ask, then be quiet long enough to hear the second answer.
