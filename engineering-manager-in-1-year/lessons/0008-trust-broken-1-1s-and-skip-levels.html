Trust, Broken 1:1s, and the Skip-Level

Trust is not a feeling — it is a measurable delay. It is how many days pass between "something is wrong" and "my manager knows."

Your win today: you'll be able to say what trust actually buys you, name the six things people need at work, repair a 1:1 that has gone flat, and use a skip-level without going around anybody's back.

What trust actually buys

"Building trust" sounds soft until you make it concrete. A trusted manager learns things earlier. That is the whole benefit, and it is enormous — early information is the only kind you can still act on.

The eventLow trust: you hear…High trust: you hear…
A deadline will slipat the sprint review, from the boardthree weeks early, in a 1:1, as "I'm worried about…"
Someone is unhappywhen the resignation letter arrivesas "I'm not sure this is the job I want anymore"
Two teammates are fightingfrom a third person, after it got loudfrom one of them, while it is still fixable
Your own decision was badnever — people work around you quietlyas "can I push back on that?"

Read the last row twice. A report telling you that you are wrong is the strongest trust signal there is. If nobody has disagreed with you in two months, that is data — and not the flattering kind.

Six things people need at work

People do not get upset at random. Paloma Medina's BICEPS model names six core needs; when one is threatened, the reaction looks much bigger than the trigger. Every person ranks these differently, and learning someone's ranking is a shortcut to understanding them.1

NeedIt meansAn example of it being threatened at work
Belongingbeing part of the groupthe team reorganises and they lose the people they sat with
Improvementgrowing, making progresssix months of maintenance tickets and nothing new learned
Choicecontrol over your own workyou assign not just the what but every detail of the how
Equality / fairnessfair treatment, equal accessone person always gets the interesting project
Predictabilityknowing roughly what is cominga reorg announced with no detail, then two weeks of silence
Significancemattering, being seentheir work is presented upward as "the team's", with no name
How to use this in a 1:1 When a reaction seems out of proportion to the event, ask which need got hit rather than arguing about the event. "It sounds like the part that stung was not the decision, but finding out after everyone else." That is the Predictability need, named plainly — and now you have something you can actually fix.

Four ways a 1:1 breaks — and the repair

1. It has become a status update the common one

Symptom: you both leave informed and nothing changed. Repair: read the board before the meeting, then ban status from the room for two weeks. Replace it with one question: "what's the most annoying part of your week?"

2. They bring nothing silence

Symptom: "all good, nothing much." Repair: structure, not pressure — the shared doc, two prompts, and your own vulnerability first. Say a real thing about your week that is not perfect. Silence usually means "I don't know what is safe to say here."

3. It keeps getting moved the quiet killer

Symptom: three reschedules, then a cancellation. Repair: put it back in the same weekly slot, be honest ("I let that slide and that was wrong"), and protect it for a month. This is the fastest trust repair available, because it costs you something visible: your calendar.

4. You do all the talking the invisible one

Symptom: you feel great after every 1:1. Repair: measure it. Put a tally in your notes for who is talking; aim for their 70%. Ask a question, then count to five before you speak again.

Watch out "We're both busy, let's make 1:1s fortnightly" almost never means "let's be efficient." It usually means the meeting has stopped being useful. Fix the shape first. If it is genuinely still valuable after four good ones, then discuss the cadence — and cadence should follow how much support the person needs right now, not how full your calendar is (Month 5 makes this precise).

The skip-level: the right tool, used the wrong way often

A skip-level is a conversation with someone two levels away — your report's report. You will not have one for a while, but you will be in them from the other side much sooner, so learn both seats now.

Why they exist. Camille Fournier's reason is honest and slightly uncomfortable: some problems will never escalate to you on their own, and those are the ones that make people leave and projects explode. A skip-level gives you a broader view of the teams — and lets you check whether what your own reports tell you matches what the people doing the work say.2

What it is not. It is not a performance check on the manager in the middle. It is not where career decisions, priorities, or feedback for that engineer get handled — that is their manager's job, and doing it yourself takes authority away from the person you hired to have it.3

DoDon't
Tell your report you are running skip-levels, and why, before you book any.Book it quietly and let them find the invite in a calendar.
Send prompts in advance, and say the meeting is mostly for their benefit.Open with "so, how's your manager doing?"
End with "have you talked to your manager about this?" and agree who does what next.Take an action that belongs to their manager.
Do it on a regular, boring cadence — roughly once a quarter per person, or a team lunch.Only appear when something has gone wrong. Then a skip-level means trouble.

Fournier's own prompt list is a good starting set: what do you like best and worst about your current project · who on the team has been doing really well · do you have feedback about your manager, good or bad · what is keeping you from doing your best work · what are we missing.2

Do it now, with no reports

  • Take the other seat. Ask your manager's manager for 30 minutes, once a quarter. Bring two or three real topics, not complaints. This is a normal, professional request, and most senior managers welcome it.4
  • Check your own trust delay. Think of the last thing that went wrong on your team. How many days passed between the first person knowing and your manager knowing? That number is the metric this whole month is trying to shrink.
  • Ask for the disagreement. In your next 1:1 with your manager: "what's one thing I should do differently?" Feel how hard it is to answer. That is exactly what your future reports will feel.

Remember this
  • Trust is a delay: the days between something going wrong and you hearing about it. Shrink the delay.
  • A report disagreeing with you is the strongest trust signal. Two months of agreement is a warning.
  • BICEPS — Belonging, Improvement, Choice, Equality/fairness, Predictability, Significance. Each person ranks them differently.
  • Four ways a 1:1 breaks: status update · silence · reschedules · you talking. Each has a mechanical fix.
  • "Let's make it fortnightly" usually means "this is not useful." Fix the shape before the cadence.
  • A skip-level runs alongside the chain, not around it: tell the manager first, send prompts, and never take over their job.
  • Skip-levels exist because some problems never escalate on their own.

If you remember one thing: you are not building a friendship — you are shortening the time until you hear the truth.
