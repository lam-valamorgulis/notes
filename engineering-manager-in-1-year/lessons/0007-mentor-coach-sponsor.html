Mentor, Coach, Sponsor

Three modes, one meeting. Engineers default to one of them, avoid the second, and forget the third completely — which is the one that changes careers.

Your win today: you'll be able to name which mode you are in mid-conversation, say what each one costs you, and spot the person you have mentored for a year and never once sponsored.

Same meeting, three different jobs

Lara Hogan's model for 1:1s is that a good manager balances three modes across the weeks — advice mode, introspection mode, and growth mode — rather than living in one.1 The glossary defines each; here is what they feel like in the room.

ModeYou say…Use it when…If you overuse it
Mentoring
advice mode		 "Here's how I've done it. Here's what bit me." They genuinely lack the experience, or the clock has run out. They stop thinking and start waiting for your answer.
Coaching
introspection mode		 "What options are you weighing? Which would you pick?" They have enough experience to find it — with help. Feels evasive when they urgently needed a straight answer.
Sponsoring
growth mode		 "I put your name forward for it." (said in a room they are not in) They are ready for something bigger than their current title. Not a real risk — the real risk is never doing it at all.

Coaching is the mode engineers skip

You were promoted for having answers. Giving one is fast, it feels helpful, and it is genuinely correct most of the time. That is what makes it dangerous: every answer you hand over is a small deposit into the Bottleneck trap. Do it for six months and your team routes every decision through your calendar.

Coaching is slower and quieter. A small bank of questions covers most cases:

  • "What have you already tried?"
  • "What are the two or three options?"
  • "If I were on holiday for a week, what would you do?"
  • "What would have to be true for option B to be the right one?"
  • "What's the part you're least sure about?"

Note the fourth one. It hands the thinking back without abandoning them — and their answer usually tells you whether they needed coaching or actually needed your experience.

Example

Bao runs a four-person billing team. Every design question landed in his DMs, and he answered each one inside the hour. For two weeks he changed one thing: he replied "what are your two options?" and then waited. By the third week two of the engineers were sending him a shortlist instead of a question, and his DMs went quiet.

A cheap rule for switching modes Ask yourself: does this person have the raw material to find the answer? If yes, coach — even though it takes longer. If no, or the deadline is tomorrow, mentor plainly and say what you are doing: "I'll just tell you this one, we're short on time." Naming the mode out loud stops coaching from feeling like you are dodging the question.

Sponsorship is the one that changes careers

Mentoring is advice, based on your experience. Sponsorship is taking an active part in someone's advancement: recommending them for the role, pushing for the promotion, handing over the visible piece of work.2 Concretely, it looks like:

  • Saying their name in the room they are not in. Staffing meetings, planning meetings, "who could lead this?" conversations.
  • Giving away the visible work. The demo to leadership, the design review, the incident write-up — not the invisible glue.
  • Sharing their work upward with their name attached, not summarised as "my team did X".
  • Vouching for them when someone doubts they are ready.
Example

Nour manages six engineers on a search team. Her strongest engineer, Ines, had written every migration runbook, but had never spoken to anyone above Nour. So Nour gave away the quarterly reliability review: Ines presented it, and Nour sat at the back and said nothing. Six weeks later a director asked for Ines by name to lead the multi-team index upgrade.

Hogan points at a pattern worth remembering: women and nonbinary engineers tend to be over-mentored and under-sponsored — plenty of advice, far fewer opportunities.2 Advice is cheap to give. Opportunity is not.

Watch out You can mentor someone warmly for a year, feel like a great manager, and never once spend your own credibility on them. If you cannot name a specific opportunity you handed a person in the last quarter — a project, a talk, a promotion case — you have not sponsored anyone yet.

The fourth mode nobody lists

The three-mode model is about growing a person. Sometimes that is not the situation. When the decision is not theirs to make — a security requirement, a legal constraint, a call already made above you — you are in direct mode: say the decision, say why, and say what is still open. Pretending to coach ("what do you think we should do?") when the answer is fixed is worse than being direct. It wastes their time and, when the real answer lands, it costs you trust.

Do it now, with no reports

  • One coaching question this week. Next time a teammate asks "how should I…", reply with "what are your options?" first. Notice how uncomfortable the pause is. Sit in it.
  • One act of sponsorship this month. You do not need authority to say a name out loud: "Mai has done this three times, she should present it." That is sponsorship, from any seat.

Remember this
  • Three modes in a 1:1: mentor (advice), coach (questions), sponsor (opportunity). Balance them over weeks, don't live in one.
  • What each costs you: your experience · your patience · your reputation.
  • Switch rule: if they have the raw material, coach. If not — or time has run out — mentor, and say you're doing it.
  • Coaching is the mode ex-strong-ICs skip, and skipping it feeds the Bottleneck trap.
  • Sponsorship happens in rooms they are not in: say their name, give away the visible work, vouch for them.
  • People are commonly over-mentored and under-sponsored. Advice is cheap; opportunity is not.
  • A fourth mode exists: direct — when the call is already made, say so plainly instead of fake-coaching.

If you remember one thing: advice is what you know; sponsorship is what you risk.
