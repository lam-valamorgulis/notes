Reference · Glossary

Glossary

The words managers use. Learn them once here; every lesson uses the same meaning. Keep real terms — don't translate them away.

Why a glossary matters: half of "sounding like a manager" is using the shared words correctly. When a term appears in a lesson, it means exactly what it means here.

People & conversations

1:1 (said "one-on-one")
A regular private meeting (usually weekly) between a manager and one report. The report's meeting, not a status update. The core ritual of management.
Skip-level
A meeting between a manager and someone two levels down (their report's report). Used to hear the ground truth and build wider trust.
Feedback
Telling someone the effect of what they did, so they can repeat it or change it. Best when specific and soon, not saved up.
SBI
A feedback format: Situation, Behavior, Impact. "In standup (S), you talked over Ana (B), and she stopped sharing (I)." Keeps feedback about actions, not character.
Radical Candor
Kim Scott's idea: care personally about the person and challenge them directly at the same time. Missing either one fails.
Mentoring
Sharing your experience and advice with someone. "Here's how I'd approach it." You give answers.
Coaching
Helping someone find their own answer by asking questions. You give questions, not answers — builds their skill.
Sponsorship
Spending your own reputation to create opportunities for someone (naming them for a project, a talk, a promotion) when they are not in the room. The most powerful — and most overlooked — growth tool.
Growth plan
A simple, shared plan for how a person will grow — the skill to build, how, and by when.
Delegation
Giving real work (and the authority to do it) to someone else. How managers scale and how people grow — not just off-loading tasks.
Task-relevant maturity (TRM)
Andrew Grove's idea: how experienced someone is at this specific task. Low TRM needs more direction; high TRM needs more freedom. Match your style to the task, not the person overall.
PIP (Performance Improvement Plan)
A formal, written plan with clear goals and a deadline for someone who is underperforming. Serious and often high-risk; must be fair and specific.
Calibration
A meeting where managers compare ratings across people so reviews are fair and consistent, not just one manager's opinion.

Team & culture

Psychological safety
A shared belief that it is safe to speak up, ask questions, disagree, or admit a mistake without being punished. Google's Project Aristotle found this is the top predictor of team performance.
Project Aristotle
Google's research study into what makes teams effective. Its headline finding was psychological safety.
Team norms
The agreed "how we work here" rules — meeting etiquette, code review, on-call, how decisions get made.
Retro (retrospective)
A regular team meeting to reflect on what went well, what didn't, and what to change.
Blameless postmortem
A write-up after an incident that focuses on the system and process failures, not on blaming a person. Makes it safe to tell the truth, so the real cause gets fixed.
On-call
A rotation where an engineer is responsible for responding to production problems during a set period.

Delivery & metrics

Roadmap
A plan of what the team intends to build and roughly when, and why. A communication tool, not a promise carved in stone.
Estimation
Predicting how long work will take. Honest estimates are ranges with uncertainty, not single false-precise dates.
OKRs (Objectives & Key Results)
A goal-setting method: one ambitious Objective plus a few measurable Key Results that show progress.
DORA metrics
Four research-backed measures of software delivery performance (from the book Accelerate): the four below. Let you talk about delivery with evidence, not vibes.
— Deployment Frequency
How often the team ships to production. Elite teams ship often and in small batches.
— Lead Time for Changes
How long from "code committed" to "running in production." Shorter is healthier.
— Change Failure Rate
What share of deploys cause a failure needing a fix. Lower is healthier.
— Time to Restore (MTTR)
How long to recover when something breaks in production. Faster is healthier.

Technical & organizational

IC (Individual Contributor)
An engineer who does the work directly and has no direct reports. Where you are now.
TLM (Tech Lead Manager)
A hybrid role: still leads technically and writes some code, while also managing a small team. A common first step into management.
Tech debt
The future cost of a shortcut taken today. Some is wise; too much slows everything down.
Conway's law
Systems tend to copy the shape of the organization that builds them. Two teams → two services. You can shape software by shaping teams — on purpose.
Team Topologies
A model of four team types: stream-aligned (owns a product flow), platform (serves other teams), enabling (helps others learn), and complicated-subsystem (deep-expertise area).
Cognitive load
How much a team must hold in its head. Too much slows delivery. Good team design keeps each team's load manageable.
RACI
A way to clarify roles on a task: who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed.
Glue work
The essential, often invisible work that holds a team together (planning, unblocking, docs, reviews). Great pre-manager practice — but make sure it's recognized.
Bus factor
How many people would have to be "hit by a bus" (leave) before the team is stuck. Low bus factor = risky knowledge silo.
Span of control
How many people report directly to one manager. Too many and 1:1s and growth suffer.
Managing up
Deliberately building a good working relationship with your own manager: sharing context, setting expectations, and making their job easier.
