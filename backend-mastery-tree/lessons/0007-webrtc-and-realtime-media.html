Domain 6 · Architecture · Real-time · Lesson 0007

WebRTC & real-time media

HTTP moves documents. This moves your voice — and for a voice, a late packet is worse than a lost one.

The win: after ~45 minutes you can explain a video call end to end — capture, connect, converse — answer "mesh or SFU, and why?" with the uplink math, and describe how a real production system does it, because this lesson is grounded in one: a production virtual-office collaboration app — our case-study codebase — whose media engine we read at the source level.

1. Why media can't ride HTTP

TCP promises reliable, ordered delivery: lose a packet, and everything behind it waits for the retransmission (head-of-line blocking). Perfect for documents — every byte matters, timing doesn't. Live audio inverts that: a conversation feels natural only if sound arrives within roughly 200 ms. A retransmitted audio packet arrives after its moment has passed — useless, and it delayed everything behind it too.

So WebRTC media rides UDP (as encrypted RTP — "SRTP"): lost packets are simply skipped, a small jitter buffer smooths timing, and the codec conceals the gap. Keep this lens for the whole lesson: late is worse than lost.

Feynman version — the live interpreter

TCP is a courier who will re-drive any lost page of your manuscript, however long it takes — right for a book. A live call is a simultaneous interpreter: if a word is missed, the interpreter doesn't stop the meeting to recover it — they skip it and keep pace, because being three sentences behind is worse than missing a word.

2. The machine has three parts

  1. Capture. getUserMedia({audio, video}) for mic and camera, getDisplayMedia() for screen share. Both return a MediaStream — which is just a bag of MediaStreamTracks. The track is the real unit: it can be enabled, stopped, and swapped (section 5 lives on this fact).
  2. Connect. RTCPeerConnection — finds a network path with ICE (section 3), secures it with a DTLS handshake, then streams SRTP media over it.
  3. Converse. Peers must agree what they're sending (codecs, tracks, addresses) — described in SDP "offer/answer" blobs. And here is the exam-grade fact: WebRTC ships no way to deliver them. Signaling is your job — any ordered message channel works, and in practice it is the WebSocket you built in lesson 0006.

How the case-study app does it: one STOMP-over-WebSocket connection carries chat, presence, call invites — and all media signaling. The media engine even builds request/response RPC on top of that fire-and-forget pub/sub channel with an EventEmitter: send createWebRtcTransport, resolve the promise when the …Response event arrives. One connection, four jobs — lesson 0006's backplane thinking, reused as a signaling plane.

3. NAT traversal: STUN, TURN, ICE

Both peers usually sit behind NAT — neither has a public address the other can reach directly. Three acronyms, one sentence each:

  • STUN — a mirror: "what do I look like from the internet?" Cheap; gives you your public address; works for most home NATs.
  • TURN — a relay of last resort: all media flows through the relay server. Expensive (real bandwidth costs), but works through anything — needed for roughly 10–20% of users behind strict/symmetric NATs.
  • ICE — the process that gathers every candidate path (local, STUN-reflexive, TURN-relay), tries them pairwise with the peer, and picks the best that connects.
Trap — ICE misconfiguration fails silently, for some users In the case-study app's source, the ICE config is TURN-only with placeholder credentials left in — and the TURN hostname variable is missing from one environment's config, which would build the literal URL turn:undefined. Nothing crashes: users on friendly networks connect anyway (the SFU's own address is reachable), and only the strict-NAT minority can't get media. That is the signature of ICE bugs everywhere: no error, just "it doesn't work at my office." Test from a hostile network on purpose.

4. Topology: mesh, SFU, MCU — do the uplink math

With N people in a call, who sends what to whom?

TopologyMechanismCost that kills it
Mesh (P2P)everyone sends to everyoneuplink × (N−1) — dead by ~4–6 people
SFU (selective forwarding unit)everyone uploads once; server forwards packets, no decodingserver bandwidth — cheap CPU, scales wide
MCUserver decodes, mixes into one stream, re-encodesserver CPU (transcoding) — expensive per room

How the case-study app does it: a mediasoup SFU (its media server is literally hostnamed …-SFU-…). The join handshake, condensed — this sequence is the answer to "walk me through joining a call": connect signaling → fetch the server's RTP capabilities → load them into a client Device → ask the server to create two transports (one send, one receive; the response carries ICE + DTLS parameters) → produce your mic/camera tracks (server assigns each a producer id) → for every other participant the server pushes a newConsumer event and you attach the incoming track to that peer's MediaStream. No raw SDP anywhere in app code — the library generates it; the app's job is moving those JSON messages over its WebSocket, in order.

5. Tracks are swappable, producers are long-lived

The most teachable production tricks in the case-study engine are all one idea: keep the pipeline (producer) alive, swap what flows through it (track).

  • The dummy-track trick. At join, the camera is never opened. The engine calls getUserMedia audio-only, then appends a fake video track: a hidden <canvas> painted dark gray, captured at 5 fps via canvas.captureStream(5). Result: a video producer exists from second one — with no camera permission prompt and no camera LED.
  • Camera on = replaceTrack, not a new connection. Turn the camera on: get the real track, videoProducer.replaceTrack({track}), resume. Turn it off: swap the dummy back in, pause the producer. No renegotiation, no flicker.
  • Mute = pause the producer, not track.enabled = false. Three ways to mute exist: stop the track (kills it — restart is slow), track.enabled = false (keeps sending a silent/black stream — uplink wasted), or producer.pause() plus a signal so the server stops forwarding — uplink actually stops, and the UI reads truth from producer.paused. The case-study source keeps the rejected options as commented-out code — a code review preserved in amber.
  • Screen share ends outside your UI. getDisplayMedia shows a browser-owned "Stop sharing" bar. The user will click that, not your button — so the engine listens for the track's ended event and runs the full stop-sharing cleanup from there. Miss this and your UI says "sharing" while nothing flows.

6. Scaling media: send rungs, consume windows

Lesson 0006's fan-out math applies to media too, but the mitigation moves into the media layer:

  • Simulcast. Each camera producer sends three quality rungs at once — in the case-study app: ~100 kbps, ~300 kbps, ~900 kbps (each with 3 temporal layers, scalabilityMode: "S1T3"). The SFU picks a rung per receiver: the thumbnail viewer gets 100 kbps, the pinned full-screen viewer gets 900. The sender pays ~1.3× bandwidth so the server never transcodes. (Screen share deliberately gets no simulcast — text needs the full rung.)
  • Dominant-speaker windowing. The client consumes video for at most 3 participants at a time; the server pushes "dominant speakers" updates and the client swaps video consumers as the loud set changes. A 40-person room costs 3 video downlinks, not 39. Pinned peers keep their consumer regardless.
  • Talking groups. The SFU forwards audio only within your group — spatial audio for the virtual office, and a bandwidth cap in disguise.
  • Pause is a bandwidth tool. Muted producer = zero uplink; consumers start paused server-side and resume only after the client wires the track — no wasted downlink either.

7. Resilience: two planes, two stories

The signaling plane reconnects like any WebSocket (lesson 0006): the case-study app disables the library's auto-reconnect and hand-rolls exponential backoff (1s doubling, capped at 30s), gated on navigator.onLine. The media plane, notably, does not: the ICE-restart code exists only as comments — if a transport dies, the user re-joins. The pragmatic liveness signal is a DataChannel heartbeat: a counter sent every 2 seconds from a Worker-backed timer (workers keep ticking when browsers throttle background tabs), so the SFU can reap dead clients. The lesson: real products often ship simpler-than-textbook resilience — knowing what they skipped, and the cost, is senior judgment.

Drill — design the mute button

Your PM says: "just make the mic button work." You have a mediasoup-style audio producer and its track.

Compare the three implementations — track.stop(), track.enabled = false, producer.pause() + server signal — on: uplink bandwidth, unmute latency, whether the server/peers know, and where the UI reads its state from. Pick one and defend it out loud before the quiz.

Remember this
  • Late is worse than lost: media rides UDP/SRTP and skips lost packets; TCP's retransmission is head-of-line poison for a 200 ms budget.
  • Three parts: capture (getUserMedia/getDisplayMedia → streams of tracks), connect (RTCPeerConnection: ICE + DTLS + SRTP), converse (SDP offer/answer over your own signaling channel — the spec ships none).
  • STUN = mirror, TURN = relay of last resort (~10–20% of users need it), ICE = try everything, pick the best. ICE bugs fail silently, for a minority, from certain networks.
  • Mesh dies at ~5 people (uplink × N−1). Every real product is an SFU: upload once, server forwards without decoding. MCU mixes — pay server CPU.
  • Producers are long-lived, tracks are swappable: dummy canvas track at join, camera via replaceTrack, mute via producer.pause() — and the browser's own "Stop sharing" fires track.ended, not your button.
  • Scale = simulcast rungs (sender pays ~1.3×, server never transcodes) + consume windows (3 videos, swapped by dominant speaker) + pause-as-bandwidth-tool.
  • Signaling reconnects with backoff; media often just re-joins — and a DataChannel heartbeat (from a Worker timer) tells the SFU who's really alive.

If you remember one thing: WebRTC gives you pipes that skip lost packets; everything product-shaped — signaling, topology, mute, scale — is engineering you do around those pipes, and the SFU is how everyone does it.
