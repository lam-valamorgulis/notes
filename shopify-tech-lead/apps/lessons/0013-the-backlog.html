The backlog

You were down four hours. "Catch up as fast as possible" is the wrong instinct.

Your win today. A recovery plan that gets the important data right first, and does not turn your own outage into a rate-limit outage.

How it works

Derive it: what is actually in a backlog?

Your service was down four hours. Twelve thousand changes queued. The instinct is to drain it at full speed. Test that instinct from first principles by asking what those twelve thousand items are.

12,000 queued changes for 4,000 distinct products product A: price → 10, price → 11, price → 12, stock → 5, stock → 3 └────────── 5 messages ──────────┘ │ only TWO facts matter now: price is 12 · stock is 3 ── the backlog is mostly OBSOLETE. draining it in order replays history you no longer care about.

This is the key realisation, and it follows directly from A3.3: because your writes are absolute, only the last value per field per record matters. Everything before it is noise. A backlog is not 12,000 units of work — it is 4,000, and you get there by collapsing on (record, field) and keeping the newest.

Feynman version: you are not replaying a story, you are answering "what is true now?" Twelve thousand messages, one question.

Then re-derive the source of truth

Push it further. If you only need the current value, do you need the queued messages at all? Mostly no. From A3.4, a message is only a hint. So the cleanest recovery is:

1. collapse the queue to a set of AFFECTED IDS (throw the payloads away) 2. re-read those ids from the source (current values, batched) 3. write them (absolute, so order-free) 4. advance the watermark to the highest seen (never to "now")

Step 1 discards data, which feels wrong and is right. The payloads were stale the moment they were written. Keeping the ids and dropping the bodies is strictly better: less to store, fewer writes, and every value you write is current.

Order the work by damage, not by arrival

Not all fields cost the same when wrong. So drain by consequence:

PriorityWhatWhy first
1Inventory, and anything price-relatedWrong now means overselling or selling at the wrong price. It costs money every minute
2New and deleted itemsA deleted item is still sellable; a new one is invisible
3Descriptions, tags, images, SEOWrong is untidy, not expensive. It can wait an hour

A first-in-first-out queue treats a product description as equal to a stock level. It is not. Give the queue a priority field — one queue, as A2.3 argued, with priority inside it.

Do not cause the second outage

The rate limit does not care that you were down. Draining at full speed spends your whole budget, gets you throttled, and slows the recovery you are trying to accelerate — while also starving the live traffic that is still arriving.

And know the wall you may hit: webhook subscriptions configured via the Admin API are deleted after 8 consecutive failures. If your outage lasted long enough, there is no backlog to drain — the queue stopped filling. Recovery then means recreating the subscription and a full re-read of the outage window, because those events are simply gone.

Verified 2026-08-23: 8 retries over 4 hours, then automatic deletion of subscriptions configured using the Admin API; recovery guidance is to re-subscribe where applicable and import the missing data — Troubleshoot webhooks.

Answer before reading on You were down six hours. Your webhook subscription was created with the Admin API. How big is your backlog?

Zero — and that is the bad answer, not the good one. After 8 consecutive failures over about four hours, the subscription was deleted. Nothing has queued since. An empty queue looks like a clean recovery and means you have lost every event from the outage. Recovery is: recreate the subscription, then scan the source for everything changed in the whole window. This is exactly why A2.4 insists that "no errors" is not "working" — an empty queue is ambiguous, and you must know which kind of empty it is.

Where it hurts

Costs money — draining at full speed You spend the whole rate-limit budget on the backlog, get throttled, and the live changes arriving now queue behind history nobody needs. The recovery takes longer than if you had paced it, and current data is wrong for longer.
Costs money — FIFO order Product descriptions from four hours ago sync before stock levels from four minutes ago. You oversell during your own recovery. Priority is not an optimisation here; it is the difference between a quiet recovery and refunds.
Fails with no error — the empty queue after a long outage The subscription was deleted, so nothing queued. Dashboards look healthy. You are receiving nothing and believe you have caught up. This is the worst failure mode in the Chapter because every signal says success.
Fails with no error — advancing the watermark to "now" after recovery It is tempting: you just did a big catch-up, so mark it done. But anything changed during the catch-up is now behind the watermark and lost. Same rule as always — the highest value you actually saw.
Replaying stale payloads instead of re-reading You write 12,000 values, most of them obsolete, and every intermediate one is briefly live on the storefront. Customers can see prices flicker through a history. Collapse to ids and read current.
Judgement, not documentation — when to stop draining and do a full sync Past some size, collapsing and re-reading a change list costs more than just re-reading everything. There is no published threshold. Measure both on your own data, write the number down, and make it a switch an operator can flip — not a decision made under pressure.

Say it out loud

Explain this to a beginner

Your service was down for six hours. 50,000 messages are waiting. Explain why you should throw almost all of them away — and why an empty queue would be worse news.

Say it out loud. Then read on.

Now compare with a plain-words answer

Because most of them are already out of date. If one product changed forty times during the outage, you do not need forty updates — you need to know its price now. So you keep the list of which things changed, throw the contents away, and go and read the current state of each. Fifty thousand messages might be four thousand actual items.

Then the order you do them in matters. Not the order they arrived — the order of what is expensive to get wrong. Stock and price first, because being wrong there sells things you do not have or at the wrong money. Descriptions last, because a stale description costs nothing.

And you must not use your whole capacity to catch up, because live customers are still shopping. Go flat out and you cause a second outage yourself, immediately after recovering from the first.

Now the empty queue. If nothing is waiting after a long outage, the comforting reading is "we did not miss anything". The likely reading is that Shopify gave up calling you and cancelled the arrangement — messages stopped being generated, not stopped being needed. So an empty queue after an outage is a warning to check your subscriptions, then re-read the whole window by hand.

If you used any of these words, try again backpressure · compaction · coalescing · priority queue · replay · thundering herd. "Forty updates for one product collapse into one question: what is it now?" is the explanation.

Build it

Task · 90 minutes Dev store + Postgres
  1. Generate 50,000 queued events across 4,000 ids, with heavy repetition.
  2. Collapse to distinct ids and discard every payload. Record both numbers.
  3. Sort the work by damage: inventory and price first, descriptions last.
  4. Cap the drain at a fraction of your rate-limit budget so live traffic keeps working. Watch the headroom number while it runs.
  5. Now remove the cap and run it again, watching live requests get throttled.

Proof it worked: 50,000 became about 4,000 units of work; the capped drain kept live traffic healthy and the uncapped one did not. You caused the self-inflicted second outage on purpose, in a safe place.

The empty-queue drill · 30 minutes Dev store
  1. Take your endpoint down long enough to burn the retries — 8 consecutive failures for an Admin-API subscription.
  2. Bring it up. Observe the queue is empty.
  3. Write the recovery: list subscriptions, re-subscribe what is missing, then re-read the full outage window.
  4. Run the recovery and confirm the missed changes land.

Proof it worked: your service treats an empty queue after an outage as an alarm, not an all-clear — and the recovery path is code you have actually run.

Remember this

  • A backlog is mostly obsolete. With absolute writes, only the last value per record and field matters.
  • Collapse to affected ids and throw the payloads away. Then re-read current values.
  • Drain by damage, not arrival. Inventory and price first, descriptions last.
  • Reserve budget for live traffic. Full-speed draining causes a second, self-inflicted outage.
  • An empty queue after a long outage is a warning, not a success — the subscription may have been deleted.
  • Admin-API subscriptions die after 8 consecutive failures. Recovery is re-subscribe plus a full window re-read.
  • Never advance the watermark to "now" after a catch-up. Highest seen, always.

If you remember one thing: do not replay history, ask what is true now — and do the expensive-if-wrong fields first.
