Worked example: integrating a stock-availability SaaS

One integration end to end, using stockinstore as the concrete case.

Your win today. One complete integration you can describe from discovery to rollout — with the two places it is genuinely hard, and the answer to "should we build this ourselves?"
Why a named vendor here stockinstore is a public commercial product, so naming it costs nobody anything — unlike a client's own systems. It is used here as a concrete example of a whole product category: Find in Store, store locator, click-and-collect, and ship from store.

How it works

What the category does

A retailer has physical shops and a Shopify store. Customers want to know whether a specific size is in a specific shop, reserve it, or have it shipped from the nearest one. Shopify alone models inventory per location — so why buy a product for this at all?

First-principles answer: Shopify tells you how many. The category answers "where, and can I have it?" That means a store locator with opening hours, availability presented per shop, a pickup flow with reservation, and allocation logic deciding which shop fulfils a shipment. Shopify gives you the numbers; almost none of the rest.

All four at once, which is what makes it a good worked example:

The two genuinely hard parts

1 · The storefront read path

A customer on a product page asks "is this near me?" That request is synchronous, on a page a customer is waiting for, and it depends on a third party.

product page → "in stock near me?" → vendor API → render budget: a few hundred ms, or the customer scrolls past vendor down → show WHAT?

Apply the heuristic from A4.1: fail open on information, fail closed on a promise. Availability display is information — if the vendor is slow or down, hide the panel. Never block the page and never guess a number. A missing badge is invisible; a wrong badge sends someone to a shop for nothing.

Then: cache, and cache with an honest label. A short cache is fine for "in stock at this shop" if the page says "checked a few minutes ago". A cache with no label reads as a live promise.

2 · Click and collect is a promise, so it must fail closed

Reservation is different in kind. You are telling a customer an item is waiting for them.

If the vendor is down at…Do
Browsing — the availability panelFail open. Hide it. No harm done
Checkout — choosing pickupFail closed. Remove pickup as an option rather than accept a reservation you cannot confirm
After the order — notifying the shopQueue and retry. This is shape 2, asynchronous

Three different answers for one vendor being down, decided by what you are promising at each moment. That table is the design.

Rollout: by shop, not by feature

1. one shop, staff only, display OFF prove the data is right 2. one shop, display ON prove the read path 3. one shop, pickup ON prove the promise 4. a cohort of shops pick DIFFERENT ones — busy, quiet, odd POS 5. all shops

Cohort by variety, not by convenience. The shop with the unusual POS or the one that counts stock weekly is the one that will find your bugs. Rolling out to five similar shops proves almost nothing.

Answer before reading on Their availability API takes 900 ms at p99. The client wants the in-stock badge on every product card in a collection grid — 24 cards per page. What do you say?

Not that way. Twenty-four synchronous calls at 900 ms will either block the page or arrive after the customer has scrolled away, and it multiplies your load on the vendor by 24. Three better options: fetch availability for the whole page in one batched call if they support it; load badges after the page renders, so a slow vendor delays only the badges; or show availability only on the product page, where the customer has actually chosen something. Recommend the third plus the second — and note that "the badge everywhere" is a design request whose cost lives entirely in someone else's latency.

Where it hurts

Costs money — reserving stock you cannot confirm Accepting a pickup order while the vendor is unreachable means promising an item that may not be there. The customer drives to the shop. That is worse than losing the sale, because it costs the client the customer.
Costs money — shelf stock treated as sellable Damaged units, held items and display stock are on the shelf and not for sale. Publish the shelf number as availability and you send people to collect things that are not available.
Fails with no error — a cache with no label Five-minute-old availability shown as if live. Nothing errors and the number is usually right. When it is wrong, a customer has made a journey. Label the freshness on the page.
Fails with no error — a shop with no Shopify location Shopify cannot express per-shop inventory for a shop that is not a location. Availability for it is silently absent, which looks like "out of stock everywhere". Audit locations against real shops during discovery.
Synchronous third-party calls in a collection grid As above. Latency multiplied by the number of cards, on a page the customer is watching. Batch it, defer it, or move it to the product page.
Judgement, not documentation — the vendor's plan gates matter too A vendor's own product tiers can require a particular Shopify plan for some features while others work on any plan. So "does this need Plus?" can have a different answer per feature. Confirm it with the vendor per feature, in writing, before you scope.

Say it out loud

Explain this to a beginner

A third-party service that tells customers which shop has an item goes down. Explain why the right response is different on the product page and at checkout.

Say it out loud. Then read on.

Now compare with a plain-words answer

Because on the product page you are giving information, and at checkout you are making a promise.

On the product page, "we cannot currently show shop stock" is a small disappointment. The customer can still buy. So you hide that section and let everything else work — you fail open.

At checkout, "collect from the Manchester shop" is a commitment. If you cannot verify it, offering it anyway means possibly telling someone to drive somewhere for something that is not there. So you withdraw the option — you fail closed. Losing that one choice is much cheaper than breaking a promise.

One outage, two correct and opposite responses. Which is why "what do we do when the vendor is down?" is the wrong question. The right question is what are we promising at this exact moment? — and that single question generates the whole design.

And if you are showing a saved copy of the numbers rather than asking live, say so on the page with its age. An unlabelled old number reads to a customer as a live promise, and they will hold you to it.

If you used any of these words, try again graceful degradation · fail open · fail closed · circuit breaker · stale-while-revalidate. "Information versus a promise" is the explanation, and it decides everything else.

Build it

Task · 90 minutes Dev store
  1. Add a fake availability service you can switch off.
  2. Show shop stock on the product page. Turn the service off. The page must still work and still sell — fail open.
  3. Offer collect-from-shop at checkout. Turn the service off. The option must disappear — fail closed.
  4. Add a saved copy with its age printed on the page.
  5. Write the one-line rule for each of the three surfaces.

Proof it worked: one switch produces three different, correct behaviours, and the cached number shows its age. If all three behave the same way, you have one rule where you need three.

Name all four shapes in it · 20 minutes no store needed

This integration is all four shapes at once, which is why it is the worked example. Label each part with its shape and its failure rule.

Proof it worked: you found the shape-3 part — the checkout decision — and gave it the shrinking-timeout and no-retry treatment rather than the background treatment.

Remember this

  • Shopify tells you how many; this category answers "where, and can I have it?"
  • It is all four shapes at once, which is why it is a good worked example.
  • Fail open on information, fail closed on a promise. One vendor outage, three different answers.
  • Availability display: hide it when the vendor is down. Pickup at checkout: withdraw the option.
  • Label cache freshness on the page. An unlabelled cache reads as a live promise.
  • Audit shops against Shopify locations in discovery. No location means no per-shop stock.
  • Cohort by variety, not convenience. The awkward shop finds the bugs.

If you remember one thing: decide what you are promising at each moment — that single question produces the whole failure design.
