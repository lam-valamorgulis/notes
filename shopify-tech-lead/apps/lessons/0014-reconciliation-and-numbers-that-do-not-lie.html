Track 2 · A3 · Lesson 6

Reconciliation, and numbers that do not lie

Counting rows proves almost nothing. Here is what to compare instead.

Your win today. One number you can put in front of a client that actually means "the sync is correct" — and the knowledge of why the obvious number does not.

How it works

Derive it: what would a proof of correctness have to do?

Every technique so far reduces the chance of drift. None of them detects it. From first principles, detecting drift requires comparing the two copies — there is no way to infer it from your own logs, because a silent failure leaves no log.

So the question is what to compare. The obvious answer is counts.

ERP: 10,000 products Shopify: 10,000 products ✓ match — done? now consider: · 50 products missing, 50 extra duplicates → count matches · every price off by one cent → count matches · every stock level from last Tuesday → count matches · one product's variants deleted, another's added→ count matches ── a count is one number summarising thousands. almost every real bug survives it.

Feynman version: a count tells you how many things there are, and every bug you care about is about what the things say.

Compare what you sent against what is there

This is what the shadow table from A2.2 exists for. It holds exactly what you last successfully sent. So reconciliation becomes a three-way comparison:

source shadow Shopify (truth) (what we sent) (what's there) │ │ │ ├── A ───────────┤ │ source ≠ shadow → we are BEHIND │ ├──── B ──────────┤ shadow ≠ Shopify → someone ELSE wrote └──────── C ───────────────────── ┘ source ≠ Shopify → drift, cause unknown
DifferenceMeansFix
A source ≠ shadowWe have not sent the latest yet. A missed change or a stuck watermarkRe-read and send
B shadow ≠ ShopifySomething else edited the field. A person in the admin, or another appOwnership question, not a bug. Decide, then enforce
C source ≠ Shopify, shadow agrees with neitherBoth moved. Genuine conflictApply the ownership rule from A3.2

That B row is the reason this is worth building. Without shadow, "the price is wrong" is unattributable — you cannot tell your own bug from a colleague editing the admin. With it, you can say which, and that changes the conversation from blame to a decision.

Compare the fields that cost money, not all of them

Comparing everything nightly is expensive against the rate limit. So pick by consequence, the same principle as the backlog:

CompareHow oftenBecause
Inventory quantity per locationEvery nightWrong = overselling
Price and compare-at priceEvery nightWrong = selling at the wrong price
Existence: in source but not Shopify, or the reverseEvery nightCatches tombstones you missed
Title, description, tags, imagesWeeklyWrong = untidy

Read it with a bulk operation, not a loop

A nightly full comparison is exactly what bulk query operations are for. Fetch the fields you compare in one asynchronous job rather than paging synchronously — which would spend your budget and take hours. And when you read counts, remember what A5 Step 0 found on a real store: productsCount returned 10000 with precision AT_LEAST, meaning a limit was reached. Counts are capped as well as uninformative.

Verified 2026-08-23: CountPrecision has two values — AT_LEAST ("a limit was imposed and reached") and EXACT, and even EXACT carries the caveat that "a write may not be reflected instantaneously" — CountPrecision. So a count read one second after a write can legitimately disagree with the write.

Then output a work list, not a percentage

"99.7% in sync" is not actionable. The output of reconciliation is a list of records with the field, both values, and the diagnosis. Drift becomes a queue of fixable items — and the count of unresolved items is the one number worth alerting on.

Answer before reading on Reconciliation reports zero differences, every night, for two weeks. Name two ways that could be a false clean report.

First: you are comparing shadow against Shopify only, and both are stale. They agree with each other and neither matches the source. You proved you sent what you sent. Second: the comparison set is empty — a filter matches no rows, or the watermark is in the future, so you compare nothing and find nothing. Both report zero. The defence is to always publish the number of records compared alongside the number of differences. Zero differences out of zero records is not a pass, and it looks identical to one unless you print both.

Where it hurts

Costs money — count-only reconciliation Every price off by one cent, or every stock level a week old, passes a count check perfectly. Teams ship this, believe they have a correctness check, and find the real drift from a customer.
Fails with no error — zero out of zero An empty comparison set reports zero differences. Publish the denominator or you cannot tell a pass from a no-op.
Fails with no error — comparing shadow to Shopify only That proves you sent what you sent. It cannot detect a missed change, because a change you never saw is in neither table. The source must be in the comparison.
Counts are capped and can be stale AT_LEAST means the number is a floor, not a value. And EXACT is not a freshness promise — a recent write may not be reflected. Never build a correctness check on a count alone, even a precise-looking one.
Auto-fixing every difference Tempting and wrong. A type-B difference means a human or another app wrote that field. Silently overwriting it starts a fight where your sync and a person take turns. Auto-fix only where ownership is unambiguous; queue the rest for a decision.
Judgement, not documentation — floating point on money Compare money as integer minor units, never as floats. 10.10 from one system and 10.1 from another are equal, and a naive string or float comparison may report drift every single night. A reconciliation that cries wolf gets ignored, which is worse than not having one.

What you say to a client/PM

When asked for one number that proves it works Every night we compare what we sent against what's actually in Shopify, field by field, for prices and stock. The report is "we checked 46,000 values and found 3 differences, here they are". If I only told you the product count matched, that would look reassuring and prove almost nothing. Gives the number, gives the denominator, and pre-emptively dismisses the weaker metric someone will otherwise ask for.
When a price is wrong and it is not your fault We sent 14.99 on Tuesday and I can show you that. It's 12.99 now, and we didn't change it — so someone edited it in the admin, or another app did. That's worth a decision: if staff need to override prices there, I'll stop overwriting that field. If not, I should be putting it back. Turns "who broke it" into "what should the rule be". Only possible because the shadow table exists.
When asked to make the sync fix everything automatically For prices and stock, yes — your ERP owns those, so I'll correct them. For anything your team edits in Shopify, I'd rather show you the difference than silently overwrite their work. Otherwise we get a tug-of-war where a person changes something and it reverts overnight. Says yes to most of it, and gives a vivid reason for the exception.

Remember this

  • Counts prove almost nothing. Missing-plus-duplicate, wrong prices, and stale stock all pass.
  • Three-way compare: source, shadow, Shopify. Each pair of differences has a distinct meaning.
  • shadow ≠ Shopify means someone else wrote it — an ownership decision, not a bug.
  • Compare by consequence. Stock and price nightly; text weekly.
  • Always publish the denominator. Zero out of zero looks exactly like a pass.
  • Counts are capped and can be stale. AT_LEAST is a floor; EXACT is not a freshness promise.
  • Compare money as integer minor units. A check that cries wolf gets ignored.

If you remember one thing: reconciliation is the only thing that measures correctness — everything else measures effort.
