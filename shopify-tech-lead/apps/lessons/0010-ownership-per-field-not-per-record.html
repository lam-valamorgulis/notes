Track 2 · A3 · Lesson 2

Ownership, per field not per record

The spine of the whole Track. Get this table right and most bugs never exist.

Your win today. A one-page table that answers "who decides?" for every field — and the ability to fill one in with a client in twenty minutes, using questions they can answer.

How it works

Derive it, do not memorise it

From A3.1: two copies can disagree. So for any given fact, either you decide in advance who wins, or the outcome is decided by timing. There is no third option. Timing is not a policy — it is the absence of one.

Now the question people get wrong. At what granularity do you decide? The instinct is per record: "the ERP owns products, Shopify owns orders." Test that instinct against one real product.

Field on one productWho genuinely decides?Why
title, descriptionERPStaff type it there. It is a catalogue fact
priceERPSet by buying and margin rules, not by the shop
SKU / identityERPIt is their primary key. Yours is derived from it
inventory quantityShopifyA sale happens here, now, and decrements it
imagesEither — pick oneOften uploaded in Shopify by a marketer
SEO title, handleShopifyThe ERP has no concept of it
tagsthe dangerous oneBoth sides write them, for different reasons

Ownership splits inside a single record. "The ERP owns products" is false for that product's quantity, and quantity is the field that costs money when it is wrong. Per-record ownership is not a simplification — it is a wrong answer that sounds tidy.

The three legitimate patterns

1. one-way, owned upstream ERP ──▶ Shopify (title, price) conflict is impossible. do this wherever you can. 2. one-way, owned downstream ERP ◀── Shopify (quantity, orders) same thing, other direction. 3. shared, with a rule ERP ◀──▶ Shopify (tags, images) ONLY with a written rule. this is where bugs live.

Pattern 3 is not forbidden, but it must never be the default. Every field placed there needs a written rule, and the rule has to be more specific than "newest wins" — because A3.4 shows you cannot reliably tell which is newest.

Splitting a shared field instead of sharing it

The best fix for a contested field is usually to stop contesting it. Tags are the classic case: both sides want to write tags, for unrelated reasons.

Instead ofDo this
Both systems write the tag listThe ERP owns tags with its prefix; Shopify owns the rest. Your sync only ever adds and removes its own prefix
Both write the imagesERP images go to a metafield; Shopify's media stays Shopify's

You turned one shared field into two owned fields. Pattern 3 became pattern 1 twice, and the conflict stopped existing rather than being resolved.

A real store, checked This Course sampled a live Plus store. Tags there were entirely freeform — mean 17 per product, up to 59, around 650 distinct values in a 250-product sample, and not one using a Prefix: value convention. So the prefix trick above is not something you will inherit; it is something you must introduce, and introducing it late means a migration.
Answer before reading on Inventory is owned by Shopify. The ERP is the system of record for the catalogue and it also tracks stock, because that is what an ERP does. Is the ERP's stock number wrong?

No — it is a different number. The ERP knows warehouse stock, including things not for sale online. Shopify knows sellable stock at its locations. Treating them as one field is the mistake; they are two fields with a relationship. Ownership is not about which number is correct, it is about which one your sync is allowed to overwrite. Overwrite Shopify's sellable quantity with a warehouse total and you will oversell, with both systems technically holding correct data.

Where it hurts

Costs money — overwriting sellable stock with warehouse stock Both numbers are right, the mapping is wrong, and the result is overselling: orders you cannot fulfil, refunds, and a customer who does not come back. This is the single most expensive ownership mistake available.
Costs money — per-record ownership "The ERP owns products" reads as sensible and quietly claims the quantity field. One nightly run then resets every stock level to a catalogue figure. The damage is store-wide and immediate.
Fails with no error — the shared tag list Your sync writes the tag list wholesale, so it deletes the marketing team's tags. They add them back. You delete them tonight. Nobody files a bug for weeks because each side blames the other's process. Own a prefix, never the list.
Fails with no error — "newest wins" as a rule It sounds fair and it depends on comparing two clocks you do not control. A3.4 covers why that comparison is unreliable. A rule that quietly resolves to "whichever machine's clock drifted" is not a rule.
Ownership agreed in a meeting and not written down Six weeks later someone adds a field and guesses. The table has to live in the repo next to the code — a file, reviewable in a diff. A shared document nobody opens is the same as no decision.
Judgement, not documentation — the field owned by a human process Sometimes the honest answer is "a person decides, ad hoc". That is a valid finding and a bad input to a sync. Surface it: either agree a system owner, or exclude the field from the sync entirely. Do not let your code arbitrate a business disagreement.

Say it out loud

Explain this to a beginner

"The warehouse system owns products." Explain why that sentence is already a bug. Then explain why "whoever edited most recently wins" is not a rule.

Say it out loud. Then read on.

Now compare with a plain-words answer

First part: "owns products" quietly claims every field on the product, including things the warehouse has no business deciding. It claims the marketing description. It claims the photos. It claims the price. Somebody edits a description in Shopify to fix a typo, and tonight the warehouse sends its own version and wipes it.

Ownership does not split by record. It splits inside a record, field by field. The warehouse knows how many there are. It does not know how to sell them.

Second part: "most recent wins" sounds like a rule but you cannot actually tell which is most recent. The two systems keep time separately, their clocks are not the same, and neither timestamp means the same thing. So you are comparing two numbers that were never comparable, and the winner is effectively decided by whichever job happened to run last.

That is not a policy. That is timing making the decision for you.

The way out is boring and reliable: decide up front, per field, who is allowed to write it. And when two sides genuinely both need input, do not share the field — split it. Own a tag prefix rather than the tag list. Keep "how many are in the warehouse" and "how many we can sell" as two separate fields, because they are two separate facts.

If you used any of these words, try again last-write-wins · vector clock · field-level ownership · authoritative source · merge strategy. "The warehouse knows how many there are; it does not know how to sell them" is the explanation.

Build it

Task · 45 minutes Dev store

Destroy a description on purpose. It takes ten minutes and it ends the argument forever.

  1. Set your sync to write the whole product, "because the ERP owns products".
  2. Edit a product description in Shopify, the way a marketer would.
  3. Run the sync. Watch the edit disappear.
  4. Now restrict the sync to the fields the other system genuinely owns. Repeat.
  5. Find one contested field and split it — own erp- prefixed tags rather than the tag list.

Proof it worked: the marketer's edit survives a sync run, and your tag logic can add and remove its own tags without ever touching a human's. Keep the before-and-after as evidence for the next person who says "the ERP owns products".

The two-stock-fields exercise · 30 minutes no store needed

Write down, as two separate named fields, "how many are physically in the warehouse" and "how many we are willing to sell". Then write the rule that derives the second from the first.

Proof it worked: they are two fields with two owners, not one field with an argument. This is the same distinction as on_hand versus available, and collapsing them is the most expensive mistake in this Course.

Remember this

  • Either you decide who wins, or timing decides. Timing is not a policy.
  • Ownership splits inside one record. "The ERP owns products" claims the quantity field and is wrong.
  • Three patterns: owned upstream, owned downstream, or shared-with-a-written-rule. Prefer the first two.
  • Split a contested field instead of sharing it. Own a tag prefix, never the tag list.
  • Warehouse stock and sellable stock are different fields, not one field with two values.
  • "Newest wins" is not a rule, because you cannot reliably tell which is newest.
  • The table lives in the repo, reviewable in a diff. A meeting decision is not a decision.

If you remember one thing: ask "who decides this field?" — and if the answer is "both", you have found the next bug before it happens.
