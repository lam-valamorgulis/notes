Track 2 · A4 · Lesson 1

Integration patterns: ERP, CRM, payments, fulfillment

Four shapes. Almost every brief you will ever get is one of them.

Your win today. A client describes their integration and you place it in one of four shapes within a minute — which tells you the direction of flow, the failure you should fear most, and the questions to ask before estimating.

How it works

Derive the shapes from the direction of value

Do not memorise a list of vendors. Ask a first-principles question instead: which way does the important information travel, and what happens if it arrives late? The answers cluster into four.

1. CATALOGUE IN ERP ──▶ Shopify products, prices late = wrong price on sale 2. EVENT OUT Shopify ──▶ system orders, customers late = an order nobody picks 3. DECISION DURING Shopify ◀──▶ system payment, tax, shipping rates late = the customer sees a spinner and leaves 4. STATE BOTH WAYS Shopify ◀──▶ system inventory late = overselling
ShapeTypical vendorTimingFear most
1 Catalogue inERP, PIM, supplier feedMinutes to dailyWrong price live
2 Event outCRM, accounting, warehouse, marketingSeconds to minutesA lost or duplicated order
3 Decision duringPayment gateway, tax, carrier ratesMillisecondsCheckout abandonment
4 State both waysInventory, POS, marketplacesSecondsOverselling

Shape 3 is a different job

This is the distinction worth internalising. Shapes 1, 2 and 4 are asynchronous. You may retry, queue, and be eventually consistent, and the customer never knows. Shape 3 is synchronous and inside a customer's checkout. There is no queue and no retry — a slow answer is a lost sale.

So everything this Course has taught about backlogs and reconciliation does not apply to shape 3. Its rules are different and harsher:

Async shapes (1, 2, 4)Shape 3 — decision during
Retry with backoffOne attempt, hard timeout
Eventually consistent is fineMust answer now or not at all
Queue on failureFail open or fail closed — decide in advance
Reconcile nightlyNothing to reconcile; the moment is gone

"Fail open or fail closed" is the whole design question. If your tax service is down, do you block checkout (fail closed, lose the sale, never charge wrong tax) or let it through with a fallback (fail open, keep the sale, risk a wrong number)? The useful heuristic: fail open on information, fail closed on a promise. A missing "available in store" badge is information — hide it. Reserving stock for click-and-collect is a promise — refuse rather than promise falsely.

The questions each shape forces

ShapeAsk before estimating
1 Catalogue inCan you tell me what changed since a timestamp? Cursor or page numbers? How are deletions signalled?
2 Event outIs your endpoint idempotent? What identifier do you dedupe on? What do you do with a refund?
3 Decision duringWhat is your p99 latency? What is your uptime commitment? What should we do when you are down?
4 State both waysWho owns the number? Is it warehouse stock or sellable stock? What is your smallest update interval?

Those are twelve questions that cost nothing to ask and routinely change an estimate by weeks. The deletion question alone — from A3.3 — can turn a simple feed into a nightly full comparison.

Answer before reading on A client wants their loyalty provider to show a customer's point balance on the checkout page. Which shape, and what is the first question?

Shape 3, decision during — even though it feels like a simple read. It is inside checkout, so it is synchronous and latency-bound. First question: what is their p99 latency, and what do we show when they are slow or down? And the answer here is clear from the heuristic: a point balance is information, so fail open — hide the panel rather than delay the checkout. Anyone who treats this as "just an API call" ships a checkout that hangs when a third party has a bad afternoon.

Where it hurts

Costs money — treating shape 3 like the others Adding a retry loop inside checkout multiplies the delay instead of fixing it. Customers leave. This is the most expensive category error in integration work, because the loss is invisible — abandoned carts do not raise tickets.
Costs money — no fail-open/fail-closed decision Undecided means the code decides by accident, usually by throwing. Then a third party's outage becomes your checkout outage. Write the choice down per integration, per field.
Fails with no error — a shape-2 endpoint that is not idempotent You retry an order push after a timeout that actually succeeded. The CRM now has two orders, the warehouse ships twice. Nothing errored — you did exactly what a good client should do. Ask about idempotency before you build the retry.
A brief that is two shapes wearing one name "Integrate our ERP" usually means shape 1 and shape 4 — catalogue in, inventory both ways. They have different owners, different risks and different timing. Estimating them as one is how a project doubles.
Judgement, not documentation — the vendor's timing is a constraint, not a target If a supplier publishes a feed once a day, no amount of engineering makes your stock hourly. Find the vendor's real cadence early and set expectations from it, rather than promising a freshness you cannot source.

What you say to a client/PM

First meeting, placing the work Let me sort this into the pieces that behave differently. Products and prices coming in is one job. Orders going out is another. Stock going both ways is the risky one. And anything that has to answer during checkout is a different kind of job entirely, because there we get milliseconds, not minutes. Breaks one vague request into four with different risks, in language with no jargon. This is usually the moment a client realises the scope.
When a third party will sit inside checkout I need a number from them before I agree to this: how fast do they respond at their worst, and what's their uptime? Because if they're slow, your customers see a spinner and some of them leave. And I need your decision on what we do when they're down — hide the feature, or stop the checkout. I'd hide it. Makes the client's own decision explicit rather than absorbing it silently into code, and gives a recommendation.

Remember this

  • Four shapes: catalogue in, event out, decision during, state both ways.
  • Shape 3 is a different job. Synchronous, inside checkout, no retries, no reconciliation.
  • Fail open on information, fail closed on a promise. Decide per integration, in writing.
  • "Integrate our ERP" is usually two shapes — catalogue in plus inventory both ways.
  • Ask about idempotency before writing a retry for shape 2, or you duplicate orders.
  • Ask how deletions are signalled for shape 1. The answer can double the project.
  • The vendor's cadence caps your freshness. A daily feed cannot become hourly stock.

If you remember one thing: find out whether the integration lives inside a customer's checkout — everything else about the design follows from that answer.
