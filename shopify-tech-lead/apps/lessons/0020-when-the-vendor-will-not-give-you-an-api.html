Track 2 · A4 · Lesson 5

When the vendor will not give you an API

A real refusal, the four routes left, and how to say it to a client without blaming anyone.

Your win today. When a vendor says no, you have four costed options and a sentence that keeps the project moving instead of stalling it in a blame conversation.
Where this comes from The patterns below were learned from a real integration investigation against a live platform. Every company, client, tenant and traceable number has been removed on purpose. The pattern is the lesson; the identity is not.

How it works

The refusal is usually structural, not obstructive

Your first instinct is that the vendor is being difficult. Usually they are not. A platform can be genuinely unable to give you access, and recognising which situation you are in changes what you ask for.

What you observeWhat it usually means
No self-serve key page anywhere in their adminThey never built external access. Nobody is refusing you; the feature does not exist
Auth requires a secret only their own app hasTheir API exists solely to serve their own front end
Browser access controls locked to their own domainA third party must integrate server-side or not at all
"Read access only, for security reasons"A policy decision, which means it can be escalated — unlike a missing feature

That distinction is the useful one. A missing capability needs a roadmap conversation. A policy needs an escalation. Asking the wrong one wastes a fortnight.

Probe politely, and record exactly what you found

Before escalating, establish the facts, using only what you are entitled to use. An unauthenticated request that returns a clear "missing authentication" is a fact you can quote. An auth attempt that fails because a required secret was never shared with you is also a fact you can quote — and it is a far stronger position than "we could not get it working".

The line you do not cross Probing documented endpoints with credentials you were given is engineering. Scraping a UI you were not granted access to, reverse-engineering a mobile app's secret, or reusing a session token captured from a browser is not — regardless of how easy it is or how much the client wants it. Get written authorisation for anything beyond documented, credentialed access, and if it is refused, that answer is itself the finding you report.

The four routes, in order of preference

1. SERVER-SIDE with real credentials ← ask for this first they issue you a key, or the client's own account is used server-to-server, so browser restrictions do not apply 2. THEIR EXPORT ← the usual real answer a scheduled file. often already exists as a UI button becomes the A4.3 connector. boring and reliable 3. THE CLIENT AS THE INTEGRATION POINT ← underrated the client already has access. they push to you no vendor cooperation needed at all 4. INVERT: make Shopify the source ← the strategic answer stop reading from them. write to them, or work around them

Route 2 is where most of these end up, and it is not a defeat. A scheduled export is a legitimate integration with one genuine advantage over an API: a full snapshot answers the deletion question that APIs make hard. Check their admin UI for an export button before assuming there is nothing — the capability frequently exists as a manual feature nobody described to you as an integration.

Route 4 is the one to raise with a client when the vendor blocks you repeatedly. If the data you need can be owned in Shopify instead, the dependency disappears. That is a business conversation, not a technical one, and it belongs on the table.

Patterns to expect from a closed platform

Real findings, company removed. These recur:

  • One endpoint serves two very different screens. Their internal API is shaped for their UI, not for you, so a single call returns far more than you want and still misses one field you need.
  • A value is derived, never stored. A price computed from rules at read time cannot be read back as a stored field, and it is not reproducible from what they expose — the inputs are partly hidden. Do not promise a client you can recompute it.
  • Their identity format encodes a location. A key like {itemId}_{locationId} means identity and place are fused, so a multi-location rollout re-opens your mapping design.
  • They hold a ledger you did not know about. Store credit or a balance living on their side means issuing the same thing in Shopify creates two ledgers for the same money. Find this before go-live, not after.
  • Ownership reverses for one field. They may own the catalogue while Shopify owns inventory, flowing back to them. Do not assume the system of record owns everything.
  • Their sync omits more than it sends. Expect gaps: per-variant images, cost, barcode, compare-at price, and most metadata. Enumerate what is missing early — it is usually a longer list than the client expects.
Answer before reading on A vendor's platform computes a buy price from rules. You have their exposed market price and the rule percentages. Your calculation gives $3.40; the platform shows $3.03. What do you tell the client?

That you cannot reproduce their number, and therefore will not display it as if you could. The gap means an input is hidden — a rounding rule, a different base price, a step you cannot see. Two bad answers: display your calculation (now you are showing a wrong price to customers), or keep tuning until one example matches (you have fitted to a sample of one, and it will break on the next). The correct answer is to read their number or show nothing, and to tell the client that recomputing it is not on the table. That sentence is worth more than a week of reverse engineering.

Where it hurts

Costs money — two ledgers for the same money The vendor holds a credit balance and you also issue store credit in Shopify. Both are real, neither knows about the other, and a customer can spend the same balance twice. This is the most expensive finding on the list and it is invisible until someone does it.
Costs money — recomputing a derived value You reproduce their formula from a small sample, it matches, and it ships. Then it diverges on data you never saw, and the store displays wrong prices. A formula fitted to one example is a guess wearing a decimal point.
Fails with no error — assuming the system of record owns everything They own the catalogue, so you assume they own inventory too, and you overwrite Shopify's stock with theirs. Both numbers were right; the direction was wrong. Establish ownership per field even when one system is obviously the master.
Fails with no error — identity that encodes a location A key that fuses item and location works perfectly for one location. The second location arrives, and every mapping assumption breaks at once — usually mid-rollout.
Escalating a missing feature Pushing hard for a key that does not exist as a product burns goodwill and weeks. Establish first whether you are facing an absent capability or a policy, and route accordingly.
Judgement, not documentation — throughput you cannot promise A closed platform publishes no rate limits. You do not know them, and you cannot discover them responsibly on someone else's production system. So refuse to quote a throughput figure. Say what you measured, on what, and that it is not a commitment.

What you say to a client/PM

When the vendor has said no They won't issue API access — I don't think that's them being difficult, I think they never built it for third parties. So here are our options, cheapest first: they already have an export button in their admin, and a scheduled version of that file would work well. Or you use your own login and push the data to us. Or we escalate for proper access, which is slower and might still be no. I'd start with the export. Removes the blame, names three costed options, recommends one. The client leaves the meeting with a decision to make rather than a problem to worry about.
When the client asks you to "just scrape it" I'd need that in writing from them, not from us. Without permission it risks breaching their terms, and it breaks the first time they change their page — usually silently, so we'd be showing stale data without knowing. If you can get written agreement I'll build it. Otherwise the export route is slower to set up and far cheaper to own. Does not lecture. Names the legal risk and the maintenance risk, then offers to proceed with authorisation. That is a lead's answer, not a refusal.
When you find the second ledger I've found something that needs a decision before we go further. Their system already tracks a credit balance per customer. If we also issue store credit in Shopify, you'd have two records of the same money and a customer could spend it twice. We need to pick one place for that balance to live — I'd say theirs, since it's already there. A finding, its consequence in plain money terms, the decision required, and a recommendation. Four sentences.

Remember this

  • Distinguish a missing capability from a policy. One needs a roadmap talk, the other an escalation.
  • Four routes: server-side credentials · their export · the client as the integration point · invert and own it in Shopify.
  • Their export is usually the real answer, and a full snapshot answers deletions better than an API.
  • Do not scrape without written authorisation. A refusal is itself a finding to report.
  • Never recompute a derived value you cannot reproduce exactly. Read it or show nothing.
  • Look for a second ledger. Two records of the same money is the most expensive thing you can miss.
  • Never quote throughput for a closed platform. No published limits means no commitment.

If you remember one thing: a vendor's "no" is a scoping input, not a blocker — arrive at the next meeting with four options and a recommendation.
