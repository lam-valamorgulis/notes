Absolute writes, safe replay and tombstones

Send the value, never the change. Then "deleted" has to become a value too.

Your win today. A sync you can re-run at any moment, from any point, without thinking about it — and which can tell the difference between "gone" and "not in this batch".

How it works

Derive it: what does a crash cost you?

A sync will be interrupted. Not might — will. A deploy, a timeout, a 429, a machine restart. So the honest starting question is not "how do we avoid interruption" but "what does an interruption cost?"

That cost is decided by one choice: whether your writes describe a change or a state.

DELTA ABSOLUTE "add 3 to stock" "stock is 12" "increase price by 2" "price is 14.99" send twice → 6 added ✗ send twice → still 12 ✓ crash mid-batch → unknown ✗ crash mid-batch → re-run ✓ replay → corrupts ✗ replay → harmless ✓

With deltas, a retry is a bug. You must therefore know exactly which writes landed, which means perfect bookkeeping across a network you do not control. That is the hard problem, and it is self-inflicted.

With absolute values the whole problem dissolves. Setting stock to 12 twice leaves it at 12. Retrying is free. You no longer need to know what landed, because sending it again costs nothing. Feynman version: an absolute write is a statement of fact, and repeating a true statement does not make it truer.

This is why the rule is not "prefer absolute writes". It is: absolute writes are what make everything else in this Chapter possible. Backlogs, replay, reconciliation repair — all of them assume you can re-send safely.

Idempotency is a property you build, not a promise you make

Absolute writes give you idempotency at the value level. You still need it at the message level, because a webhook can arrive twice and an ERP page can repeat a row. That is what the inbox table in A2.2 is for: the message id is a primary key, so two racing workers cannot both process it. The loser's insert fails.

Note the layering. Absolute writes make a duplicate harmless. The inbox makes it unnecessary. You want both, because they fail in different ways.

Then deletion breaks it

Absolute writes cover "this is the value". They say nothing about "this no longer exists". And here is the structural problem, from first principles:

your sync reads a page of changed items. item X is not in it. does that mean (a) X was deleted? (b) X did not change? (c) X is on a later page? (d) the source had an error? ── an ABSENCE cannot distinguish these. It carries no information.

So deletion has to be represented by a presence, not an absence. That is a tombstone: a record that says "this is gone", with a timestamp, that flows through your sync like any other change.

Hard delete — the row vanishesNoDeleted items live forever in Shopify
Soft delete — a flag flips, updatedAt movesYesIt arrives as a normal change you can act on

This is why the Fake ERP in the Project has a soft-delete flag from its first line. If the real vendor hard-deletes, you have a genuine problem to raise — and the only remedies are expensive: a periodic full-list comparison, or asking them for a deletions feed.

What "deleted" should mean in Shopify

Rarely an actual delete. Deleting a Shopify product destroys its id, its handle, its URL and its history — and if the ERP un-deletes it tomorrow, you cannot put it back. Prefer to archive or unpublish, and record the tombstone on your side. Reversible beats tidy.

Answer before reading on Your sync sends "set stock to 0" for an item, then crashes. It restarts and re-sends the same message. Meanwhile a customer bought nothing and the ERP still says 0. Any harm? Now answer the same question if the message had been "reduce stock by 5".

Absolute: no harm at all. Setting 0 twice leaves 0, and you never had to know whether the first attempt landed. Delta: you have reduced stock by 10 when the truth was 5, and the store now shows negative or blocked availability. Worse, you cannot detect it from the message — you would have to compare against the source, which is the reconciliation work you were trying to avoid. One choice removed a whole category of bug, and it was made before any code was written.

Where it hurts

Costs money — an ERP that only emits deltas Some genuinely do: "3 units received". You cannot make those safe to replay. The fix is not cleverness — it is to convert to absolute at the boundary by reading the current total after applying the delta, and to make that read part of the same job. If the vendor cannot give you a total, say so in writing before the estimate.
Costs money — deleting instead of archiving A Shopify product delete destroys the id, handle and URL. If the source un-deletes, you create a new product with a new URL, losing SEO and any external link. Archive, unpublish, and keep the mapping.
Fails with no error — the hard-deleted source row Items removed from the ERP simply stop appearing. Your sync sees nothing and does nothing. The products stay live and sellable for months. There is no error, no count mismatch, and no complaint until someone buys one.
Fails with no error — partial absolute writes Absolute means complete. Sending a product with three of its five variants, using a mutation that sets whole state, deletes the other two. "Absolute" and "partial" together are a destructive combination. Send whole objects.
Idempotency implemented as an if Checking "have I seen this id?" then inserting is two steps, and two workers can both pass the check. Make the id a primary key and let the database refuse the second one. A constraint cannot have a race condition.
Judgement, not documentation — tombstones need a lifetime Keep tombstones forever and the table grows without bound. Delete them too early and a replayed old message resurrects a deleted item. Keep them at least as long as your longest possible replay window, and write that number down next to the retention rule.

What you say to a client/PM

When a client asks what happens if the sync crashes mid-run We just run it again. Every update we send is a complete statement — "this price is 14.99" — rather than an instruction like "add two". So sending it twice changes nothing. That's deliberate, and it's why a failed run isn't an incident. Turns a scary question into a designed-for answer, with a concrete example the client can picture.
When you discover the vendor hard-deletes records There's a gap I need to flag. When you remove a product from your ERP, nothing tells us — it just stops appearing, which looks identical to "unchanged". So deleted items would stay on sale. Two options: ask them for a deleted-items flag, or we compare full lists nightly, which is slower and costs more. I'd push for the flag. Names the invisible failure, gives two costed options, and states a preference. That is the shape of every good escalation.
When someone asks you to delete products that leave the catalogue I'd rather archive them. Deleting destroys the product's web address and its history, so if it comes back next season it comes back as a new page with no search ranking. Archived is invisible to customers and reversible in one click. Frames a reversibility argument in terms of SEO and next season, which are things the client already worries about.

Remember this

  • Send the value, not the change. "Stock is 12", never "add 3".
  • Absolute writes make replay free, which is what every other technique in this Chapter assumes.
  • Absolute must also mean complete. A partial payload to a whole-state mutation deletes the rest.
  • Idempotency at two levels: absolute values make a duplicate harmless, the inbox primary key makes it unnecessary.
  • An absence carries no information. Deleted, unchanged, next page, and source error look identical.
  • Deletion needs a tombstone — a presence that says "gone", flowing like any other change.
  • Archive, do not delete, in Shopify. A delete destroys the id, handle and URL irreversibly.

If you remember one thing: make every write safe to repeat, and a crash stops being an incident.
