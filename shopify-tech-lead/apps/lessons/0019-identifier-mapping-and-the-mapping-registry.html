Track 2 · A4 · Lesson 4

Identifier mapping and the mapping registry

Their id, your id, Shopify's id. This is where syncs die.

Your win today. A matching strategy that survives a product being renamed, a variant being re-created, and a vendor changing their SKU format — plus the one metric that tells you whether your mapping is actually any good.

How it works

Derive it: what does "the same product" even mean?

Two systems each hold a record. You claim they are the same thing. On what basis? Every sync answers this, and most answer it badly by accident.

Match onSurvives?Fails when
TitleNoAnyone edits a word. Two products share a name
HandleNoThe title changes and the handle is regenerated
SKUMostlyThe vendor changes format, or reuses a retired SKU
BarcodeMostlyBlank on many products; shared across variants
Shopify's numeric idYes, until it changesVariant ids are destroyed and recreated by some edits
A stored mappingYesYou lose the table

The conclusion is unavoidable: never derive identity, store it. Matching on any attribute means identity depends on data that people are allowed to edit. A stored mapping means identity is a fact you own.

The variant-id trap

This one catches almost everyone. Changing a product's option structure can delete and recreate its variants — which means new variant ids. Anything holding the old ids now points at nothing, silently.

So your mapping must key variants on something stable across that operation. In practice: the source system's own variant key, held in your registry, with the Shopify id treated as a cached value that may need re-resolving rather than a permanent truth.

Hub, not pairwise — and the reason

PAIRWISE HUB erp_id ↔ shopify_id erp_id ─┐ erp_id ↔ market_id ├─▶ local_id ◀─┬─ shopify_id shopify_id ↔ market_id │ └─ market_id ... pos_id ─┘ n systems → n(n-1)/2 links n systems → n links 3 systems → 3 links adding one = an INSERT 5 systems → 10 links not a migration

Your own id in the middle. This is the id_map table from A2.2, and both unique constraints matter: one external id must not map to two locals, and one local must not map to two external ids in the same system. Only writing the first is the usual mistake, and it lets one product acquire two Shopify records.

Let Shopify hold the mapping too

A stored mapping in your database is authoritative, but writing the source's id into Shopify as well is cheap and pays for itself. It gives you a mapping that survives losing your database, and it lets a human in the admin answer "what is this in the ERP?".

Shopify supports upserting by an identifier rather than by its own id, which lets you write and match without a lookup round trip:

mutation UpsertByCustomId($input: ProductSetInput!, $identifier: ProductSetIdentifiers!) {
  productSet(input: $input, identifier: $identifier) {
    product { id handle }
    userErrors { field message code }
  }
}

Validated against the 2026-07 Admin schema on 2026-08-23 — productSet accepts an identifier argument of type ProductSetIdentifiers. Required scopes: write_products, read_products. Check the current field names in GraphiQL on your own store before building against it; identifier support differs by resource type, and it is broader for lookup than for create-or-update.

Found on a real store A live integration this Course sampled wrote its external ids into product metafields — but the product-level id corresponded to only one of the variants. Parsing it at product level therefore matched that variant and silently mismatched every other. If you store an external id, store it at the level it actually identifies.

The metric that tells you if your mapping is good

Coverage as a percentage of rows is misleading. Weight it by something that matters:

"97% of 50,000 SKUs are mapped" sounds excellent → 1,500 unmapped products invisible on the storefront weight by SALES instead: "we cover 99.4% of units sold" and name the gap → the 1,500 include your 3 bestsellers? then it is a bad number

Report both: coverage by row, and coverage weighted by units sold. A gap in the long tail is a nuisance. A gap in a bestseller is an emergency, and a row-count percentage cannot tell them apart.

Answer before reading on You match on SKU. A vendor retires ABC-100, then eighteen months later reuses the same SKU for a different product. What happens, and when do you notice?

Your registry still holds a mapping for ABC-100, so the new product updates the old Shopify product — new title, new price, on a page that has the old product's URL, reviews and history. Nothing errors, because from the sync's point of view a mapped record was updated normally. You notice when a customer buys the wrong thing, or never. The defence is a tombstone on retirement plus a rule that a mapping for a retired key is not reusable — the new row needs a new local id. This is why deletions from A3.3 and identity are the same problem.

Where it hurts

Costs money — a reused SKU The scenario above. A new product silently overwrites an old one on its existing URL. Tombstone retired keys and refuse to reuse a mapping.
Costs money — matching on title or handle A marketer fixes a typo. Your next run cannot find the product, creates a duplicate, and both are now live. Sales split, inventory splits, and reconciliation reports drift on both.
Fails with no error — the recreated variant id A product option change destroys and recreates variants. Your cached Shopify variant ids point nowhere. Writes fail or, worse, silently target nothing. Treat the Shopify id as a cache and be able to re-resolve it.
Fails with no error — a product-level id that identifies one variant Verified on a real store. Parsing it product-level matches one variant and mismatches the rest, with no error at any layer. Store an identifier at the level it identifies.
Only one unique constraint Guard both directions or a single local record acquires two Shopify products. You find out by seeing duplicates in the admin, which is a bad way to find out.
Coverage measured by row count 97% sounds like a pass and hides 1,500 invisible products, possibly including bestsellers. Always report coverage weighted by units sold alongside the row percentage.
Judgement, not documentation — blank and shared barcodes Barcode looks like a natural key and is frequently blank, and sometimes shared across variants of one product. A unique index on it will fail at import time on a real catalogue. Verify uniqueness on the client's actual data before designing around it.

What you say to a client/PM

Before building, asking about identity I need one thing that never changes for a product. Not the name — those get edited. Something like a product code that stays the same for the life of the product. And one important question: do you ever reuse a code after discontinuing something? Because if so I need to handle that, or a new product will overwrite an old one. Asks for the stable key and pre-empts the reuse trap in the same breath. Most clients have never been asked the second question.
When 1,500 products are unmapped We're matching 97% of your catalogue, but I'd rather show you it differently: those 1,500 unmatched products include four of your top sellers, so it's more serious than 3% sounds. Here's the list — they mostly have blank product codes, so I need those filled in or a rule for what to do without one. Refuses to let a comfortable percentage stand, and hands over a concrete action rather than a problem.

Remember this

  • Never derive identity, store it. Matching on any editable attribute makes identity fragile.
  • Titles and handles are not keys. They change, and handles regenerate.
  • Treat Shopify ids as a cache. An option change can destroy and recreate variant ids.
  • Hub, not pairwise. Your id in the middle: n links, and a new system is an insert.
  • Both unique constraints, or one record acquires two Shopify products.
  • Write the external id into Shopify too, at the level it actually identifies.
  • Report coverage weighted by units sold, not only by row count. And tombstone retired keys.

If you remember one thing: identity is something you record, never something you compute — and a reused key is the quietest disaster in this trade.

Ask questions. Then "next" for the vendor who will not give you an API at all.
