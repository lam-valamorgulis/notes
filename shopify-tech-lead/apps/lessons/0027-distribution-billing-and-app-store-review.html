Distribution, billing and App Store review

Only matters if the client sells the app. But the decision is made on day one and cannot be undone cheaply.

Your win today. You can tell a client what changes the day they decide to sell their integration to someone else — and why that is a different project, not a feature.

How it works

Derive it: what does "selling the app" actually require?

A custom app is one merchant's plumbing. A public app is a product with customers, and Shopify's requirements follow from that: someone must be able to trust it, pay for it, and get support for it.

CustomPublic
Installed byOne storeAny store, via the App Store
App reviewNoYes
Billing APICannot use it at allYes
Mandatory compliance webhooksNot required by the App StoreRequired — even if you store no personal data
Protected customer dataNot part of an App Store reviewApproval, and at level 2 a data protection review
Support obligationsBetween you and the clientDocumentation, and a current emergency contact

The Billing API row is the commercially decisive one. A custom app cannot charge through Shopify. The client bills you or the customer directly — usually what they want, until they decide to resell.

Free testing, and how to detect a dev store

If you distribute publicly, you will want your app free on development stores so other partners can try it. The detection is a documented one-liner:

{ "partnerDevelopment": true }   # from ShopPlan

That is queried from the ShopPlan resource, and it is exactly the check this Course uses to confirm a store is safe to load synthetic data into. Two useful follow-ons: subscribe to SHOP_UPDATE so you know if a development store converts to a paid plan — at which point you block access and create a charge — and a free-for-dev-stores app can be added to Shopify's Partner-friendly app list by contacting support.

What review actually checks

The App Store requirements are the same checklist the review team uses, and the categories that matter most for integration work:

AreaWhat it means for you
Compliance webhooksThe three topics from A1.4, implemented, regardless of what you store
Minimum scopesApproval depends on requesting the minimum needed for the functionality
Theme app extensions, not manual editsOnline store apps must not require merchants to paste code into a theme
App proxies for storefront dataThe sanctioned way to forward requests and show data on the storefront
Minimal brandingStandard attribution at 24×24 pixels unless customers interact with the branded element directly
Preview before publishMerchants must be able to preview storefront changes before they go live
Support and contactShopify-specific documentation, and a current emergency developer contact in the Partner Dashboard

That last row is not paperwork. The emergency contact is where webhook failure warnings are sent — the ones that arrive before your subscription is deleted after eight consecutive failures. A stale contact means the warning goes nowhere.

Built for Shopify raises the bar again

Beyond review, Built for Shopify status has its own requirements — and they are specific per app category. For subscriptions, for example, they mandate the Selling plan, Subscription contract and Customer payment method APIs, an Online Store 2.0 app block on the product page, plan details on product, cart and order pages, theme-matched styling by default, Customer Account UI extensions for self-service, and the Customer Account API as the primary authentication method — as covered in P9.2.

Status also unlocks practical things: a Built for Shopify app can request an exception to the 100 KB theme app extension Liquid limit, as can an app using custom distribution.

Answer before reading on Your client's ERP integration works beautifully. Two of their suppliers ask to use it. The client asks how long it would take to sell it. What is the honest answer?

It is a new project, not an extension of this one — and the reasons are structural rather than about effort.

Distribution changes. Custom becomes public, which means App Store review, mandatory compliance webhooks even though you store nothing personal, and protected customer data approval.

Billing appears from nothing. A custom app cannot use the Billing API, so there is currently no charging mechanism at all. That is new work, plus pricing decisions the client has not made.

Multi-tenancy arrives. Everything in A3.7 — per-store credentials, per-store watermarks, per-store rate budgets, failure isolation. If the store id was a parameter from line one, this is manageable. If it was a global, it is a rewrite.

So the honest answer is: "weeks, and mostly not the parts you'd expect — the sync barely changes; the distribution, billing and multi-store plumbing is the work." And ask the question A1.1 said to ask on day one: was this ever going to be resold? If the answer was yes then, some of this is already done.

Where it hurts

Costs money — choosing custom, then needing to sell it No Billing API, no review passed, no compliance webhooks, no multi-tenancy. Each is a real piece of work and the combination reads as a rebuild.
Costs money — assuming compliance webhooks are optional Required for every App Store app regardless of whether it collects personal data. "We don't store customers" is not an exemption.
Fails with no error — a stale emergency developer contact It is where webhook failure warnings are sent. Stale, and the warnings before your subscription is deleted go nowhere. Then the app silently receives nothing.
Requiring merchants to paste code into a theme App Store expectations are explicit: use theme app extensions. An install process involving copied snippets is both a review problem and a support burden forever.
Over-branding the storefront Standard attribution is 24×24 pixels unless customers interact with the branded element. Bigger reads as self-promotion in someone else's shop.
Not detecting development stores Without the partnerDevelopment check and SHOP_UPDATE, a store that converts to a paid plan keeps free access indefinitely.
Judgement, not documentation — ask the resale question on day one "Will anyone else ever install this?" costs nothing to ask at the start and is expensive to answer late. Even a "probably not" is worth recording, because it justifies the custom-app decision later when someone questions it.

What you say to a client/PM

On day one One question that shapes the build: will anyone other than you ever install this? If it's only ever yours, we build it as a custom app — faster, no Shopify review. If there's a real chance you'd sell it to suppliers or peers, I'd build differently from the start, because retrofitting that is significant work rather than a feature. Gets the decision on the record when it is free, which is the entire point.
When resale comes up later Genuinely possible, and I want to be straight that it's a new project rather than an add-on. The sync itself barely changes. What's new is Shopify's app review, billing — a custom app can't charge through Shopify at all — and making it work for many stores at once with separate credentials and separate rate limits. Weeks, and mostly on the parts you can't see. Names three concrete pieces of work and says the surprising part out loud: the valuable code is not the expensive part.

Remember this

  • Custom apps cannot use the Billing API. The commercially decisive difference.
  • Ask "will anyone else install this?" on day one. Free then, expensive later.
  • Compliance webhooks are required for every App Store app, even if you store nothing personal.
  • Keep the emergency developer contact current — webhook failure warnings go there.
  • Theme app extensions, not pasted snippets. App proxies for storefront data.
  • Detect dev stores with partnerDevelopment and watch SHOP_UPDATE for conversions.
  • Going public also means multi-tenancy — all of A3.7 arrives at once.

If you remember one thing: distribution is chosen on day one and changing it later is a new project — so ask the resale question in the first meeting.
