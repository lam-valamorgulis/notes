Track 1 · P6 · Lesson 3

The huge catalogue load, and the throttle that starts at 500,000

This number decides whether a project is a month or a year. It moved once already.

Your win today. You can size a large catalogue load honestly, and you know why quoting a number you have not measured on the real store is the mistake that ends projects.

How it works

The throttle, as it stands today

RuleValue
Threshold before the extra throttle applies500,000 product variants on the store
After thatNo more than 10,000 new variants per day
Plus storesExempt
AffectsproductCreate, productUpdate, productVariantCreate, and the matching REST product endpoints
Signal429, with a message that a throttle has been applied

So below half a million variants this throttle never fires, and what actually paces a load is the ordinary rate limit from P1.2 — plus, on a non-Plus store, the smaller REST bucket.

Verified 2026-08-23 on both pages, which now agree — Shopify API limits and REST Admin API rate limits.

Why this Lesson used to say something else

The number moved, and that is the real lesson As of 3 August 2026, two Shopify pages disagreed about this throttle: one said the threshold was 50,000 variants with a cap of 1,000 per day, the other said 500,000 and 10,000 per day. Both agreed Plus was exempt.

Re-checked on 23 August 2026, they agree — shopify.dev was corrected upward to the looser figure. So the number changed by a factor of ten in under three weeks of checking.

The habit survives even though the disagreement did not. A number that can move like that is a number you measure on the client's real store before you put it in a proposal.

Derive it: why does one number decide a project?

Take a 300,000-variant catalogue and do the arithmetic under each figure:

at 10,000 new variants/day → 300,000 / 10,000 = 30 days at 1,000 new variants/day → 300,000 / 1,000 = 300 days same catalogue. same code. same client. one is a project. the other is not deliverable.

That is why this is not trivia. Almost no other single fact in Shopify development can change a timeline by 10×, and a proposal built on the wrong one is a commercial failure rather than a technical one.

How to size a load honestly

StepWhat
1Count variants, not products. Products are what clients count; variants are what the platform limits
2Do not trust productsCount. On a real store it returned 10,000 with precision AT_LEAST — a floor, not a value
3Read the store's plan. Plus changes both the buckets and the throttle exemption
4Load a real sample — say 5,000 variants — and measure the wall-clock rate
5Extrapolate from that, and quote a range with the measurement date

Step 2 is worth dwelling on. This Course queried a live Plus store and both productsCount and productVariantsCount returned exactly 10000 with precision: AT_LEAST, which the schema defines as "a limit was imposed and reached". You cannot size a catalogue from a count query. To get a true figure, page the connection or run a bulk query.

And even EXACT is not a freshness promise — the documentation notes that "a write may not be reflected instantaneously".

Answer before reading on A client says they have 80,000 products and asks how long the initial load takes. What do you need before you answer, and what do you say now?

You need four things: the variant count rather than the product count, their plan, a measured rate from a real sample load, and the date you measured it. 80,000 products at four variants each is 320,000 variants — still under the 500,000 threshold, so the extra throttle would not apply, but that is a conclusion you reached from a number you have not verified.

What you say now is a range with the reason for the range: it depends on variant count and plan, and you will have a real number after one sample load. Naming why you will not guess is what makes the eventual number believable.

Where it hurts

Costs money — quoting from a remembered limit This exact number changed by 10× between two checks three weeks apart. A timeline built on a remembered figure is a commitment built on nothing. Date every limit you quote.
Costs money — sizing from products instead of variants Clients count products; the platform limits variants. A four-variant average makes the real number four times bigger, and every estimate derived from the wrong unit is wrong by that factor.
Fails with no error — sizing from productsCount It can return a capped value with precision: AT_LEAST. Verified on a real store at exactly 10,000. Read the precision or you are planning against a floor.
Fails with no error — testing on Plus, shipping to Basic Plus has ten times the REST bucket and no variant throttle. A load that flew in testing crawls in production, and nothing in your code changed.
Assuming the throttle is the bottleneck when it is not Below 500,000 variants it never fires. If a load is slow there, the cause is the ordinary cost-based limit or your own query size — and waiting will not fix an over-1,000-point query.
Judgement, not documentation — do not measure on a client's live store A sample load teaches you the rate, and doing it on a production store spends the merchant's rate limit and creates products you must remove. Measure on a Dev store of the same plan, and say that is what you did.

Say it out loud

Explain this to a beginner

A client has 400,000 sellable items to load. Another has 600,000. Explain why the second one might be a completely different project, and why you would not trust the published limit to decide.

Say it out loud. Then read on.

Now compare with a plain-words answer

Above a certain size, Shopify stops letting you add new sellable items quickly. It puts a daily cap on them. So below the line you are limited by ordinary speed, and above the line you are limited by the calendar. Same work, but one is a few days and the other can be most of a year.

That is why one number decides a whole project, and why "how many products?" is the wrong question. Clients count products; the platform counts the individual sellable versions. A shop with 10,000 products and five sizes each has 50,000 sellable items. The multiplier is where estimates die.

Now the honest part. That threshold number moved by a factor of ten inside three weeks while this Course was being written, and two of Shopify's own pages disagreed about it at the same time. So quoting it from memory is not carefulness, it is luck.

Which is why you measure instead. Load a real sample onto a store on the same plan as theirs, time it, and extrapolate from your own number. Then quote that, with the date you measured it.

One more trap: even asking "how many products are there?" can give you a floor rather than a real answer, because the count can come back as "at least this many".

If you used any of these words, try again throttle threshold · rate ceiling · extrapolation · SKU count · precision hint. "Below the line you are limited by speed; above it you are limited by the calendar" is the explanation.

Build it

Task · 2 hours Dev store on the client's plan

Produce your own number, and never quote anyone else's again.

  1. Count variants, not products. Write both numbers down side by side so the multiplier is visible.
  2. Load a real sample — a few thousand variants — onto a Dev store on the same plan as the client's. Time it.
  3. Extrapolate to the full catalogue. Write the date next to your figure.
  4. Check whether the client is above or below 500,000 variants, and whether they are on Plus (Plus is exempt from the extra throttle).

Proof it worked: your estimate is a measurement with a date, not a quoted limit. If someone challenges it you can say "I loaded 3,000 variants on a store like theirs on this date and it took this long" — which no doc page can contradict.

The count that is not a count · 15 minutes Dev store

Query productsCount and read the precision field, not just the number.

Proof it worked: you have seen AT_LEAST with your own eyes — it was verified returning exactly 10,000 on a real store, meaning "10,000 or more". And note that EXACT is not a freshness promise either: a recent write may not be reflected yet.

Remember this

  • The extra throttle starts at 500,000 variants, then caps new ones at 10,000 per day. Plus is exempt.
  • This number moved 10× in three weeks of checking. Date every limit you quote.
  • Count variants, not products. Clients count products; the platform limits variants.
  • productsCount can return AT_LEAST — a floor, not a value. Verified at 10,000 on a real store.
