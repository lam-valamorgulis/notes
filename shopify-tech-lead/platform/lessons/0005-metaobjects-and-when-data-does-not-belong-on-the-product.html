Track 1 · P2 · Lesson 2

Metaobjects, and when data does not belong on the product

The moment two products need the same size chart, it stops being a product field.

Your win today. A rule for choosing between a metafield and a metaobject that takes five seconds and is almost always right.

How it works

Derive it: when does a field stop being a field?

A metafield holds a value on one resource. Fine for "fabric: linen". Now the client wants a size chart — several sizes, each with several measurements — and forty products share the same chart.

Store it as a metafield on each product and you have copied the chart forty times. Change one measurement and you must find forty copies. That is not a Shopify problem; it is the oldest problem in data modelling, and the answer is the same as it has always been: give the thing its own identity and point at it.

METAFIELD METAOBJECT a value ON a thing a THING of its own, referenced product ── fabric: "linen" product ─┐ product ── fabric: "linen" product ─┼──▶ [ Size Chart: Womens Tops ] product ── fabric: "cotton" product ─┘ S · M · L rows edit = one product edit = one place, forty products update

Feynman version: a metafield is an adjective, a metaobject is a noun. "Linen" describes a product. A size chart is a thing that exists whether or not any product points at it.

The five-second rule

AskThen
Is it one value, belonging to exactly this one resource?Metafield
Does it have several fields that belong together?Metaobject
Will more than one resource point at the same instance?Metaobject
Would a merchant want to manage a list of these?Metaobject

Any yes after the first means metaobject. And the two combine: the product still carries a metafield, but its type is a reference to a metaobject rather than a raw value. That is what a metaobject_reference type is for, and lists of them allow up to 1,024 items.

Found on a real store The live store this Course sampled used exactly this pattern. Product metafields included a measurement field typed as a metaobject reference, and around 55 of its definitions were Shopify's own standard taxonomy fields — almost all typed as list.metaobject_reference. So Shopify itself models its product taxonomy this way. Following the pattern is not exotic; it is the platform's own design.

Definition first, then entries

A metaobject definition is a schema: a type identifier, its fields with types and validation, access permissions, capabilities, and how entries display. One definition, many entries — one "size chart" definition holds every individual chart.

Ownership decides how you create it — and this trips people

The trap is in that middle row. TOML configuration only ever creates app-owned definitions. If you want something the merchant manages in the admin and other apps can read, it must be created through GraphQL. Declaring it in TOML gets you a working definition that is the wrong kind, and the wrongness only shows up when the merchant cannot edit it or another app cannot see it.

Answer before reading on Your integration needs to store, per product, the three suppliers who can provide it — each with a name, a lead time and a minimum order quantity. Metafield or metaobject, and why?

Metaobject, on two of the four tests at once. A supplier has several fields that belong together (name, lead time, minimum), and many products share the same supplier — so storing it per product copies the supplier's details across the catalogue and makes a lead-time change a catalogue-wide edit. Model supplier as a metaobject definition, create one entry per real supplier, and put a list.metaobject_reference on the product. And because the merchant's buying team will want to manage that supplier list in the admin, it must be merchant-owned — which means creating it via GraphQL, not TOML.

Where it hurts

Costs money — duplicating a shared thing across products A size chart or supplier copied onto 400 products. The first correction becomes a 400-record migration, and until it finishes the store shows two different answers to the same question.
Merchant-owned declared in TOML TOML only creates app-owned definitions. The deploy succeeds and you get the wrong kind — discovered when the merchant cannot edit the entries, or another app cannot read them. Use GraphQL for anything merchant-owned.
Fails with no error — a reference to a deleted entry A metaobject reference points at an entry someone deleted in the admin. The reference resolves to nothing and the storefront renders a gap rather than an error. Anything reading a reference must handle the empty case.
A metaobject for something that is really one value The overcorrection. "Fabric" as a metaobject means an extra lookup on every read and an extra screen for the merchant, for no benefit. Apply the five-second rule honestly in both directions.
Storefront access not granted A definition's access settings decide whether the storefront can read it. A metaobject that works perfectly in the admin and renders nothing on the storefront is almost always this. Set storefront access when you create the definition.
Judgement, not documentation — reference lists cap at 1,024 Generous, and finite. A design that points a product at "every review" or "every related item" will meet it eventually. Where a list could grow without bound, model the relationship from the other side instead.

Say it out loud

Explain this to a beginner

When should a piece of information stop being a field on the product, and become a thing of its own?

Say it out loud. Give the rule and one example. Then read on.

Now compare with a plain-words answer

Here is the test: the moment two products need the same piece of information, it stopped being a product field.

Say every product has a "care instructions" note. Fine as a field. Now say forty products all share the same washing instructions. Write it as a field and you have forty copies of the same sentence. Change the wording and you edit forty products, and you will miss some.

So you make the washing instruction a thing, once, and each product points at it. Fix a typo in one place and all forty change.

The short version: a field is an adjective — it describes this one product. A thing of its own is a noun — it exists whether or not any product points at it. If you can say "we have five of them" about the information itself, it is a noun.

If you used any of these words, try again metaobject · normalisation · denormalise · entity · foreign key · single source of truth. "Forty copies of the same sentence, and you will miss some" is the explanation. Everything in that list is a name for it.

Build it

Task · 40 minutes Dev store

Build a shared "size guide" that several products point at, and then break it in the one way everybody breaks it.

  1. Create the definition first, then two entries. Notice the order is forced — you cannot make an entry for a thing that has no shape.
  2. Add a metafield on the product whose type is a reference to that thing. Point three products at the same entry.
  3. Edit the entry once. Confirm all three products show the change.
  4. Now the classic bug: do not set storefront access. Look at the product in the admin — the data is there. Look at it on the storefront — blank.
  5. Turn storefront access on and look again.

Proof it worked: you produced "works in admin, blank on the storefront" on purpose and then fixed it. That symptom is almost always this one cause, and knowing it saves an afternoon of theme debugging.

The ownership trap · 15 minutes no store needed

Write down which of your definitions are app-owned and which are merchant-owned, and next to each, how it gets created.

Proof it worked: your note says that TOML creates only app-owned definitions, and merchant-owned ones must go through GraphQL. Code that tries to create a merchant-owned definition from TOML fails in a way that reads like a permissions problem.

Remember this

  • A metafield is an adjective; a metaobject is a noun.
  • Several fields together, or shared by many resources → metaobject. One value on one thing → metafield.
  • They combine: the product carries a metafield whose type is a reference to a metaobject.
  • Definition first, then entries. One definition, many entries.
  • TOML only creates app-owned definitions. Merchant-owned must be created via GraphQL.
  • Set storefront access at creation. Works-in-admin-but-blank-on-storefront is almost always this.
  • Reference lists cap at 1,024. Unbounded relationships should be modelled from the other side.

If you remember one thing: the moment two products need the same piece of data, it is no longer a product field.
