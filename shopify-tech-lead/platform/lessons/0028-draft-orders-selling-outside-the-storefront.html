Draft orders: selling outside the storefront

The answer to "can we take an order over the phone?" — and to most wholesale requests.

Your win today. You know the mechanism behind phone sales, quotes, wholesale invoicing and B2B purchase orders — and it is one object, not four features.

How it works

Derive it: what if there is no customer at a checkout?

Everything so far assumed a customer choosing things and paying. But real commerce has cases where that is not what happens:

The situationWhat is different
A phone orderStaff build the cart, not the customer
A quoteNobody has agreed to buy yet
WholesaleCustom prices, and payment later
A B2B purchase orderAn invoice, terms, and no card at all

All four need the same thing: an order that exists before it is paid, that staff can edit, and that can be sent to someone. That is a draft order. One object, four business cases.

draftOrderCreate staff build it. line items, custom prices, a customer, shipping, discounts │ ├── invoice ────▶ the customer receives a link and pays │ └── complete ───▶ marked paid without a payment (already paid by transfer, cash, etc.) │ ▼ a real Order

Two exits, and the difference matters. Invoice means the customer pays through a Shopify checkout link. Complete means you assert it is paid — no money moves through Shopify at all.

What a draft order can do that a cart cannot

CapabilityWhy it matters
Arbitrary line pricesStaff can override a price. This is how negotiated pricing works without touching the catalogue
Custom line itemsCharge for something that is not a product — a fee, a service, a bespoke item
Exists unpaid, indefinitelyA quote can sit for weeks
Editable by staffUnlike a customer's cart, which you cannot reach
The consequence of arbitrary prices A draft order can charge any amount for anything. So an integration that creates draft orders can sell a £500 item for £5 with no validation anywhere — it is a documented capability, not a bug. If your code sets prices, the price validation is entirely yours to write.

Inventory: the question to ask before building anything

A draft order is not a sale, so the obvious question is whether it holds stock. This is the single most important thing to establish for your client's use case, because it decides whether quotes can oversell.

Do not assume either way. Test it on a Dev store: create a draft order for an item with one unit left, then try to buy that unit on the storefront. Whatever happens is your answer, and it is worth ten minutes because a wholesale customer discovering their quote is unfulfillable is an expensive conversation.

Where this connects

Draft orders are how B2B invoicing works — see P9.1. A company, a company contact and a company location each have draft orders, and the documented B2B flow is literally "manage draft orders for B2B clients by calculating and sending invoices".

Verified 2026-08-23: draft orders existing on Company, CompanyContact and CompanyLocation, and the documented B2B flow of managing draft orders by calculating and sending invoices — DraftOrderSortKeys and Start building for B2B on Shopify. Not verified today: whether a draft order reserves inventory, and the exact behaviour of draftOrderComplete regarding stock. Test both on a Dev store before designing around them.

Answer before reading on A client wants their sales team to build quotes in your app, and the quotes should be valid for 30 days. Name the two questions you must answer before writing any code.

1 · Does a draft order hold stock? If it does, thirty days of open quotes could tie up your client's entire catalogue, and their storefront will show items as unavailable that are physically on the shelf. If it does not, a quote can be accepted for something that has since sold — so someone must handle that at acceptance. Either answer is workable; not knowing which is not.

2 · Who is allowed to override a price, and by how much? A draft order can charge any amount, and there is no platform validation. Thirty-day quotes plus unrestricted price editing is a sales team quietly discounting without a ceiling. That is a business control the client must set, and offering it unprompted is the tech-lead move.

Where it hurts

Costs money — no price validation on draft orders Arbitrary line prices are a feature. Your code is the only guard. A bug or a careless staff member can sell at any price, and it looks like a legitimate order afterwards.
Costs money — complete when you meant invoice Completing marks an order paid with no money moving. Confuse the two and the merchant ships goods nobody paid for, and their reports show revenue that does not exist.
Fails with no error — assuming stock behaviour If draft orders reserve stock, long-lived quotes starve your storefront. If they do not, quotes can be accepted for sold-out items. Both are silent. Test it.
Draft orders arriving in the ERP as sales A draft is not a sale. Anything consuming order data must distinguish them, or your client's revenue reporting counts quotes as income.
Judgement, not documentation — quotes need an expiry someone enforces Shopify will keep a draft order forever. Nothing expires it, nothing warns anyone, and a two-year-old quote at last year's prices is still completable. If the client wants 30-day validity, you are the thing that enforces it.

What you say to a client/PM

When asked for phone or wholesale ordering That's a draft order — your team builds the order, then either emails the customer a link to pay, or marks it paid if they've already transferred the money. Same mechanism handles quotes and wholesale invoices. Two things I need from you: who's allowed to change a price, and by how much. Names the mechanism once for all four use cases, then asks the control question before it becomes a problem.
On quote expiry Shopify won't expire a quote — a draft order sits there until someone acts on it. So if you want 30-day validity, I build that, and I'd also want a rule for what happens when an expired quote is accepted anyway. Do we block it, or re-price it? Names a platform gap, offers to fill it, and asks for the business rule rather than choosing one.

Remember this

  • One object, four cases: phone orders, quotes, wholesale, B2B purchase orders.
  • Two exits: invoice (customer pays via a link) or complete (marked paid, no money moves).
  • Arbitrary line prices and custom items are features. Price validation is entirely yours.
  • Test whether a draft order holds stock on a Dev store. Both answers have consequences.
  • Nothing expires a draft order. If quotes have validity, you enforce it.
  • A draft is not a sale. Downstream systems must distinguish them or revenue reporting is wrong.
  • This is the mechanism behind B2B invoicing — see P9.1.

If you remember one thing: a draft order can charge any amount for anything, so if your code sets prices, your code is the only thing standing between a sales team and an unlimited discount.
