Track 1 · P6 · Lesson 4

The cutover and the proof

The night it goes live. What you check, in what order, and what you refuse to do at 1am.

Your win today. A cutover plan with a written stop condition — so the decision to abort is made in advance by a calm person rather than at 2am by a tired one.

How it works

Derive it: what makes a cutover different from a deploy?

A deploy is reversible. You ship, it is wrong, you roll back, and the world is as it was.

A cutover is not, and the reason is that reality moves during it. Customers place orders. Staff edit prices. Stock changes. So you cannot simply undo — the state you would roll back to no longer exists. Any data written during the window has to be reconciled forward, not reverted.

DEPLOY CUTOVER code changes code AND live data change roll back = restore roll back = reconcile forward the world waited the world did not wait

Everything below follows from that one asymmetry. It is why you freeze what you can, why the order of checks matters, and why the stop condition must be written before you start.

The order, and why it is this order

#StepWhy here
1Freeze edits on the source and announce itReduces the moving target. This is the cheapest risk reduction available
2Final delta load — everything changed since the last full loadSmall and fast, because the bulk already happened days ago
3Reconcile: counts, then valuesCounts catch gross failure fast. Values catch the expensive kind
4Spot-check money by hand — ten products, real pricesThe only check that catches a wrong-but-plausible price
5Enable the ongoing sync, watch one cycle end to endProve the steady state before anyone depends on it
6Unfreeze, then watch reconciliation for a full dayDrift shows up in the first 24 hours or not at all

Step 4 is the one people skip because it feels unsophisticated. It is the only step that catches a price that is wrong but believable — the failure that automated comparison passes when both systems agree on the wrong number.

Do the big load days early

The bulk of the data should already be in Shopify, loaded and reconciled, days before the cutover. Then the cutover window contains only a small delta. A plan whose window includes a full catalogue load is a plan with no room for anything to go wrong, and something will.

Write the stop condition down, in advance

The single most valuable paragraph in this Lesson Before the cutover starts, write down — and agree with the client — the conditions under which you stop and roll back. For example: "If reconciliation shows more than 1% of variants with a price difference, or any difference in a top-50 seller, we stop and revert to the old system."

Write it while calm. At 2am, tired, with a client on the phone, nobody makes this judgement well — and the pressure runs entirely one way, toward continuing.

What you refuse to do at 1am

  • No code changes. Fix forward tomorrow or roll back tonight. A cutover is not the time to write anything.
  • No "we'll fix that in the morning" for anything touching money. Prices and stock are either right or you stop.
  • No skipping the reconciliation because it is late and the counts looked fine. Counts are the check that passes when everything is subtly wrong.
  • No unfreezing before step 5 is proven. Unfreezing is the irreversible moment, not the load.

The proof you hand over

A cutover ends with a document, not with a feeling. Four numbers, each with its denominator:

products expected / loaded / failed variants expected / loaded / failed prices compared / matching / differing stock compared / matching / differing plus: the ten hand-checked products, listed by name plus: the stop condition, and confirmation it was not met

That document is what lets everyone agree the next morning that it worked — and it is what protects you if something surfaces in week two.

Answer before reading on It is 1:40am. Everything reconciles except 12 products whose prices differ by small amounts. The client is on the call and wants to go live. What do you do?

You read the stop condition you wrote down, and you follow it. That is the whole point of having written it — so this decision is not being made now.

If the condition says "any difference in a top-50 seller stops us", check whether those 12 are top sellers; if they are, you stop, and you have a pre-agreed reason rather than an opinion. If they are long-tail products and below your threshold, you go live and put the 12 on a list with a named owner and a deadline.

What you do not do is investigate the root cause at 1:40am. Twelve small price differences is tomorrow's work. And notice that "small amounts" is exactly what a wrong-but-plausible failure looks like — which is why the hand-check in step 4 matters more than it feels like it should.

Where it hurts

Costs money — a full load inside the cutover window No slack for anything to go wrong, and something will. Load days early, reconcile early, and keep the window for a small delta.
Costs money — no stop condition The decision to continue gets made by whoever is most tired and most under pressure, and the pressure is always toward going live. Written in advance, it is a policy. Decided at 2am, it is a hope.
Costs money — code changes during a cutover Untested code, shipped by a tired person, onto a live store, at the moment of maximum change. Roll back or accept the issue; do not write.
Fails with no error — count-only reconciliation Every price wrong by a cent, or every stock level a week old, passes a count check perfectly. Compare values, and hand-check ten products.
Fails with no error — unfreezing too early Once edits resume on both sides, you are in the two-copies world from Track 2 and can no longer simply reload. Unfreeze is the irreversible step, not the import.
No named owner for the leftover list "We'll sort those twelve out" with nobody's name on it means they are still wrong in a month. Every item that survives the cutover gets an owner and a date, in the handover document.
Judgement, not documentation — pick the quietest hour, not the middle of the night Everyone assumes 2am. But the real requirement is low order volume and available people, and those two rarely peak together at 2am. For many retailers an early weekday morning is quieter and fully staffed. Look at the client's actual order-by-hour data rather than following convention.

Say it out loud

Explain this to a beginner

Explain why you cannot "just roll back" a go-live the way you roll back a deploy.

Say it out loud. Then read on.

Now compare with a plain-words answer

Because while you were switching over, the world kept moving.

A normal deploy touches code. If it goes wrong you put the old code back and nothing has been lost, because nothing happened in between.

A go-live touches a live shop. During it, real customers place real orders and real stock moves. Put the old system back and those orders now exist in one place and not the other. There is nothing to revert to, because the thing you would revert to is already out of date. So "rolling back" actually means reconciling forward — working out what happened during the window and fixing both sides.

Which changes how you plan it. You do the enormous slow part days earlier, so the risky window only has to carry a small difference. You check counts, then values, and then you look at ten real things by hand, because a count can never catch a price that is wrong but believable.

And the most valuable thing you write is the sentence that says when to stop. Agree it while everyone is calm and unhurried, because at two in the morning the pressure only ever pushes one way — carry on, it is probably fine.

Last thing: the irreversible step is not the import. It is letting customers back in.

If you used any of these words, try again rollback · idempotent replay · delta load · freeze window · reconciliation · abort criteria. "While you were switching over, the world kept moving" explains all of it.

Build it

Task · 60 minutes no store needed

Write the run sheet before you ever need it. Then the hard bit: write the stop condition.

  1. Write the steps in order: freeze · load the difference · reconcile counts · reconcile values · hand-check money · turn the sync on · unfreeze and watch.
  2. Put a wall-clock time and an owner next to every step.
  3. Write the stop condition as one paragraph, in numbers. "If more than N products mismatch, or we are past HH:MM, we abort and unfreeze the old system."
  4. Write the list of things you refuse to do during the window. No code changes is the first one.
  5. Get the stop condition agreed in writing by whoever owns the shop.

Proof it worked: someone other than you has agreed the abort criteria before the day. That one paragraph is worth more than the rest of the preparation, because it is the only part that helps when you are tired and being pushed.

Rehearse it · half a day Dev store

Do the whole run sheet on a Dev store, including a deliberate mismatch that should trigger your stop condition.

Proof it worked: the rehearsal aborted when it was supposed to. A run sheet that has never triggered its own abort has not been tested.

Remember this

  • A cutover is not a deploy. Reality moves during it, so rollback means reconciling forward.
  • Do the big load days early. The window should hold only a small delta.
  • Order: freeze · delta load · reconcile counts then values · hand-check money · enable sync · unfreeze and watch a day.
  • Hand-check ten products. The only check that catches wrong-but-plausible prices.
  • Write the stop condition down and agree it in advance. At 2am the pressure runs one way.
  • No code changes during a cutover. Roll back or accept; do not write.
  • Unfreeze is the irreversible step, not the import. Prove the steady state first.

If you remember one thing: agree the abort criteria while everyone is calm — that one paragraph is worth more than every other precaution combined.
