Customer segments, and the query that defines them

A segment is a saved question, not a saved list — and that one distinction explains every surprise.

Your win today. You know why segment membership changes without anyone touching it, and why a wrong segment is silent by construction.

How it works

Derive it: a saved list, or a saved question?

You want to email everyone who spent over a hundred pounds last year. There are two ways to hold that.

You could save the list of people. Simple, and immediately wrong — somebody buys something tomorrow and the list is stale, with nothing to tell you.

Or you could save the question, and answer it fresh every time somebody asks. That is what a segment is: a group of customers that meet specific criteria, where the criteria are stored and the membership is derived.

So the mental model is a saved search, not a saved list. Which explains every surprising behaviour: membership changes on its own, two runs a day apart give different answers, and nothing in your code changed in between. None of that is a bug.

SAVED LIST [alice, bob, carol] fixed, and stale by tomorrow SAVED QUESTION "spent > 100 last year" answered fresh, every time Shopify stores the QUESTION. So membership is a result, never a fact.

What the object actually holds

A Segment is deliberately thin. Four fields carry everything:

FieldWhat it is
nameWhat a human calls it
queryThe definition — conditions on facts about customers, written in ShopifyQL
creationDateWhen it was added to the store
lastEditDateWhen the definition last changed

Notice there is no member list on the object, and no count. That absence is the design: the definition is the thing, and the members are computed.

The query field holds ShopifyQL conditions — purchase history, location, engagement patterns and similar facts about customers. So a segment is a small program somebody wrote, stored as text, and it can be wrong in exactly the way a small program can be wrong.

Reading segments requires the read_customers scope, and the user must not have restricted access. That second condition is worth noting: it is a per-user restriction, not just an app scope, so your app can hold the right scope and still see nothing for a particular staff member.

Why this belongs in a conversion chapter

Segments drive things that touch money — marketing sends, and automated discount eligibility. So a wrong segment does not throw an error. It sends the wrong offer to the wrong people, or makes a discount available to customers who should not have it.

Which puts it firmly in this Chapter's pattern, alongside search filters in P10.1: the failure is invisible in logs and visible in revenue. Nothing is broken. The wrong people simply received something.

Verified 2026-08-23: that a Segment is a group of customers meeting criteria defined through ShopifyQL query conditions; that its fields are name, query, creationDate and lastEditDate, where query is "a precise definition… composed of a combination of conditions on facts about customers"; that common use cases are customer analytics, targeted marketing campaigns and automated discount eligibility; and that it requires read_customers and that the user must not have restricted access — Segment.

Answer before reading on A client says "the VIP discount went to 400 people last month and 900 this month, and nobody changed anything". Give two explanations, and say which field would tell them apart.

One: nobody changed the segment and the customers changed — more people now meet the condition, because a segment is a question answered fresh. Two: somebody did edit the definition, and lastEditDate would show it. So that single field separates "the world moved" from "someone changed the rule", and it is the first thing to read before investigating anything else.

Where it hurts

Costs money — treating a segment as a fixed list Membership is recomputed. A discount aimed at 400 people can reach 900 next month with nothing changed in your code, and the first sign is the cost.
Fails with no error — a wrong condition The query is a small program stored as text. Get a condition subtly wrong and the segment returns confidently wrong people. Nothing validates intent, and the offer still goes out.
Fails with no error — caching membership Cache the members and you have converted the saved question back into a stale list, reintroducing exactly the problem the design avoids. And the staleness has no event to react to.
Costs money — automated discount eligibility on an unreviewed segment A segment can gate a discount automatically. A definition nobody checked is then a spending decision nobody reviewed.
Fails with no error — the restricted-access condition Reading segments needs read_customers and the user must not have restricted access. Your app can hold the scope and still see nothing for a given staff member, which looks like an empty store rather than a permission.
Not reading lastEditDate first It separates "the customers changed" from "somebody changed the rule". Investigating without it means guessing between two completely different explanations.
Assuming segments are level 0 data Segments are conditions on facts about customers. Touching them puts you in the privacy territory of A1.4, not in catalogue territory.
Judgement, not documentation — a segment definition needs an owner and a review It is business logic written in a text field, usually by a marketer, often once, and then it silently governs spending for years. Treat a segment that gates money the way you would treat code: someone owns it, and somebody other than its author has read it.

Say it out loud

Explain this to a beginner

A "VIP customers" group had 400 people last month and has 900 today. Nobody edited anything. Explain why that is correct behaviour.

Say it out loud. Then read on.

Now compare with a plain-words answer

Because the group is not a list of names. It is a question, and the answer is worked out fresh every time somebody asks.

Somebody wrote down a rule once — something like "people who have spent more than a hundred pounds". That rule is what got saved. Nobody saved the people.

So between last month and today, more customers crossed that line by simply buying things. The rule did not change. The answer did.

Compare it to a folder versus a search. If you drag files into a folder, the folder holds those files until you move them. If you save a search for "files with 'invoice' in the name", the results change as you create files, and you did not touch the search.

This is the saved search. Which is good — it can never go stale. And it means anything you attach to it, like a discount, is attached to a moving target. If a hundred more people qualify tomorrow, a hundred more people get the discount, and nobody decided that today.

If you used any of these words, try again dynamic segment · ShopifyQL · predicate · materialised view · membership evaluation. "A folder versus a saved search" is the explanation. If you said "it is a dynamically evaluated predicate", you named it and taught nothing.

Build it

Task · 45 minutes Dev store

Watch a segment change underneath you.

  1. Create a segment with a spend condition. Record the member count and today's date.
  2. Place enough test orders to push one more customer over the line.
  3. Read the count again. It moved, and lastEditDate did not.
  4. Now edit the definition and read lastEditDate again.

Proof it worked: you have two events — membership changing with no edit, and an edit — and you can tell them apart from one field. That is the diagnostic you will actually use.

Then gate money with it, carefully · 45 minutes Dev store
  1. Attach a discount to the segment.
  2. Add a customer who newly qualifies. Confirm the discount reaches them with no action from you.
  3. Write down the worst case: how many people could qualify, and what that costs.
  4. Then check the read_customers plus restricted-access condition by reading segments as a restricted user.

Proof it worked: you have a costed worst case for an automatic discount, and you have seen the empty result a restricted user gets — which looks like no data rather than no permission.

Review one like code · 20 minutes a real store

Take a real segment that gates money. Read its query out loud as a sentence, and get somebody other than its author to confirm that sentence is what they meant.

Proof it worked: either it was right, or you found a condition nobody had re-read since it was written. This is business logic in a text field, and it deserves the same review as code.

Remember this

  • A segment is a saved question, not a saved list. Membership is computed fresh every time.
  • So membership changes with no edit — and lastEditDate is how you tell that from someone changing the rule.
  • The query field is the segment. ShopifyQL conditions on facts about customers.
  • Never cache the members. That turns the saved question back into a stale list.
  • Segments can gate discounts automatically, so an unreviewed definition is unreviewed spending.
  • Needs read_customers and an unrestricted user. The wrong user sees nothing, not an error.
  • It is customer data — the privacy rules in A1.4 apply, not the catalogue ones.

If you remember one thing: a segment is a question answered fresh, so anything you attach to it is attached to a moving target — read lastEditDate before you investigate anything.

Ask questions. Then "next" for marketing surfaces — and one more deprecation worth knowing before you build.
