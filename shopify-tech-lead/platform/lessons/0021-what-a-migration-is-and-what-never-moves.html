What a migration is, and what never moves

Passwords never move. Neither do several things clients assume will.

Your win today. You can tell a client, in the first meeting, which of their data will not survive the move — before they promise their customers otherwise.

How it works

Derive it: three categories, not two

People plan migrations as "what we move" and implicitly assume everything else is small. The honest model has three categories, and the third is the one that causes trouble:

CategoryExamplesEffort
Moves cleanlyProducts, variants, prices, collections, customers (without credentials), inventory levelsMechanical. This is the work everyone plans for
Moves with lossOrders, discounts, content pages, redirects, gift cardsMoves, but not identically. The differences need decisions
Never movesPasswords. Platform-specific features. Third-party app data. SEO authority tied to old URLsNot a task. A change to how the business works

Passwords: the one nobody expects

Customer password hashes do not transfer between platforms. They are hashed with the old platform's scheme and secret, so there is nothing meaningful to import — and importing them would be a security failure if it were possible.

So every existing customer must set a new password, or sign in without one. This is not a technical inconvenience. It is a communications project: every customer needs to be told, before they hit a login form that no longer recognises them, or your support queue becomes the migration's most visible cost.

Modern Shopify customer accounts can sign a customer in with a one-time code sent to their email, which removes the password problem entirely for many stores. That is worth raising early, because it changes the shape of the communications plan.

Order history: moves with loss, and the loss is specific

Historical orders can be created via the API — but a created order is not a replayed order. Things that will not be identical: the payment transaction as the gateway recorded it, the exact tax calculation from that day, discount applications from the old platform's engine, and anything the old system computed at the time.

So decide what historical orders are for, because the answer changes the work:

PurposeWhat you need
Customers seeing their own past ordersLine items, dates, totals. Approximate is fine
Support answering "what did I buy?"Same. Approximate is fine
Accounting, tax or auditDo not migrate for this. Keep the old system readable, or export to a warehouse
Returns and exchanges on old ordersA decision: a cut-off date, or a manual process

That third row is the most useful thing in this Lesson. Trying to make migrated orders accounting-accurate is an enormous amount of work for a purpose a read-only archive serves better. Say so early.

The categories clients forget entirely

  • Redirects. Old URLs will be linked from Google, emails and other sites forever. Without redirects you lose that traffic silently. This is usually the highest-value, lowest-glamour task in the whole project.
  • Third-party app data. Reviews, loyalty points, subscriptions, wishlists. Each lives in an app, each has its own export, and some have none. Enumerate the installed apps in week one.
  • Content and SEO metadata. Pages, blogs, meta titles and descriptions. Mechanical but voluminous, and always underestimated.
  • Anything platform-specific. A feature the old platform had that Shopify does not is not a migration item — it is a scope decision, and it belongs in front of the client immediately.

Answer before reading on A client says "and obviously all our customers' logins carry over." What do you say, and what do you ask for?

They do not — passwords cannot move between platforms, so every customer will need to set a new one or sign in with an emailed code. Then immediately ask for two things: who owns the customer communication, and when it goes out relative to go-live. Because the failure here is not technical, it is that thousands of people hit a login form that does not recognise them on the same morning, and nobody warned them. Raising it in the first meeting turns it into a planned email campaign. Raising it in week eight turns it into a support crisis.

Where it hurts

Costs money — no redirects Every old URL indexed by search engines or linked from an email becomes a 404. Traffic drops and nothing errors. It is invisible in your logs and extremely visible in the client's revenue.
Costs money — discovering app data late Reviews and loyalty points are the client's most emotionally valuable data, and they live in apps with their own exports — or none. Found in week one it is a task. Found in week eight it is a crisis.
Costs money — migrating orders for accounting A recreated order is not the original transaction. Building toward audit accuracy is enormous effort for a purpose a read-only archive serves better and cheaper.
Fails with no error — the login surprise Passwords cannot transfer, so on go-live morning every returning customer fails to sign in. Nothing is broken. Everything is working as designed, and the support queue does not care.
A platform-specific feature treated as a migration task If Shopify does not do it, it is not a data problem — it is a scope decision. Escalate it rather than quietly building a substitute nobody agreed to.
Judgement, not documentation — customers will notice small differences Order numbers that do not match old receipts, dates shifted by a timezone, an address formatted differently. Individually trivial, collectively they read as "they lost our data". Decide deliberately what will visibly differ, and tell the client which ones customers will see.

What you say to a client/PM

First meeting, unprompted Three things I want to flag now rather than later. Customer passwords can't move between platforms — everyone will need to set a new one, or sign in with a code we email them, so that needs a communication plan. Second, anything living in your current apps — reviews, loyalty points — is a separate export each, and I need the app list this week. And third, we need redirects from every old URL, or you lose your search traffic on day one. Three items, all of which are cheap now and expensive later. Delivering them unprompted is the difference between a supplier and a lead.
When asked to migrate ten years of orders Let me ask what they're for. If it's so customers can see what they bought, we can bring across the items, dates and totals and that works well. If it's for accounting or tax, I'd keep the old system readable instead — a recreated order isn't the original transaction, and making it accurate enough for an audit is a project on its own. Which one matters? One question that can remove the largest and least valuable chunk of a migration from the scope entirely.

Remember this

  • Three categories: moves cleanly · moves with loss · never moves.
  • Passwords never move. That makes it a communications project, not a data task.
  • One-time email codes remove the password problem for many stores. Raise it early.
  • A recreated order is not the original transaction. Never migrate orders for accounting.
  • Redirects are the highest-value, least glamorous task. Without them, traffic dies silently.
  • Enumerate installed apps in week one. Reviews and loyalty are the client's most treasured data.
  • A missing platform feature is a scope decision, not a migration item. Escalate it.

If you remember one thing: ask what the historical data is for — the answer usually removes most of the work.
