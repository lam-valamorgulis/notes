Track 1 · P10 · Lesson 3 · depth

Content, files and translations

Low pain per item, high pain in aggregate. Always underestimated in a migration.

Your win today. You can size a content migration honestly, and you know why translations multiply everything else you have estimated.

How it works

Derive it: why does content behave differently?

Products are structured — you can count them, diff them, and reconcile them. Content is prose, files and layout, and none of those has a clean equality test. A page that "moved successfully" can still be visibly wrong, and no automated check will say so.

So content is the one area where a human has to look, and that is what makes it expensive in aggregate while being trivial per item.

what "content" actually contains pages about us, shipping policy, size guides blogs articles, authors, comments navigation menus, which break when handles change files images and documents not attached to products metaobjects structured content — see P2.2 translations ALL of the above, times every language redirects the highest-value, least glamorous item of all

Files are not product images

Product media is attached to a product. Files are the loose assets — a PDF size chart, a lookbook image used in a page, a logo in an email template. They live in their own place, and a migration that only moves product images leaves every page referencing missing files.

Two practical notes. Files can be created by having Shopify fetch them from a URL, which makes a migration a list of URLs rather than a huge upload. And a file reference is a legitimate metafield type, so structured content can point at files properly rather than storing a URL string that later rots.

Translations multiply, they do not add

A translation is a separate stored value per locale, not a runtime transformation. So every content item you have estimated is multiplied, and any content the integration creates arrives untranslated by default. That is the part that catches integrations: your sync writes an English title, and three markets now show English where they used to show their own language.

This connects to Markets in P5.1: a market configures content translations, so "which language does this customer see" is a market question, and a title is per-market content rather than one string.

Redirects: the one to do first

Repeating this from P6.5 because it belongs here too. Old URLs are linked from search engines, emails and other people's sites forever. Without redirects that traffic becomes 404s, silently — nothing in your logs, everything in the client's revenue.

It is mechanical, unglamorous, and usually the highest-value hour in a content migration. Bulk redirect import exists for exactly this.

How to size it honestly

StepDo
1Count pages, blog articles, files, menus, metaobject entries. Actual numbers, from the source
2Multiply by locales. Then say the multiplied number out loud to the client
3Sample five pages and migrate them by hand. Time it. That is your per-item cost
4Decide who checks. Content needs a human eye, and it is usually not you
5Separate the redirect list as its own task with its own priority

Step 4 is the one to get agreed in writing. You can move content; only the client can say whether it reads correctly. A migration where nobody owns the reading is a migration that ships with broken pages.

Answer before reading on A client's migration brief says "and move our 40 content pages across". They sell in four languages. What do you say the number is?

160, and say it in the meeting. Forty pages times four locales is a hundred and sixty stored values, each of which someone has to confirm reads correctly in a language you probably do not speak.

Then say the two things that follow. First: who checks the non-English versions — because you cannot, and if nobody is named it ships unchecked. Second: the redirects for those forty pages are a separate task, and if they are skipped the pages will be migrated perfectly and unreachable from Google.

Saying "160, and here are the two questions" in the first meeting is worth more than any code you write on this part of the project.

Where it hurts

Costs money — estimating content without multiplying by locales Four languages makes every content number four times bigger, including the human checking. This is the single most common content underestimate.
Costs money — no redirects Old URLs become 404s. Search traffic dies silently and the cause is invisible in your logs.
Fails with no error — files left behind Moving product images but not loose files leaves pages referencing assets that do not exist. The page loads; the size chart does not.
Fails with no error — an integration writing untranslated content Your sync writes an English title and markets that previously showed a translation now show English. Nothing errors, and only a customer in that market notices.
Broken navigation after handles change Menus point at handles. Change a page or collection handle in a migration and the menu silently links to nothing.
Storing a file URL in a text metafield Use file_reference. A URL string does not survive the file being replaced, and nothing tells you it has rotted.
Judgement, not documentation — nobody owns "does it read correctly" This is the failure mode of content work. You can prove a page moved; only a person can say it reads properly, and in a language you may not speak. Get a named owner per locale in writing, or accept that it ships unchecked.

Say it out loud

Explain this to a beginner

Why is moving 500 pages to a new shop four times the work if the shop is in four languages — and what makes content harder to check than products?

Say it out loud. Then read on.

Now compare with a plain-words answer

Because a translation is not something the system works out on the fly. Each language is a separately stored piece of writing. Four languages means four stored versions of every page — and, crucially, four times the human reading, because someone has to check each one.

That last part is what makes content different from products. You can prove a product moved: the price matches, the code matches, done. You cannot prove a paragraph "matches". It can arrive complete, in the right place, with every character present, and still read badly — wrong tone, broken sentence, a link that now points nowhere. The only test is a person reading it, and possibly in a language you do not speak.

So the real cost is not the copying. It is the reading. And that is the number people leave out of estimates, every single time.

Two silent traps. An integration writing content writes it in one language — so your nightly sync can overwrite a good translation with English, and only a customer in that market ever sees it. And navigation menus point at page addresses, so changing an address in a migration breaks the menu quietly, with nothing in any log.

If you used any of these words, try again localisation · i18n · locale-scoped resource · content parity · canonical URL. "Four languages means four times the human reading, and reading is the cost" is the explanation.

Build it

Task · 45 minutes no store needed

Produce the number people leave out.

  1. Count every content item: pages, blog posts, collection descriptions, loose files, menu entries.
  2. Multiply by the number of locales. Write the multiplied number down.
  3. Add a line for hours of human reading, and name who does it per language.
  4. Say the multiplied number out loud before it enters an estimate.

Proof it worked: the estimate contains a named person per language and an hours figure for reading. If it does not, you have estimated the copying and not the job.

The two silent breaks · 45 minutes Dev store
  1. Add a second locale and translate one product title.
  2. Run a sync that writes that title in English. Look at the translated market.
  3. Change a page's handle and check every menu that pointed at it.
  4. Store a file's URL in a text field, then replace the file. Then redo it with file_reference.

Proof it worked: you overwrote a good translation with English and nothing errored; the menu broke silently; the URL string rotted while the reference survived. Now add the guard: your sync must not write a localised field unless it knows the locale.

Remember this

  • Content has no clean equality test. A human must read it, and that is the real cost.
  • Translations multiply everything. A separate stored value per locale, not a runtime transformation.
  • Integration-created content arrives untranslated and can replace good translations with English.
  • Files are not product media. Loose assets have their own home and get left behind.
  • Use file_reference, not a URL string. URLs rot silently.
  • Menus point at handles. Change a handle in a migration and navigation breaks quietly.
  • Redirects first. Highest value, least glamour, invisible when missing.

If you remember one thing: multiply every content count by the number of locales, and say the multiplied number out loud before anyone writes it into a plan.
