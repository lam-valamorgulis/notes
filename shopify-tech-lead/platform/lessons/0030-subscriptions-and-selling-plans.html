Track 1 · P9 · Lesson 2

Subscriptions and selling plans

Uninstall the app and every selling plan is deleted 48 hours later.

Your win today. You know the four policies that define a subscription, the one inventory choice that decides whether you oversell, and the deletion rule that makes backups non-optional.

How it works

Derive it: what is a subscription, minimally?

Not "a recurring order". It is an alternative way to buy the same product. The product does not change; the terms do. So Shopify models it as a plan attached to variants, not as a different product:

SellingPlanGroup a selling METHOD "Subscribe and save" · "Prepaid" │ name ← what the customer sees │ merchantCode ← what the merchant sees │ options ← the storefront drop-down │ ├── products / variants what it applies to │ └── SellingPlan one way to buy. "Deliver weekly" ├── billingPolicy how often you CHARGE ├── deliveryPolicy how often you SHIP ├── inventoryPolicy WHEN stock is committed └── pricingPolicy fixed, or recurring

Billing and delivery are separate on purpose. A prepaid plan bills once for twelve weeks and delivers weekly — two different frequencies for one plan.

The inventory policy is the one that costs money

One choice, two very different behaviours Inventory can be committed when the order is created, or when the order is fulfilled.

Commit at creation and a twelve-month subscription reserves stock it will not ship for a year — your storefront shows sold out while the shelf is full. Commit at fulfilment and you can take subscriptions you cannot supply, which surfaces as a failed delivery to a paying customer months later.

Neither is right. It is a business decision, and it must be a deliberate one.

Pricing policies come in two shapes

TypeMeans
FixedOne policy for the whole subscription. "A flat 15% off, always"
RecurringA follow-up policy after the initial one expires. "20% off the first cycle, 15% thereafter"

And a detail that matters for anyone who has read P9.1: selling plan pricing policies work in conjunction with price lists. So a B2B customer on a subscription has a price shaped by both. That is a third override layer on top of market and price list.

Buying one takes two IDs

To purchase with a purchase option you submit a variant ID and a selling plan ID through the Cart API. That is the whole storefront mechanic — a subscription line item is an ordinary line with a plan attached.

Checkout then handles pricing, messaging, payment method vaulting and customer consent. You do not build recurring billing; the platform stores the payment method and the subscription contract records the agreement between merchant and customer.

The deletion rule

This is the fact to remember from this Lesson Subscriptions, pre-orders and try-before-you-buy records — including SellingPlanGroup, SellingPlan, the policies, and their associations to products and variants — are deleted 48 hours after a user uninstalls the app.

Products and variants survive. The plans do not. Shopify's own recommendation is to back these records up in case you need to restore them.

So: a merchant uninstalls to try a competitor, changes their mind on day three, and their entire subscription configuration is gone. If your app does not hold its own copy, it cannot be restored.

Two more constraints worth knowing before you scope

ConstraintDetail
One interval unit per planWEEK or MONTH, not both in the same plan. "Every 6 weeks or every 2 months" is two plans
Read-only without an extensionIf your app provides no app extension to edit selling plans, they appear read-only on the product page in the admin. The merchant cannot change what you created

For advanced delivery behaviour — "fulfilment starts on the 1st of each month" — the mechanism is anchors in the API call, and category should be SUBSCRIPTION.

If it is a public app, the bar is explicit

Built for Shopify requires subscription apps to use the Selling plan API, the Subscription contract API and the Customer payment method API; to add subscriptions on the product page with an Online Store 2.0 app block; to display plan name, price and savings on the product, cart and order detail pages; to match the theme's colours and fonts by default; and to use Customer Account UI extensions plus the Customer Account API for customers to manage their own subscriptions.

Answer before reading on A client sells coffee on a monthly subscription. They have 400 subscribers and 200 bags in stock. Which inventory policy do you choose, and what do you tell them either way?

Commit at fulfilment, almost certainly — and then say the consequence out loud.

Committing at creation would reserve stock for every future cycle of every subscription, so 400 subscribers would immediately consume far more than 200 bags and the storefront would show sold out permanently. That is unusable for a repeat-consumable business.

But committing at fulfilment means the platform will happily let them take a 401st subscriber they cannot supply this month. So the honest sentence is: "I'll commit stock when we ship, not when they sign up — otherwise your website shows sold out forever. That means Shopify won't stop you over-subscribing, so you need a view of subscribers against incoming stock. That's a report, and it's worth having before you grow."

Notice the shape: you made the technical choice, named what it does not protect against, and proposed the thing that does. That is the whole pattern from Track 3.

Where it hurts

Costs money — uninstall deletes the plans in 48 hours All selling plans, policies and associations gone. Products survive. If your app holds no backup, a merchant's change of mind is unrecoverable.
Costs money — committing inventory at creation for a consumable Future cycles reserve stock now. The storefront shows sold out while the warehouse is full.
Costs money — committing at fulfilment with no subscriber report The platform will let the merchant over-subscribe. The failure lands months later as a missed delivery to a paying customer.
Fails with no error — three stacking price overrides Market override, B2B price list, and a subscription pricing policy can all apply. "What does this cost?" has no single answer, and reconciling only the variant price confirms nothing.
Fails with no error — a subscription order treated as one-off Downstream systems seeing only the first order will report a customer's lifetime value as one cycle, and will not expect the next eleven.
Mixing interval units in one plan WEEK or MONTH, not both. "Every 6 weeks or every 2 months" is two selling plans, and discovering that mid-build changes the storefront options.
Judgement, not documentation — a contract outlives your code A twelve-month subscription taken today will still be billing after your next two refactors and several API version bumps. Anything you encode into a plan is a commitment you cannot easily change for existing subscribers. Design the plan set conservatively, and assume you will be supporting today's plans for years.

Say it out loud

Explain this to a beginner

A twelve-month coffee subscription. One setting decides whether the shop shows "sold out" when the warehouse is full, or oversells and fails a customer in month seven. Explain the setting.

Say it out loud. Then read on.

Now compare with a plain-words answer

The setting is when the stock gets set aside: at the moment the customer signs up, or at each delivery.

Set it aside at sign-up, and one customer signing up for a year immediately reserves twelve months of coffee. Do that for a hundred customers and the shop shows sold out while the warehouse is full. You have reserved stock for deliveries that are a year away.

Set it aside at each delivery, and nothing reserves anything early — so the platform will happily let the shop sign up more subscribers than it can ever supply. That failure does not appear now. It appears in month seven, as a missed delivery to somebody who has been paying every month.

So neither option is safe by itself. The second one is usually right, but only if somebody is watching a report of how many subscribers there are against what can actually be supplied. That report is the thing people forget to build.

And one more, which is the sharpest fact here: if the app is uninstalled, every subscription plan is deleted 48 hours later. The products survive; the plans do not. So a merchant changing their mind for two days is unrecoverable unless your app kept its own copy. Keeping that backup is not optional.

If you used any of these words, try again selling plan group · inventory policy · billing policy · deferred purchase · subscription contract. "When does the stock get set aside — at sign-up or at each delivery?" is the explanation.

Build it

Task · 90 minutes Dev store
  1. Create a selling plan group with all four policies: billing, delivery, inventory, pricing. Notice billing and delivery are separate frequencies — pay monthly, ship quarterly.
  2. Set inventory to commit at creation. Set stock to 12. Subscribe for 12 cycles. Look at the storefront.
  3. Switch to commit at fulfilment. Subscribe 20 customers against 12 units of stock.
  4. Try to build one plan with "every 6 weeks or every 2 months". Watch it refuse — one interval unit per plan.

Proof it worked: you produced both failures — a sold-out storefront with a full warehouse, and an oversubscribed shop with no warning. Then build the subscriber-versus-supply report, because that is the only thing that catches the second one.

The 48-hour one · 30 minutes Dev store
  1. Write code that exports every selling plan, policy and association your app created.
  2. Run it. Store the output outside Shopify.
  3. Write the restore path too, and test it.

Proof it worked: you can rebuild every plan from your own copy. Also add a note about price: a subscription pricing policy is a third override layer, after market overrides and B2B price lists — so "what does this cost?" has no single answer, and reconciling against the variant price proves nothing.

Remember this

  • A subscription is an alternative way to buy the same variant, not a different product.
  • Four policies: billing · delivery · inventory · pricing. Billing and delivery are separate frequencies.
  • Inventory committed at creation or at fulfilment. That one choice decides whether you show sold out or oversell.
  • Uninstall deletes all plans after 48 hours. Products survive. Keep your own backup.
  • Buying takes a variant ID and a selling plan ID through the Cart API.
  • One interval unit per plan. Weeks or months, never both.
  • Pricing policies stack with price lists — a third override layer after markets and B2B.

If you remember one thing: an uninstall destroys every selling plan 48 hours later, so your app holding its own copy is not optional.
