Orders, money, and the Shopify → Shopify replatform

The omitted line price is the money bug that a count-only check will never catch.

Your win today. You know the specific way an imported order can silently misstate what a customer paid — and why "Shopify to Shopify" is not the easy migration it sounds like.

How it works

Derive it: what is an order, really?

A product is a description of a thing. It can be re-derived, corrected, re-imported. An order is different in kind: it is a record of a completed financial event. It happened once, at a particular moment, for a particular amount.

That means the ordinary migration instinct — send the data, let the platform fill in what it knows — is exactly wrong for orders. Anything the platform "helpfully" computes is a value it is inventing about the past.

The money bug If you create an order and omit a line item's price, the platform can fall back to the variant's current price. So an order from three years ago, when the item cost $20, imports at today's $35.

Nothing errors. The order exists. The counts match. The line items match. Every automated check passes — and the customer's history now says they paid $35 for something they bought for $20. Multiply across a decade of orders and the store's entire revenue history is wrong, in one direction, invisibly.

Always send every price explicitly on an imported order. Never let a default fill in a historical amount.

This is the concrete reason the reconciliation Chapter insists on comparing values and not counts. A count-only check cannot see this at all, and it is the most expensive silent failure in migration work.

The four things to be explicit about

FieldIf you omit it
Line item priceMay be filled from the variant's current price. Always send it
The order's dateImports as today, so the whole history collapses onto the migration date
Fulfilment stateDefaults to unfulfilled, so a decade of shipped orders looks like a backlog
Tax and discount amountsRecalculated by today's rules rather than the rules that applied then

The pattern is one thing: for an order, every default is a lie about the past. Be explicit about everything, and treat a field you cannot source as a decision to escalate rather than a blank to leave.

And the notification problem

Creating orders can trigger customer-facing emails. Importing ten years of history could email customers about purchases they made in 2016. Before any bulk order import, confirm what notifications will fire and switch them off. Test on a Dev store with a real email address and watch what arrives — do not reason about it.

Shopify to Shopify: easier, and not easy

Moving between two Shopify stores sounds trivial — same platform, same shapes, same API. Some of that is true. What is not:

AssumptionReality
Ids carry overNo. Every product, variant, customer and order gets a new id. Every reference must be re-mapped
Apps come with itEach app must be reinstalled and reconfigured, and its data separately exported and imported
Metafields come with itValues move, but the definitions must exist first — and definitions are per store
Themes are portableFiles are. Their settings and content are separate, and app blocks reference apps that are not installed yet
Nothing is lostOrder history, saved payment methods and gift cards each have their own rules and limits

The genuine advantages: the shapes match so no field mapping is needed, and you can read the source with the same API you write the destination with — including bulk operations on both sides. So the transformation is easy. The identity re-mapping is the whole job, and it is the same mapping-registry problem from A4.4.

What I did not verify today The exact mechanics: the field name for backdating an order, the scope and token type orderCreate requires, per-minute order creation caps on development and trial stores, how many notification emails fire per imported order, and which gateways permit saved payment methods to move. These were verified for an earlier version of this material on 3 August 2026 and not re-checked today. Confirm each before planning against it — the variant throttle in P6.3 moved 10× in three weeks, so none of these should be inherited on trust.
Answer before reading on You import 50,000 historical orders. Counts match exactly, every order has the right number of line items, every customer is linked. The client signs it off. What have you not checked?

Whether the money is right. Counts, line-item counts and customer links can all be perfect while every price is today's price rather than the price paid. And this failure is directional — prices generally rise, so migrated history will overstate historical revenue, which makes every report built on it wrong in a way that looks plausible.

The check is to pick ten old orders, look up what the customer actually paid in the old system, and compare. Ten manual comparisons catch a failure that 50,000 automated row counts cannot. That is the same hand-check as step 4 of the cutover, and this is why it is there.

Where it hurts

Costs money — the omitted line price The order imports with today's price. Customer history, revenue reports and any analysis built on them are all wrong, in one direction, with every automated check passing.
Costs money — emails to customers about 2016 orders A bulk order import firing notifications. Thousands of confused customers and a support crisis, from a setting nobody checked. Test on a Dev store with a real inbox first.
Fails with no error — orders dated today Omit the date and a decade of history stacks onto the migration day. Every cohort analysis, repeat-rate and seasonality report the client owns becomes meaningless, and the data looks complete.
Fails with no error — everything unfulfilled The default state makes ten years of shipped orders look like an open backlog. Operations teams and fulfilment integrations both react to that.
Costs money — assuming Shopify-to-Shopify is trivial Every id changes, so every reference must be re-mapped and every app reinstalled and re-exported. Quoted as "same platform, should be quick", it overruns badly.
Metafield values before definitions Definitions are per store. Import values into a store with no definitions and you recreate the inert-metafield problem from P2.1 at full scale. Definitions first, always.
Judgement, not documentation — order numbers will not match Customers hold old receipts and support search by old numbers. Decide deliberately: store the original number in a metafield and make support able to search it, or accept the mismatch knowingly. Do not discover it from a support ticket.

What you say to a client/PM

Before importing any orders One thing I want to be careful about. When we recreate old orders, if we don't send the exact price paid, the system can fill in today's price instead — so an order from 2019 would show what the item costs now. Nothing would look broken; the numbers would just be wrong. So I'll send every price explicitly and then check ten old orders by hand against your current system before we sign it off. Explains a silent failure in the client's own terms and commits to the specific check that catches it. This is the sentence that makes a client trust the whole migration.
When asked to quote a Shopify-to-Shopify move as "simple" It's simpler in one way — the data shapes match, so there's no translation. But every product, customer and order gets a brand new ID in the new store, so everything that references anything has to be re-mapped. And every app has to be reinstalled and its data exported separately. That's the actual work, and it's the part that looks invisible from outside. Concedes the genuine advantage first, which makes the rest credible rather than defensive.

Remember this

  • An order is a record of a financial event. Every default is a lie about the past.
  • Always send line item prices explicitly. Omitted, they can fill in today's price.
  • Be explicit about date, fulfilment state, tax and discounts too.
  • Check what emails will fire before a bulk order import. Test on a Dev store with a real inbox.
  • Shopify to Shopify: every id changes. Identity re-mapping is the whole job.
  • Metafield definitions must exist before values. Definitions are per store.
  • Hand-check ten old orders against the source. No row count can catch a wrong price.

If you remember one thing: on an imported order, never omit a price — the platform will fill in today's, and every automated check will pass.
