Liquid or headless: the client conversation

Not a technical preference. A hiring, cost and speed decision you have to defend.

Your win today. When a client asks "should we go headless?", you can answer in ninety seconds with the three questions that actually decide it — and say no without sounding old-fashioned.

How it works

Derive it: what do you give up by leaving the theme?

A Liquid theme is rendered by Shopify. Going headless means you render the storefront and call Shopify for data. The interesting question is not "which is more modern" — it is what does Shopify stop doing for you?

Shopify does this for you in a themeHeadless
Hosting, CDN, scaling for a sale spikeYou own it
The theme editor — merchants change content without youYou build an editing story, or they file tickets
Apps that inject storefront UI via theme app extensionsMostly stop working
Checkout, integratedStill Shopify's, but the handoff is yours to get right
Search, filtering, recommendations, out of the boxYou wire them up

That third row is the one clients never anticipate. Much of the Shopify app ecosystem works by adding UI into a Liquid theme. Go headless and a reviews widget, an upsell block or a store locator often needs replacing with custom work — repeatedly, forever.

Feynman version: headless buys you control of the front end and bills you for everything Shopify was quietly doing.

The three questions that decide it

QuestionIf yes
1. Is the storefront experience genuinely unusual — a configurator, a heavy interactive tool, content that is not a product catalogue?Headless earns its place
2. Does the client employ, or will they retain, front-end developers permanently?Headless is sustainable
3. Do the merchant's own team need to change pages without a developer?Strong argument for a theme

Question 2 is the one that quietly kills most headless projects. A custom storefront is not a build, it is a dependency: someone must maintain it every quarter forever. If the honest answer is "we'd use an agency once and then stop", a theme is the responsible recommendation regardless of how good the headless build would be.

Say the middle option out loud

The choice is usually presented as binary and rarely is. A Liquid theme can carry a genuinely custom, app-driven section for the one page that needs it, while the other ninety-nine pages stay in the theme editor. That gets the client the unusual experience where it matters and keeps the maintenance surface small.

all theme ─────────── one custom section ─────────── all headless ↑ ↑ ↑ cheapest usually the right answer genuinely merchant-editable custom where it matters unusual only apps work theme editor everywhere else
Answer before reading on A client says "our competitor's site is faster, we should go headless." What do you say?

That speed is not the reason to go headless, because it is not caused by Liquid. A slow Shopify storefront is almost always too many apps injecting scripts, oversized images, and render-blocking third parties — all of which are fixable in the theme, cheaply, this month. A headless rebuild that carries the same third-party scripts will be slower, not faster, because you have added a hosting layer you now have to tune. Offer to measure the current site first. If the fix is in the theme, you have saved them a rebuild; if it genuinely is not, you now have evidence for the conversation.

Where it hurts

Costs money — headless with no permanent front-end team The build lands, the agency leaves, and nothing changes for two years because every content edit needs a developer. The merchant ends up worse off than with a theme they could edit.
Costs money — losing the app ecosystem Apps that inject storefront UI stop working. Each one becomes a custom build and a permanent maintenance item. Enumerate the client's installed apps before recommending headless, and price the replacements.
Fails with no error — headless "for speed" Speed problems are usually third-party scripts and images, which follow you into any stack. The rebuild ships, the score does not move, and nobody can explain why.
Underestimating the editing story "The merchant can edit content" is a feature you must build, and it competes with a theme editor that Shopify improves for free. Budget it explicitly or it becomes support tickets.
Judgement, not documentation — the recommendation is usually a theme Most Shopify stores sell a catalogue of products, which is exactly what themes are for. Recommending a theme can feel like the unambitious answer. It is usually the correct one, and being willing to say so is a mark of seniority rather than of caution.

What you say to a client/PM

When asked "should we go headless?" Three questions decide it for me. Is your storefront doing something genuinely unusual, beyond showing a catalogue? Will you have front-end developers on staff permanently, not just for the build? And does your team need to change pages without asking a developer? If it's no, no, yes — which is most businesses — a theme is the right answer, and we can still build one fully custom section for the page that needs it. Three questions, an honest default, and a middle option so "no" does not sound like a refusal.
When the client has already decided and you disagree I'll build it, and I want one thing on the record first: this needs a front-end developer available every quarter, permanently — for Shopify's API versions, for security updates, and for the apps we'll be replacing by hand. If that resource exists, headless is a good fit. If it doesn't, the site will freeze about a year from now. Which is it? Does not fight the decision. Names the one condition that makes it work and hands the risk back, in writing.

Remember this

  • Headless buys front-end control and bills you for what Shopify was doing — hosting, the theme editor, and the app ecosystem.
  • Theme app extensions mostly stop working. Enumerate installed apps and price their replacements.
  • Three questions: unusual experience · permanent front-end team · merchant self-editing.
  • The permanent team question kills most headless projects. It is a dependency, not a build.
  • Offer the middle option: a theme with one genuinely custom section.
  • "Headless for speed" is almost always wrong. Measure first — the cause is usually scripts and images.
  • A theme is usually the right recommendation. Being willing to say so is seniority.

If you remember one thing: ask who maintains the front end in two years — the answer decides this, not the technology.

Ask questions. The rest of P4 is marked depth: skip it if themes are already your strength.
