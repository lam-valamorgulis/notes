Track 1 · P9 · Lesson 1

B2B: companies, catalogs and price lists

The price stops living on the variant. That breaks every price-sync assumption in this Course.

Your win today. You can explain why "just sync our prices" has a completely different answer once B2B exists — and you know the five objects that replace "a customer with a price".

How it works

Derive it: what does a business buyer need that a consumer does not?

A consumer is one person, one address, one price, paying now. Take each of those away and you get B2B:

ConsumerBusiness buyerSo Shopify needs
One personSeveral people, with different authorityCompany contacts, with roles
One addressBranches, each with its own tax and shippingCompany locations
The listed priceA negotiated price, per customerCatalogs and price lists
Pays now, by cardPays in 30 days, on invoicePayment terms

Five objects, and each one exists because a consumer assumption failed.

Company the business entity ├── CompanyContact a person acting for it │ · linked to a CUSTOMER account │ · assigned to one or more locations, WITH ROLES │ roles decide access to catalogs, pricing, payment terms │ └── CompanyLocation a branch. the important one. · its own billing and shipping addresses · its own TAX settings · its own checkout configuration · its own Catalog objects → which products, at which prices · BuyerExperienceConfiguration → PaymentTerms → whether orders REQUIRE MERCHANT REVIEW Catalog ──▶ PriceList the prices └─▶ Publication the products that are even visible

The fact that changes your sync

Read this if you take one thing from the Lesson A B2B price does not live on the variant. It lives in a price list, attached to a catalog, attached to a company location.

So everything Track 2 says about syncing prices — ownership per field, absolute writes, reconciling shadow against Shopify — was written about one price per variant. With B2B there are N+1 prices: the retail one on the variant, plus one per price list that overrides it.

Writing the variant price does not change what a B2B customer pays. And reconciling only variant prices will report everything correct while every wholesale customer is being charged last year's rate.

This is the same shape as the Markets override trap in P5.1, and it compounds with it: a price can be overridden by market and by price list. "What does this cost?" is not a question with one answer.

Two behaviours that surprise people

BehaviourConsequence
Catalogs control visibility, not just priceA product can be invisible to one company location and visible to another. "Missing product" is often a catalog question, not a publishing one
Orders can require merchant reviewBuyerExperienceConfiguration can make a B2B order need approval. So an order can exist in a state where it is placed but not accepted — a state consumer flows never produce

That second row is the one that breaks integrations. Anything treating "order created" as "order confirmed" will push unapproved orders into a warehouse.

How a B2B sale actually happens

1. create the company and its location 2. set the location's tax exemptions and payment terms 3. attach a price list and a publication to a B2B catalog → that decides WHICH products and at WHAT price 4. the contact orders — or staff raise a DRAFT ORDER and invoice it

Step 4 is why P8.3 comes first: the documented B2B flow is "manage draft orders for B2B clients by calculating and sending invoices". Draft orders are the B2B invoicing mechanism, not a separate feature.

Answer before reading on Your ERP sync has been running happily for a year. The client turns on B2B and onboards twelve wholesale accounts. What silently breaks, and what does your reconciliation say?

Your price sync stops affecting wholesale customers entirely — and your reconciliation reports zero differences.

Here is why. You write the variant price; you record it in shadow; you compare shadow against the variant price in Shopify. All three agree. Meanwhile the twelve wholesale accounts pay whatever is in their price lists, which your sync has never touched and never checked. If the ERP raises prices, retail customers see the rise and wholesale customers do not.

The failure is invisible for exactly the reason A3.6 warns about: you are reconciling the field you write, not the field the customer pays. The fix is to make price lists part of the ownership table and part of the comparison — and the moment to do that is when B2B is switched on, not when someone notices the margin.

Where it hurts

Costs money — syncing only the variant price B2B prices live in price lists. Your sync writes a price nobody in B2B pays, and reconciliation confirms it perfectly. Wholesale margin quietly drifts.
Costs money — treating "order created" as "order confirmed" B2B orders can require merchant review. Push those to a warehouse and you ship goods on an unapproved order.
Fails with no error — a product invisible to one company location Catalogs control visibility as well as price. A B2B customer reporting a missing product is usually a catalog question, and nothing errors — the product simply is not in their catalog.
Costs money — payment terms ignored downstream A B2B order may be payable in 30 days. An ERP treating every order as paid-on-placement will report revenue and chase nothing, and the cash-flow picture is wrong.
Fails with no error — price list and market override stacking A price can be overridden by market and by price list. Code assuming one override level reads a price that is right in one context and wrong in another.
Roles ignored A company contact's role determines their access to catalogs, pricing and payment terms. An integration that treats all contacts as equivalent grants the wrong person the wrong prices.
Judgement, not documentation — B2B is a second store inside the store Different prices, different visibility, different tax, different payment, different approval flow. Estimating it as "add wholesale pricing" is the classic underestimate. Treat it as a second commercial model that happens to share a catalogue, and price it accordingly.

What you say to a client/PM

When B2B enters the conversation Worth flagging early, because it changes something we already built. Right now our sync writes one price per product. B2B prices don't live there — each business customer gets their own price list, so writing the normal price won't change what they pay. I need to add price lists to what we sync and to what we check nightly. Otherwise your wholesale margin drifts and our report says everything is fine. Names the invisible failure precisely, and asks for the work before it becomes a margin problem. This sentence is the whole value of the Lesson.
When scoping B2B I'd treat this as a second way of selling rather than a pricing feature. Business customers get their own prices, their own product visibility, their own tax settings, payment on terms rather than by card, and optionally an approval step before an order is accepted. Each of those touches something we've already built. It's a project, not a setting. Five specific differences, each checkable, which is what makes "it's a project" land rather than sound like padding.

Remember this

  • Five objects: Company · CompanyContact (with roles) · CompanyLocation · Catalog · PriceList.
  • The price is not on the variant. It is in a price list, on a catalog, on a company location.
  • So a variant has N+1 prices. Add price lists to the ownership table and to reconciliation.
  • Catalogs control visibility too. A "missing product" is often a catalog question.
  • Orders can require merchant review. "Created" is not "confirmed".
  • Payment terms mean an order may be unpaid for 30 days. Downstream systems must know.
  • Roles decide who sees which catalog and price. Contacts are not interchangeable.

If you remember one thing: with B2B you are reconciling the field you write, not the field the customer pays — and that gap reports as zero differences.
