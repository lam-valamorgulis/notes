Track 1 · P3 · Lesson 1

Three ways in-store stock reaches the storefront

Three architectures, and three different lies they tell the customer.

Your win today. A client says "show what's in our shops on the website" and you can name the three ways to do it, what each one costs, and how wrong each one can be.

How it works

Derive it: where can a number live?

A customer on a product page wants to know whether a shop has an item. Somewhere there is a number. From first principles there are only three places it can be at the moment the page renders:

1. IN SHOPIFY the shop is a Location. the number is native. 2. IN A THIRD SYSTEM asked live, per page view. 3. IN A COPY pushed to Shopify or a cache earlier. read locally.

Every product in this category is one of those three. The differences that matter are freshness and who is on the critical path of a page load.

1 · Native: make each shop a Location

The cheapest correct answer, and the one people skip. If each shop is a Shopify Location with real inventory levels, the storefront already knows. No integration, no third party, no cache.

The prerequisite is real work though: the shop's stock must actually be in Shopify, which usually means Shopify POS or a POS integration. If shop stock lives only in a separate till system, you have a different project first.

2 · Live third party: correct, and on the critical path

Ask a vendor per page view. Always current — and now a third party's latency is your page's latency. This is shape 3 from A4.1, so its rules apply: one attempt, a hard timeout, and a decided failure mode. Availability is information, so fail open — hide the panel rather than delay the page.

3 · A copy: stale on purpose, and honest about it

Push shop stock into Shopify or a cache on a schedule. Nothing is on the critical path and nothing breaks when the source is down. The cost is that the number is old, and how old is a decision you must make explicit.

The rule that makes option 3 acceptable Label the freshness on the page. "In stock at Newtown — checked 15 minutes ago" is honest and useful. "In stock at Newtown" from the same data is a promise you cannot keep. The engineering is identical; the words are the difference between a happy customer and a wasted journey.

Then the number itself can be wrong

All three inherit the problem from P1.1: a shop's shelf count is not the same as sellable stock. On a shop floor, items are held for customers, damaged, in a fitting room, or on display. Publishing the shelf number sends people to collect things that are not there.

So the honest design usually publishes a threshold, not a number: "in stock", "low stock", "not available" — with the low-stock band set high enough to absorb the everyday inaccuracy of a shop floor. A precise number invites a precise expectation you cannot support.

Answer before reading on A client wants "exact stock numbers per shop, live, on every product page". What do you push back on, and what do you offer instead?

Two things. First, "exact" — a shop floor is not accurate to the unit, so an exact number will be wrong often enough to cost trust. Offer bands instead. Second, "live on every product page" puts a third party on the critical path of your busiest pages; if their availability lookup is slow, every page is slow. Offer: bands rather than counts, loaded after the page renders rather than blocking it, and hidden entirely when the source is unreachable. You have given them the feature and removed both failure modes, and it is a shorter build.

Where it hurts

Costs money — publishing shelf stock as available Held, damaged and display items counted as sellable. Customers travel to a shop for an item that is not there. That costs the client the customer, not just the sale.
Costs money — a third party blocking the product page A slow availability lookup adds its latency to every product view. The loss is invisible: nobody reports an abandoned page. Load it after render, or move it behind a click.
Fails with no error — an unlabelled cache Twenty-minute-old data presented as current. Usually right, so nothing looks broken. When it is wrong, someone has made a journey. Label the age.
Fails with no error — a shop that is not a Location Native availability for it is simply absent, which renders as "not available anywhere" rather than as an error. Audit the Location list against the real shop list before designing anything.
Exact counts on a shop floor Shops miscount. An exact figure sets an expectation the physical world does not meet. Bands absorb the error and still answer the customer's real question, which is "is it worth going?"
Judgement, not documentation — the last unit "One left" is the most dangerous number to display, because it is the most likely to be wrong and the most likely to make someone travel. Many retailers deliberately show nothing below a threshold. Raise it with the client rather than deciding silently in code.

What you say to a client/PM

First conversation There are three ways to do this and they cost very differently. If your shop stock is already in Shopify, it's nearly free — the website already knows. If it lives in a separate till system, we either ask that system every time someone loads a page, which makes your pages depend on its speed, or we copy the numbers over every few minutes and say how fresh they are. Where does your shop stock live today? Three options with their real costs, then one question that decides it. The client leaves knowing why the answer differs.
When pushing back on exact numbers I'd show "in stock" or "low stock" rather than a count. Shop floors are never accurate to the single item — things get held, damaged, or sit in a fitting room. If we say "2 left" and someone drives over for it, being wrong costs you that customer. Bands answer the question they actually have, which is whether it's worth the trip. Grounds a technical recommendation in a physical reality the client knows better than you do. Very hard to argue with.

Remember this

  • Three architectures: native Locations · live third-party lookup · a scheduled copy.
  • Native is cheapest and needs each shop to be a Location with real inventory levels.
  • A live lookup puts a third party on your page's critical path. Fail open, and load it after render.
  • A copy never breaks, it only goes stale. Label the age on the page.
  • Shelf stock ≠ sellable stock. Held, damaged and display items are on the shelf and not for sale.
  • Publish bands, not counts. A precise number invites a precise expectation you cannot support.
  • Audit Locations against real shops first. A shop that is not a Location is invisible, not erroring.

If you remember one thing: the question a customer is really asking is "is it worth the trip?" — and a band answers that better than a number you cannot guarantee.
