Dawn, Horizon and theme blocks

What is genuinely new. Not Liquid from zero — you already have that.

Your win today. You can explain what changed in modern themes in terms of who gets to compose a page, which is the part that matters to a merchant.

How it works

Derive it: who is allowed to compose a page?

Skip the feature list and ask the structural question. In an old theme, a developer decided what went on a page and in what order; a merchant could change text and images. Every layout change was a ticket.

Modern themes moved that boundary. A section is a composable band of a page a merchant can add, remove and reorder. A block is a composable piece inside a section. The direction of travel is consistent: push composition from the developer to the merchant, one level deeper at a time.

old developer composes the page merchant edits text sections merchant composes the PAGE from sections developer builds the sections theme blocks merchant composes INSIDE sections too developer builds smaller, more general pieces

That reframes your job. The better a modern theme is, the fewer and more general your components are — because the merchant is doing the arranging. A developer who ships one rigid bespoke section per page has technically used the system and gained none of its value.

What this means for your estimates

RequestOld answerModern answer
"Move that band above the reviews"A developer taskThe merchant does it
"Add this promo to five pages"Five templatesOne section, added five times by the merchant
"A new kind of content band"A developer taskStill a developer task — but reusable everywhere

So the honest conversation about a modern theme is: a higher share of future requests become self-service. That is the value, and it is worth saying out loud because it is the return on building things generally rather than specifically.

The practical guidance

  • Build sections that take settings, not sections that hardcode content. A hardcoded section is a template with extra steps.
  • Prefer smaller blocks over one large section. Composability is the point.
  • Read the theme you are working in before adding to it. Modern reference themes differ from each other in real ways — in how they structure CSS, how much JavaScript they ship, and how components are organised.
  • Do not assume a feature exists because a modern theme is modern. Grep the theme. A reference theme can simply not include a feature you expected, and its absence is a design choice rather than an oversight.
What I did not verify today Specific file counts, per-theme architecture details, and exactly which reference theme ships which feature. Those are version-specific and drift with every theme release, so quoting them here would be a fact with a short shelf life. Check the theme in front of you — the version you are editing is the only authority on what it contains. That habit is worth more than any number this Lesson could give you.
Answer before reading on A client asks for "a testimonials band on the home page, the collection page, and two landing pages". How many things do you build?

One — a section with settings for its content, which the merchant then adds in four places. The tempting answer is four, because each page's design differs slightly, and that is exactly the instinct modern themes exist to break. Build one general piece with enough settings to cover the variation, and the fifth request costs the client nothing and you nothing. Four bespoke bands means four things to maintain and a fifth ticket next month.

Where it hurts

Costs money — bespoke sections per page Each one is a permanent maintenance item and none is reusable. The client keeps paying for variations of something they already own, and every theme update multiplies the work.
Costs money — hardcoded content in a section Text or images baked into Liquid instead of exposed as settings means every wording change is a deploy. The merchant loses self-service and you gain a queue of trivial tickets.
Fails with no error — assuming a feature is present A reference theme may simply not ship something you expected. Building on the assumption produces code that silently does nothing on that theme. Grep before you assume.
Section settings with no sensible defaults A merchant adds your section and sees an empty band. They conclude it is broken. Give every setting a default that renders something reasonable.
Judgement, not documentation — composability has a limit Exposing every possible setting produces a section a merchant cannot understand and can easily break. There is a real trade-off between flexibility and usability, and the right answer depends on the team who will use it. Ask who edits the store before deciding how many knobs to expose.

Say it out loud

Explain this to a beginner

Why is one flexible page section with options better than four separate sections that each do one thing?

Say it out loud. Then read on.

Now compare with a plain-words answer

Because of who has to do the work next time.

If you build four fixed sections, the shop owner can only ever have those four arrangements. The fifth request comes in and it needs you — a developer, a quote, a wait. And it always comes in.

If you build one section with settings, the owner rearranges it themselves. They pick the heading, the image side, how many columns. The fifth request costs nothing because they just do it.

That is really the whole direction of modern themes: each version hands a bit more of the composing over from the developer to the shop owner. So the better the theme, the fewer and more general your pieces should be.

There is a limit though. Every option you expose is another thing to understand. Twenty settings is not flexible, it is confusing. So you ask who actually edits this shop before you decide how many knobs to give them — and you give every setting a sensible starting value, or they will drop your section on the page, see nothing, and think it is broken.

If you used any of these words, try again composability · schema-driven · atomic components · configuration surface · abstraction level. "The fifth request costs nothing because they just do it themselves" is the explanation.

Build it

Task · 60 minutes Dev store + theme

Take four bespoke sections and collapse them into one.

  1. Find four sections in a theme that are near-duplicates of each other.
  2. Write down what actually differs between them. It is usually two or three things.
  3. Build one section whose settings cover all four cases.
  4. Give every setting a default that looks correct with no configuration at all.
  5. Reproduce all four original layouts using only the editor.

Proof it worked: you reproduce all four arrangements without touching code, and a fifth arrangement you did not plan for also works. Then drop the section on a blank page with no settings touched — it must look deliberate, not empty.

The habit that prevents a wrong estimate · 15 minutes the actual theme

Before estimating any theme work, grep the theme in front of you for the feature you assume exists.

Proof it worked: you found at least one thing you assumed was there and is not, or is there under a different name. "It's a modern theme so it must support X" is how theme estimates go wrong.

Remember this

  • Sections and blocks move composition from the developer to the merchant, one level at a time.
  • The better the theme, the fewer and more general your components.
  • Settings, not hardcoded content. A hardcoded section is a template with extra steps.
  • One general section beats four bespoke ones. The fifth request should be free.
  • Read and grep the theme in front of you. Do not assume a feature exists because the theme is modern.
  • Give every setting a sensible default, or a merchant will think your section is broken.
  • Flexibility trades against usability. Ask who edits the store before exposing every knob.

If you remember one thing: build one thing with options, not four things with none.
