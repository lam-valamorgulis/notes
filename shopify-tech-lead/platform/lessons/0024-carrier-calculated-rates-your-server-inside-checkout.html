Track 1 · P7 · Lesson 2

Carrier-calculated rates: your server inside checkout

The timeout shrinks as your traffic grows. There is no retry. And Shopify documents its own fail-open.

Your win today. You can build a rate endpoint that degrades correctly under load — and you know why the busiest store you serve gets the shortest timeout.

How it works

What this is

A carrier service is a callback URL you register. When a customer reaches checkout, Shopify calls you and asks for shipping rates. Whatever you return is what they see.

customer at checkout │ ▼ Shopify ──POST──▶ your callback URL { rate: { origin, destination, items[], currency } } │ ◀──JSON──── { rates: [ { service_name, service_code, currency, total_price, description } ] } │ ▼ the rates the customer sees

This is shape 3 from A4.1 in its purest form: synchronous, inside a customer's checkout, no retries. Everything this Course says about queues and reconciliation does not apply. Different rules, and they are harsher.

The response, and the five required fields

FieldReqNotes
service_nameYesWhat the customer sees. "Expedited Mail"
descriptionYesAlso customer-facing. "Includes tracking and insurance"
service_codeYesMust be stable across requests. See the trap below
currencyYes
total_priceYesIn subunits. 500 = 5.00 CAD. For currencies without subunits, ×100 — 100000 = 1000 JPY
phone_requiredNoForces a phone number at checkout
min_delivery_date / max_delivery_dateNoShown as an estimate
metafieldsNoWhere dynamic data belongs — not in the service code
The service_code trap It must be stable and consistent for the same shipping option, and must not contain dynamic values — no session IDs, timestamps or request-specific identifiers. Put those in metafields instead. Bake a timestamp into the code and every request looks like a brand-new shipping option to Shopify.

The timeout inverts as you grow

This is the fact that changes your design, and almost nobody knows it. The read timeout is dynamic, based on requests per minute, per shop-and-app pair:

Requests per minuteYour timeout
Under 1,50010 s
1,500 – 3,0005 s
Over 3,0003 s

Read that again. The busier the store, the less time you get. So an endpoint that comfortably answers in 6 seconds works perfectly on a quiet store and fails on the client's biggest sale day — the one day it matters. Design for 3 seconds, always, and treat the 10 as headroom you never use.

Shopify says these are upper limits, "not a goal to develop towards", and that they may be adjusted outside normal versioning timelines. So this is a number that can change without an API version bump.

The four ways to answer — including two deliberate failures

You want to sayReturn
Here are the rates20x with the rates array
I cannot handle this shipmentAn empty array with any 20x
Use the store's backup rates instead40x or 50x with any content. 404 is a good choice
(accidentally) use backup ratesTime out, error, or redirect to a different domain

Those middle two rows are the important ones, and they are genuinely different. An empty array means "no rates from me" — the customer may then see nothing at all. A 4xx means "fall back to the store's configured rates" — the customer sees something.

Apply the rule from A4.1: shipping at checkout is a promise, not information. So when your service is unhealthy, fail to backup rates — return a 404 — rather than returning an empty array and leaving the customer unable to check out. Shopify built you an explicit fail-open, and using it is the whole point.

Redirects, precisely A 30x is followed only within the same domain as the original callback URL. Redirect to a different domain and it triggers backup rates instead. So a load balancer that redirects to another hostname silently turns your service off.

There is no retry mechanism

Stated plainly in the documentation: the response must succeed on the first try. So retries, backoff and jitter — the whole toolkit from A2.3 — are unavailable here. If you call a carrier's API from inside this handler, their latency is inside your budget, and their bad day is your bad day.

The design that follows: never call a carrier live from this endpoint. Pre-compute or cache rates on your side, and serve them from memory. Your handler should be a lookup, not an integration.

Shopify caches for you — and the cache key is documented

A request that matches all of these is served from Shopify's cache instead of hitting you:

variant IDs default shipping box weight and dimensions variant quantities carrier service ID origin address destination address item weights and signatures cache expires: 15 MINUTES after a successful response 30 SECONDS after an error

Two consequences. First, your real traffic is much lower than checkout traffic — identical carts inside 15 minutes cost you nothing. Second, notice the asymmetry: an error is cached for only 30 seconds, so a failing endpoint gets hammered roughly 30× harder than a working one. A brief outage becomes a load spike exactly when you are least able to serve it.

And the cache key tells you what to vary on: if your rate depends on something not in that list — say a customer tag or the time of day — Shopify will serve a stale cached rate and never ask you.

Answer before reading on Your rate endpoint calls a courier's API, which takes 2.5 seconds at p99. You test on the client's quiet staging store and it is fine. What happens on Black Friday?

Three things compound, in this order.

1 · Your timeout shrinks. Traffic pushes the store over 3,000 requests per minute, so your budget drops from 10 seconds to 3. Your 2.5-second p99 now has 0.5 seconds of headroom for everything else.
2 · The courier slows down too, because everyone's Black Friday traffic is hitting them as well. You cross 3 seconds and start timing out.
3 · Timeouts cache for 30 seconds, not 15 minutes. So instead of your load dropping, it rises roughly thirty-fold — Shopify keeps asking because it has no good answer to cache. You are now being hammered at your weakest moment.

The fix is not a bigger server. It is never calling the courier from inside the handler. Pre-compute rates, serve them from memory in single-digit milliseconds, and refresh them out of band. Then step 1 is irrelevant and steps 2 and 3 cannot happen.

Where it hurts

Costs money — calling a carrier live inside the handler Their latency is inside your budget and there is no retry. The failure lands on the highest-intent page in the store, on the busiest day. Pre-compute and serve from memory.
Costs money — designing for the 10-second timeout It shrinks to 3 seconds over 3,000 requests per minute. An endpoint tuned to the generous number fails precisely when the store is busiest. Design for 3.
Costs money — an empty array when you meant "I'm broken" An empty array is "no rates from me", and the customer may see no shipping option at all and abandon. To fall back to the store's own rates you must return a 4xx or 5xx. Two different meanings, one of which loses the sale.
Fails with no error — the 30-second error cache Success caches for 15 minutes; an error caches for 30. So a failing endpoint receives roughly 30× the load of a healthy one. Your outage amplifies itself and your dashboards show a traffic spike you did not cause.
Fails with no error — a rate that depends on something outside the cache key Customer tags, time of day, live stock — none is in the cache key. Shopify serves the cached rate and never calls you, so your logic silently does not run.
Fails with no error — a dynamic service_code A timestamp or session id in the code makes every response a new shipping option. Put dynamic data in metafields.
A cross-domain redirect 30x is followed only within the same domain. A load balancer redirecting to another hostname silently triggers backup rates — your service appears to be off.
Judgement, not documentation — decide your own inner deadline Shopify's number can change without a version bump, so do not build to it. Pick your own budget — 500 ms is a reasonable target for a lookup — and return a 404 if you are going to exceed it. Failing fast to backup rates beats answering late, because answering late is the same as not answering.

Say it out loud

Explain this to a beginner

Your server gets to decide the shipping price live, at checkout. Explain why your time limit gets shorter as the shop gets more successful — and why that is the worst possible direction.

Say it out loud. Then read on.

Now compare with a plain-words answer

Because Shopify is protecting the checkout, not you. The busier the shop, the more customers are waiting at once, so the less time any one of them can be made to wait. So your allowance shrinks exactly when the shop is at its busiest.

Think about when that bites. Not on a quiet Tuesday when you tested it. On the biggest sales day of the year, when the most money is moving, your allowance is at its smallest. So you do not design for the generous limit — you design for the smallest one, always.

Two more things make this unforgiving. There is no retry: if you are late, that is the answer, and a customer is standing there. And "I have no prices for you" and "I am broken" are different answers — one shows the customer no shipping at all, and the other tells Shopify to fall back to the shop's own prices. Confuse them and a working checkout turns into a lost sale.

And the cruel detail: a failing answer is remembered for a much shorter time than a good one. So a broken endpoint gets asked far more often than a healthy one — your outage makes itself worse.

Which gives one rule: never phone a courier while a customer is waiting. Work the prices out in advance and make this endpoint a lookup that answers instantly.

If you used any of these words, try again synchronous callback · RPM tier · p99 latency · cache TTL · fail open · thundering herd. "Your allowance shrinks exactly when the shop is busiest, and there is no second chance" is the explanation.

Build it

Task · 2 hours Dev store + a public URL

Build the endpoint, then prove all four answers behave the way you think.

  1. Serve a rate from a pre-computed lookup. No live courier call. Measure your response time and confirm it is milliseconds, not seconds.
  2. Return all five required fields, with total_price in subunits — 500 means 5.00. Get this wrong and you undercharge by 100×.
  3. Answer with an empty array. Look at checkout: no shipping option at all.
  4. Answer with a 4xx. Look again: the shop's own backup rates appear.
  5. Now add a deliberate 4-second delay and watch it time out with no retry.

Proof it worked: you can state which of empty-array and 4xx loses the sale, from having seen both. Your handler's failure path returns 4xx, not an empty array. And your budget is 3 seconds, because that is the allowance above 3,000 requests per minute.

The cache key trap · 30 minutes Dev store

Make a rate that depends on something outside the cache key — a customer tag, or the time of day — and watch Shopify serve a cached answer without calling you at all.

Proof it worked: your endpoint was never called and the wrong price was shown. Now write down what is in the key: variant ids, box weight and dimensions, quantities, carrier service id, origin, destination, item weights and signatures. Anything else cannot vary your answer.

Remember this

  • Shopify calls you at checkout. Synchronous, customer waiting, no retry mechanism.
  • The timeout shrinks as traffic grows: 10 s under 1,500 RPM · 5 s to 3,000 · 3 s above. Design for 3.
  • total_price is in subunits. 500 = 5.00.
  • service_code must be stable. Dynamic data goes in metafields.
  • Empty array = "no rates from me". 4xx/5xx = "use backup rates". Fail to backup, not to empty.
  • Success caches 15 minutes; an error caches 30 seconds — so a broken endpoint gets ~30× the load.
  • Never call a carrier live from the handler. Pre-compute; serve a lookup.

If you remember one thing: your timeout gets shorter as the store gets busier — so build a lookup that answers in milliseconds, and fail to backup rates rather than late.
