Track 1 · P8 · Lesson 6 · depth

Payouts and finance reconciliation

P8.2 covered taking the money. This is where it lands — and the question comes from an accountant, not a developer.

Your win today. You can explain why an order total never equals a bank deposit, and you know which objects answer a finance team's questions.

How it works

Derive it: why does the bank figure never match the orders figure?

Take a day with exactly a thousand pounds of orders. The bank receives something else. Every time. Not because of a bug — because a deposit and a day of sales are not the same kind of thing.

Four reasons, and they compound:

ReasonEffect
FeesThe processor keeps a percentage. Gross is not net.
TimingMoney is paid out on a schedule, so a deposit spans part of one day and part of another.
Refunds and adjustmentsThey reduce a payout, and they relate to orders from any earlier period.
Non-gateway moneyGift cards and store credit (P8.5) recognise revenue with no deposit at all.

So "orders today" and "money today" are two different questions and always will be. The useful skill is not making them match — it is explaining the difference precisely, which is exactly what an accountant is asking for when they say the numbers are wrong.

The objects that answer it

ShopifyPaymentsAccount |- balances, per supported currency |- ShopifyPaymentsPayoutSchedule when money moves |- ShopifyPaymentsBalanceTransaction every charge, refund, adjustment |- ShopifyPaymentsBankAccount |- ShopifyPaymentsPayout one transfer |- net amount, issue date, status |- ShopifyPaymentsPayoutSummary |- fees and gross, BY TRANSACTION TYPE

Two of those deserve attention.

ShopifyPaymentsBalanceTransaction is the individual level — each charge, refund and adjustment that moved the balance. This is the layer that lets you explain a payout rather than merely report it.

ShopifyPaymentsPayoutSummary breaks a payout down into fees and gross amounts by transaction type — charges, refunds, adjustments. That summary is the bridge between "orders" and "bank", and it is the thing finance actually wants.

Note the direction too: ShopifyPaymentsPayoutTransactionType distinguishes a deposit (into the bank) from a withdrawal (back to Shopify Payments). Payouts can go backwards. Code that assumes money only flows one way will produce a negative it cannot explain.

The permission, which is not a normal scope conversation

Reading a payout requires the user to have access to payouts. This is a more sensitive surface than product data, and the client's finance people may reasonably refuse. That is a conversation to have before you promise a finance dashboard, not after.

Verified 2026-08-23: that ShopifyPaymentsPayout requires the user to have access to payouts and is a transfer between the Shopify Payments balance and a ShopifyPaymentsBankAccount, carrying net amount, issue date and ShopifyPaymentsPayoutStatus; that ShopifyPaymentsPayoutSummary breaks down fees and gross amounts by transaction type such as charges, refunds and adjustments; that ShopifyPaymentsPayoutTransactionType distinguishes deposit from withdrawal; and that ShopifyPaymentsAccount tracks balances across supported currencies, payout schedules and ShopifyPaymentsBalanceTransaction records — ShopifyPaymentsPayout and PayoutSortKeys.

Answer before reading on An accountant says "we invoiced fifty-two thousand last month and only forty-nine arrived. Where is the rest?" Name four places it went, using the right object names.

One: processor fees, visible in the ShopifyPaymentsPayoutSummary as the gap between gross and net. Two: timing — the last day or two of the month is in a payout issued next month, so the money exists and has not arrived yet. Three: refunds and adjustments, which appear in the summary by transaction type and may relate to orders from earlier months. Four: gift card and store credit redemptions, which recognised revenue with no deposit at all. Only the fourth is invisible in the payout data, which is why P8.5 comes first.

Where it hurts

Costs money — reconciling order totals against bank deposits They cannot match. Fees, payout timing, refunds and non-gateway money all separate them. Building a check on the assumption produces permanent false alarms, and a permanently red report gets ignored.
Fails with no error — assuming payouts only add money ShopifyPaymentsPayoutTransactionType includes a withdrawal back to Shopify Payments. Code that sums payouts as income produces a total that is quietly too high.
Costs money — promising a finance dashboard before checking access Payout data requires the user to have access to payouts, and finance may refuse. Scope that conversation before the build, not after the demo.
Fails with no error — reporting the payout without the summary The net figure alone cannot explain itself. The ShopifyPaymentsPayoutSummary breakdown by transaction type is the part that answers "where did the difference go", and omitting it turns your report into another number nobody trusts.
Costs money — ignoring currency The account tracks balances per supported currency. Summing across them, as warned in P5.1, produces a meaningless figure that looks authoritative.
Attributing a refund to the wrong period A refund reduces the payout it appears in, not the month the original order was placed. Anything that files it by order date makes two months wrong instead of one.
Judgement, not documentation — this is not your number to interpret Same boundary as tax in P7.3 and fraud in P10.4. You can build the report that shows where the difference went. You do not decide what it means for their accounts, and you do not tell them how to book it.

Say it out loud

Explain this to a beginner

A shop sold exactly a thousand pounds today. Explain why the bank will not receive a thousand pounds — and why that is normal rather than a bug.

Say it out loud. Then read on.

Now compare with a plain-words answer

Four things happen between a sale and a bank deposit, and every one of them changes the number.

The processor takes a cut. Handling the card costs something, and that is taken out before the money is sent on. So the amount leaving is already smaller than the amount sold.

The money is sent on a timetable, not per sale. So a deposit does not correspond to a day. It covers part of one day and part of another, and today's last few sales will be in tomorrow's transfer.

Refunds come out of it. And a refund can be for something bought weeks ago, so it reduces today's transfer for a reason that has nothing to do with today.

Some sales bring no money at all. Gift cards and store credit were paid for earlier, so spending them adds revenue without adding a penny to the bank.

So the two numbers are answers to different questions: "how much did we sell" and "how much money moved". The job is not to make them equal. The job is to be able to say exactly which of those four things accounts for the gap — because that is what the person asking actually needs.

If you used any of these words, try again gross versus net · settlement · payout cadence · clearing · chargeback · accrual. "The processor takes a cut, the money moves on a timetable, refunds come out of it, and some sales bring no money at all" is the explanation, and it needs none of those words.

Build it

Task · 90 minutes real store, read-only, payout access

Build the bridge report — the one thing finance asks for and almost nobody produces.

  1. Query one ShopifyPaymentsPayout with its summary. Note net, issue date and status.
  2. Pull the ShopifyPaymentsBalanceTransaction records behind it.
  3. Produce a single table that starts at orders in the period and ends at the deposit, with one row per reason: fees, timing, refunds, adjustments, non-gateway redemptions.
  4. Check the transaction type on every payout and handle withdrawals as negative.
  5. Group by currency. Never add across currencies.

Proof it worked: your table reconciles exactly, and every line names the reason for its part of the gap. If any line is called "other", keep going — "other" is where the real problem hides.

Ask before you build · 15 minutes a real client

Payout data needs payout access. Ask for it before designing anything, and record the answer.

Proof it worked: you have a yes or a no in writing. A no is a useful finding: it changes the deliverable from a dashboard to an export finance runs themselves.

The sentence · 5 minutes nothing

Say out loud: Those two numbers answer different questions. Let me show you the four things between them — then you can tell me which one you want me to track.

Proof it worked: it comes out without defensiveness. "The numbers do not match" is almost never an accusation about your code, and treating it as one is how the conversation goes wrong.

Remember this

  • Order totals and bank deposits cannot match. Fees, payout timing, refunds and non-gateway money separate them.
  • Your job is to explain the gap, not to close it. The bridge report is the deliverable.
  • ShopifyPaymentsPayoutSummary breaks a payout into fees and gross by transaction type — that is the bridge.
  • ShopifyPaymentsBalanceTransaction is the individual level: every charge, refund and adjustment.
  • Payouts can be withdrawals, not just deposits. Summing them as income overstates it.
  • Payout access is a separate permission and finance may refuse. Ask before you promise.
  • Balances are per currency. Never add across them.

If you remember one thing: "how much did we sell" and "how much money moved" are different questions — build the report that names every step between them.
