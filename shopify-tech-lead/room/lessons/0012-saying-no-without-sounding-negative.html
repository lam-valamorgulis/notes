Track 3 · R3 · Lesson 2

Saying no without sounding negative

Refuse the request, keep the relationship, offer the smaller thing.

Your win today. A three-part sentence shape that lets you decline anything without becoming the person who says no.

How it works

Derive it: why does "no" damage the relationship?

Because a bare no answers a different question than the one asked. They asked "can we have X?" and heard "no". What they actually wanted was the outcome behind X — and you have not addressed it, so the conversation ends with them wanting something and you blocking it.

So the shape follows: acknowledge the outcome, name the real constraint, and offer the nearest thing that serves the outcome.

1. ACKNOWLEDGE the goal "I can see why you want that" 2. NAME the constraint one specific, checkable reason 3. OFFER the nearest thing "what I can do is…" ── the request is refused. the goal is not.

The three kinds of no, and each needs different words

KindThe honest framing
Not possible — the platform will not allow itName the platform rule. It is not your decision, so do not own it
Possible but unwise — it will hurt themName the consequence, then offer the alternative. This is the hardest one
Possible but not now — it competes with somethingMake the trade-off visible and hand the choice back

Not possible

Drill these That one isn't available on your plan — Shopify restricts app content on the payment step to Plus. What I can do on your plan is everything after the order is placed.
Shopify doesn't let us do that. What I can get close to is X — want me to show you? Attributes the limit to the platform, which is where it belongs, then immediately offers something. Never let "not possible" be the last thing you say.

Possible but unwise — the one that takes courage

Drill these I can do that, and I want you to know what it costs before you decide. Adding a customer name here moves us into a higher tier of Shopify's privacy rules — extra approval and a review. If support just needs to find an order, an order number does the same job with none of that. Which would you like?
Happy to build it. The thing I'd worry about is that it'll oversell — that number includes items already sold. Want me to use the sellable figure instead? Never refuse outright and never comply silently. Price it, offer the cheaper option, and hand the decision back. They almost always take the cheaper option.

Possible but not now

Drill these I can do that this sprint if we move the reconciliation report to next. Your call — I'd keep the report, because it's how we'd catch anything the sync gets wrong.
Yes, and it's about three days. Where would you like those three days to come from? The second sentence is the most useful in this Lesson. It never refuses anything and it makes the cost unavoidable.

The words to avoid

Do not sayBecauseSay
"That's impossible"Usually untrue, and it invites a challenge"Shopify doesn't allow that on this plan"
"That's a bad idea"Judges them, not the idea"The thing I'd worry about is…"
"We'd never do that"Makes it about identity"I'd want to check one thing first"
"You don't need that"You do not get to decide what they need"What's the outcome you're after?"
"It's not best practice"Means nothing to them and sounds like preferenceName the specific failure it causes

The last row is the most common mistake among good engineers. "Best practice" is an appeal to authority you do not have. A named failure is an argument.

Answer before reading on A client asks you to scrape a vendor's website because the vendor will not give you API access. Say no in three parts.

Something close to: "I can see why — they're blocking us and you need the data. Two problems though. Without their permission it risks breaching their terms, and it breaks the first time they change their page — silently, so we'd be showing stale data without knowing. What I'd rather do is ask them for the scheduled export instead; there's usually a button for it in their admin already. If you can get written permission for the other approach, I'll build it."

Note the last clause. You have not refused permanently — you have named the condition under which you would proceed, which keeps the decision with them and keeps you out of the position of being the obstacle. And you have offered a concrete alternative that is probably faster anyway.

Where it hurts

Costs money — complying silently with something unwise You build the thing you know will oversell, because saying so felt confrontational. The consequence arrives later and is now partly yours, because you knew.
"It's not best practice" An appeal to an authority the client does not recognise. It sounds like personal preference and loses the argument. Name the specific failure instead.
A bare no with nothing after it The conversation ends with them wanting something and you blocking it. Always follow no with "what I can do is".
Owning a platform limit as your decision "I won't do that" when the truth is "Shopify doesn't allow it" makes you the obstacle for no reason. Attribute limits accurately.
Fails with no error — saying yes to everything Every yes is a no to something else, silently. The project slips and nobody can point to a decision. "Where would you like those three days to come from?" makes the trade visible.
Judgement, not documentation — say it once, then do it If you raise a concern and the client reaffirms the request, that is their decision. Say "understood, I'll build it as asked and I've noted the concern" and then build it properly. Repeating the objection is how a reasonable position becomes obstruction.

What you say to a client/PM

Not possible That specific page is Plus-only — Shopify doesn't allow app content on the payment and shipping steps below that plan. What I can do on your plan is everything after the order is placed, plus the server-side rules. If that page genuinely matters, it's a plan conversation, and I'd want to be sure it's worth it for one message. Names the platform rule, offers the alternative, and declines to upsell them. That last part is what makes the no trustworthy.
Possible but unwise I can build it. Before you decide — that number includes stock that's already been sold but not shipped, so sending it across would let customers buy things you can't ship. I'd use the sellable figure instead. Same work, and it won't oversell. Shall I do that? Yes, then the consequence, then the better version, then a question. They get to choose and the right thing is the easy choice.
Possible but not now Yes — about three days. Where would you like those three days to come from? Right now they'd have to come out of the reconciliation report, and I'd keep the report if it were me, because it's how we'd catch anything going wrong quietly. Never refuses, makes the cost unavoidable, and gives a recommendation. This is the most reusable sentence in the Chapter.

Remember this

  • Three parts: acknowledge the goal · name the constraint · offer the nearest thing.
  • Refuse the request, never the outcome. A bare no answers the wrong question.
  • Three kinds of no: not possible · possible but unwise · possible but not now.
  • Never comply silently with something unwise. Price it and hand the decision back.
  • "Where would you like those three days to come from?" — never refuses, always makes the cost visible.
  • Never say "best practice". Name the specific failure.
  • Say the concern once. If they reaffirm, build it properly and note it.

If you remember one thing: always follow no with "what I can do is" — a no with an offer after it is not a refusal.

Ask questions. Then "next" for giving a number without giving a promise.
