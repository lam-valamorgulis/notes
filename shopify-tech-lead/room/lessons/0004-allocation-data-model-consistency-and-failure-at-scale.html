Track 3 · R1 · Lesson 4 · depth

Allocation, data model, consistency and failure at scale

The second half of the design, where the genuinely hard trade-offs live.

Your win today. You can defend a consistency choice out loud — and explain why allocating an order to the "nearest" shop is the wrong rule.

How it works

Allocation: which place fulfils this order?

An order arrives. Three places hold the item. Which one ships it? The obvious answer — nearest — is wrong, and the reason is instructive.

RuleOptimisesBreaks because
Nearest placeDelivery timeBusiest shops get every order. Their staff cannot serve customers
Most stockAvoiding stock-outsIgnores distance entirely. Slow and expensive delivery
Cheapest to shipMarginConcentrates work in one place and ignores its capacity
Weighted scoreA business decisionNeeds the business to state its weights, which is the real work

So allocation is not an algorithm you choose, it is a set of weights the business owns: distance, stock depth, the place's remaining capacity today, and how much you want to protect a shop's walk-in trade. Your job is to make the weights visible and adjustable, not to pick them.

The word for the missing constraint is capacity. A place can fulfil a limited number of orders per day before its staff stop serving customers. Any allocation rule without a capacity cap will eventually break one shop, and the shop's complaint will arrive as "the website is sending us too much".

Fairness, and why it is a real requirement

Left alone, an efficient rule concentrates work. Three consequences: one shop's staff are overloaded, its stock depletes fastest so it stops being able to serve walk-ins, and the other shops' stock sits idle. Deliberate spreading is cheaper than the alternative — and it is a sentence you will need, because "fair" sounds like a soft requirement until you name those three effects.

The data model, and the one hard decision in it

item (id, external ids) place (id, kind, capacity_per_day, buffer) count (item, place, quantity, as_of) ← the timestamp again promise (item, place, quantity, expires_at) ← expiring claims allocation (order, item, place, state) ← who was chosen, and why

Two things earn their place. as_of on every count, as in R1.3. And allocation as a stored record rather than a computed answer — because when a shop asks "why did we get this order?", the answer must be retrievable months later. A decision you cannot explain afterwards is a decision you will be blamed for.

Consistency: pick one, and be able to say why

This is the question a grader will push on, and the honest answer is not "strongly consistent".

OperationConsistency neededBecause
Showing availability on a product pageEventually consistentIt is information. Slightly stale is fine if labelled
Creating a promise (a reservation)Strongly consistentTwo customers must not be promised the last one
Ingesting a count updateEventually consistentAbsolute writes make ordering irrelevant
Allocating an orderStrongly consistent per placeCapacity must not be exceeded by a race

That table is the answer. Consistency is per operation, not per system — and saying so is what separates a considered design from a slogan. Reserve the expensive guarantee for the two operations that make a promise, and let the read path be cheap and fast.

What breaks at scale, in order of likelihood

#BreaksDesign that survives it
1The read path — every product page asksCache with a labelled age. Never a live third-party call per page view
2One noisy tenant — a huge retailer starves the restPer-tenant work budgets, and per-tenant metrics rather than averages
3Ingestion bursts — a nightly full feed from every retailer at onceStagger schedules; do not let every tenant pick 2am
4Promise expiry — millions of rows needing timely expiryExpire lazily on read as well as on a sweep, so a late sweep cannot oversell
5Fleet blindness — 399 healthy tenants hide one brokenReport the worst tenant and the count outside tolerance, never the mean

Item 4 is the subtle one. If expiry only happens on a scheduled sweep, a delayed sweep means expired promises still consuming stock. Expiring lazily on read as well means correctness does not depend on a job running on time.

Answer before reading on A grader asks: "is your system strongly or eventually consistent?" What is the strongest answer?

Refuse the framing, politely, and answer per operation. "Both, deliberately. Availability display is eventually consistent — it is information, and I label how fresh it is. Reservations and allocation are strongly consistent, because those make a promise to a customer and two people must not be promised the same last item. I would not pay for a strong guarantee on the read path, because it is the hottest path in the system and nothing depends on it being exact."

That answer demonstrates the thing the question is actually testing: whether you know consistency is a cost you spend where it buys something, rather than a property you declare about a whole system.

Where it hurts

Costs money — allocation with no capacity cap Efficient rules concentrate orders. One shop gets buried, its staff stop serving walk-ins, and its stock depletes while others sit idle. The complaint arrives as "the website is sending us too much".
Costs money — strong consistency on the read path The hottest path in the system, paying for a guarantee nothing needs. It becomes the bottleneck and the cost centre simultaneously.
Fails with no error — promise expiry only on a sweep The sweep is late, expired promises still hold stock, and availability is quietly understated — or, if the arithmetic goes the other way, oversold. Expire lazily on read too.
Fails with no error — allocation not stored A shop asks why they received an order and nobody can answer, because the decision was computed and discarded. You lose the argument by default.
Every tenant scheduled at 2am Default schedules cluster. Your ingestion peak is self-inflicted and arrives all at once. Stagger by tenant at signup.
Judgement, not documentation — fairness needs a name in the requirements If "spread the load" is not written down as a requirement, the first optimisation pass will remove it, because it looks like inefficiency. Name it, with the three consequences, in the design document.

Say it out loud

Explain this to a beginner

Explain why "which shop should fulfil this order?" is not a question for an algorithm — and why you must store the answer.

Say it out loud. Then read on.

Now compare with a plain-words answer

Because the right answer depends on things only the business knows and only the business can weigh. Nearest shop? Cheapest to ship? The one with too much stock? The one whose staff have time today? Spread it around so no single shop gets buried?

Those are trade-offs about money, staff and fairness. An engineer picking one is quietly making a business decision. So the design should hold weights the business owns and can change, not a clever rule you chose.

And the constraint everyone forgets is capacity. Without a limit per shop, the "best" shop wins every time and gets buried in work while others sit idle — and it will look like your system is broken when it is doing exactly what you told it.

On storing it: in three months somebody will ask "why did we get this order?" If you did not record the decision and the inputs, the honest answer is "we cannot tell you", and that answer destroys trust in the whole system. So you store what was decided and why.

One more: how careful you need to be varies by operation. Showing a number on a page can be a little out of date and nobody is harmed. Making a promise, or allocating work, cannot — two customers cannot both get the last one.

If you used any of these words, try again allocation algorithm · optimisation function · strong versus eventual consistency · audit trail · load balancing. "Those are trade-offs about money and staff, so an engineer picking one is making a business decision" is the explanation.

Build it

Task · 60 minutes paper
  1. Write the allocation inputs as named weights a business person could change without a deploy.
  2. Add a capacity per place constraint. Then simulate 100 orders without it and watch one shop take nearly all of them.
  3. Write fairness as a requirement with its three consequences, so it cannot be quietly optimised away later.
  4. Design the record that answers "why did we get this order?" — decision, inputs, timestamp.
  5. Make a table of your operations and mark each eventual or strong. Display is eventual; promises and allocation are strong.

Proof it worked: you can answer "why did we get this order?" for a specific simulated order, months later, from stored data. And your consistency table shows you are not paying for strong guarantees on things that just display a number.

The conversation it enables · 20 minutes, written no store needed

Write the one-page note that hands the weights back to the business: here are the levers, here is what each one does, here is who changes them.

Proof it worked: the note contains no algorithm names, and a non-engineer could change the behaviour after reading it.

Remember this

  • Allocation is weights the business owns, not an algorithm you pick.
  • Capacity per place is the missing constraint. Without it, one shop gets buried.
  • Fairness is a real requirement with three named consequences. Write it down or it gets optimised away.
  • Store the allocation decision. "Why did we get this order?" must be answerable months later.
  • Consistency is per operation. Eventual for display, strong for promises and allocation.
  • Expire promises lazily on read as well as on a sweep, so correctness does not depend on a job's punctuality.
  • Stagger tenant schedules. Every default is 2am, and that peak is self-inflicted.

If you remember one thing: consistency is a cost you spend where it buys something — strong for promises, eventual for information.
