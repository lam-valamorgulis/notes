Track 3 · R1 · Lesson 1

System design, explained out loud

Knowing the design and being able to say it are two different skills. This is the second one.

Your win today. A fixed order for describing any system, so you never get lost mid-explanation and never leave out the part that matters most.

How it works

Derive it: why do good engineers explain badly?

Because they explain in the order they built it, and that is the reverse of the order someone needs to hear it. You built from the bottom — database, then services, then the flow. The listener needs the top: what problem, then what moves, then where it breaks.

So the fix is not "communicate better". It is a fixed running order you follow every time, which removes the improvisation that makes people ramble.

1. THE PROBLEM one sentence. what breaks if we do nothing 2. THE PIECES three to five boxes. no more 3. THE FLOW follow ONE thing through, end to end 4. WHERE IT HURTS volunteer two or three failures 5. HOW WE KNOW how you would tell it was working

1 · The problem, in one sentence

Not "we need a sync". Rather: "Their staff edit prices in the ERP, customers buy on Shopify, and right now nobody knows which number is right." A problem stated as a consequence, not as a missing feature.

2 · Three to five boxes

Working memory is small. Five boxes is the practical ceiling for someone hearing this for the first time, and four is better. If your design genuinely has nine components, group them — you can always open a box later if asked.

3 · Follow one thing through

This is the step that makes it click, and the one people skip. Do not describe the components and their responsibilities. Take one price change and walk it from the ERP to the storefront, saying what happens at each box.

A single concrete journey teaches more than a complete architecture, because the listener can hold it. And it exposes gaps in your own design immediately — if you cannot narrate one item's journey, you do not have a design yet.

4 · Volunteer the failures

Two or three, unprompted. "The webhook can arrive twice, so this table makes that harmless." This is the single largest upgrade to how any technical explanation lands, because a design that describes only the happy path sounds complete and reads as inexperienced — and the listener cannot say why they feel uneasy.

5 · How you would know it works

Almost nobody reaches this. Ending on measurement makes the whole answer feel operational rather than theoretical: "every night we compare what we sent against what is actually there, and report the differences."

That third row is worth practising deliberately. A checkpoint every ninety seconds turns a monologue into a conversation, and it lets you discover you have lost someone while it is still cheap.

Do this now, out loud Describe the sync from Track 2 in the five-step order, in under three minutes, without notes. Where did you stall?

Most people stall at step 3 — the walkthrough — because it demands specificity that steps 1 and 2 let you avoid. "The ingest layer normalises data" is comfortable and says nothing. "A price changes in the ERP at 9:04; a webhook arrives; we ignore what is in it and read the current value; we write it to Shopify; we record what we sent" is uncomfortable and is the actual answer.

If you stalled at step 4 instead, it is not a speaking problem — you have not yet decided what your design does when things fail, and the listener would have found that out one question later.

Where it hurts

Starting with the database Concrete, so it feels safe. But the schema is a consequence of decisions the listener has not heard yet, so you are giving answers before questions. Lead with the problem.
Nine boxes Past five, the listener stops tracking and starts nodding. You will not notice, because nodding looks like understanding. Group aggressively and open boxes on request.
Fails with no error — the uninterrupted monologue Four minutes with no checkpoint. They lost you at minute one and are too polite to say. You finish, they say "great", and nothing was communicated. Check every ninety seconds.
Only the happy path Sounds complete, reads as junior. The listener's unease has no name, so it becomes a general lack of confidence in you rather than a specific question you could answer.
Renaming their domain They say "styles", you say "product variants". Every sentence now needs a mental translation and some of them will be wrong. Use their nouns, even when yours are more precise.
Judgement, not documentation — do not defend, revise When someone challenges a design, the instinct is to defend it. The stronger move is "that's a good point, that would change this box". Visibly updating a design in front of someone builds far more confidence than defending it does — and it is why you keep the drawing editable.

What you say to a client/PM

Opening — the one-sentence problem The situation today is that your team edits prices in the ERP and customers buy on Shopify, and nothing keeps those two in step. So the price on your website is whatever it was last time somebody updated it by hand. That's what I want to fix, and I'll show you how in about three minutes. States the problem as a consequence they recognise, and sets a time budget so they know to listen rather than wait for a gap.
The checkpoint That's the shape of it — three parts. Before I go into how a price actually travels, does that much make sense, or shall I go back over any of it? Explicitly offers going back, which makes it safe to admit confusion. "Any questions?" gets "no" from a room that is completely lost.
Closing — measurement And the way you'll know it's working isn't the absence of errors — a sync doing nothing has no errors either. Every night we compare what we sent against what's actually in Shopify and report the differences. If that number is zero, it's working. That's the number I'd put in front of you. Ends on something they can hold and ask for. It also quietly demonstrates you have thought past go-live, which most people have not.

Remember this

  • Fixed order: problem · pieces · flow · failures · measurement. Never improvise the order.
  • State the problem as a consequence, not as a missing feature.
  • Three to five boxes. Past five, people nod instead of following.
  • Walk one thing through end to end. If you cannot, you do not have a design yet.
  • Volunteer two or three failures. The single biggest upgrade to how this lands.
  • Check in every ninety seconds, and offer to go back.
  • Revise in front of them rather than defending. It builds more confidence than being right.

If you remember one thing: walk one item through the whole system out loud — that one habit fixes most bad explanations.

Ask questions. Or say "drill me" and I will interrupt you mid-answer the way a real client does.
