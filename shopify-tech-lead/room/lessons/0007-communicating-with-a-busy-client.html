Track 3 · R2 · Lesson 3

Communicating with a busy client

Five days of silence, then a decision needed today. How to write so you get an answer.

Your win today. A message format that gets replies from people who do not read long messages — and a rule for what to do when nobody answers at all.

How it works

Derive it: why do your messages not get answered?

Not because the client is rude. Because answering costs them something and your message did not make that cost small. A busy client reading your message has to work out: what is this about, is it for me, what do you want, and by when. If any of those takes effort, the message moves to "later", and later does not arrive.

So the format follows directly: put the ask first, make it a single decision, and give it a date.

BAD GOOD context… ONE-LINE ASK, at the top more context… │ what you tried… the two options a question, buried │ another question what happens if no answer by │ ── requires reading to find context, below, for whoever wants it the ask. gets deferred.

The four-line message

LineContent
1 · The askOne sentence, and it must be answerable with a choice, not an essay
2 · The optionsTwo, with the consequence of each. Never three or more
3 · Your recommendation"I'd do the first one, because…" Always include this
4 · The default"If I don't hear by Thursday I'll go with the first one and we can change it later"

Line 3 is the one people leave out to seem neutral. Do not — a recommendation converts a question into an approval, and approving is far cheaper than deciding. Most busy clients will simply say "yes, do that", which is exactly what you wanted.

Line 4 is the one that unblocks you. A stated default with a date turns silence into an answer. It is not a threat and it is not passive-aggressive — it is how you keep moving without either guessing or stalling. Only use it where the default is genuinely reversible.

Two questions in one message is one question too many

A message with two decisions in it gets zero answers, because the reader cannot finish it in one pass. Send them separately, or bundle them into a single "here is what I'm going to do unless you object" list. One decision per message, always.

The weekly note nobody asked for

Send a short update every week even when nothing needs a decision. Three lines: done, next, blocked. It costs you five minutes and it buys two things — the client never wonders what is happening, and when you do need something urgently, you are not a stranger sending an emergency.

Done this week: the delta load and the reconciliation report Next week: inventory flowing back from Shopify Blocked on you: ERP credentials — chased Tuesday, still waiting

That third line is the important one. A blocker written down every week, dated, becomes a shared fact rather than a surprise. The first time it appears it is information. By the fourth week it is evidence, and nobody can be surprised by the delay.

When nobody answers at all

AttemptDo
1The four-line message, with a default and a date
2After the date: "Going with option A as flagged. Easy to change — say if you'd rather not"
3If it is genuinely irreversible: escalate to whoever owns the outcome, naming the cost of waiting

Never let an irreversible decision be made by silence. And never let a reversible one block you for a week.

Answer before reading on You have not heard from the client in five days. You need to know whether their team edits product descriptions in Shopify, because the answer decides whether tonight's run overwrites their work. Write the message.

Something close to: "Quick decision needed before tonight's run. Does anyone on your team edit product descriptions directly in Shopify? If yes, I'll leave descriptions alone and only sync prices and stock. If no, I'll sync descriptions from the ERP too. I'd guess yes and leave them alone — safer, and easy to switch on later. If I don't hear by 4pm I'll go with leaving them alone."

Note what that does. The ask is first and answerable with one word. Both options have a stated consequence. There is a recommendation. And the default is the safe, reversible one — leaving data alone can be undone; overwriting it cannot. When you pick a default, pick the direction that is recoverable.

Where it hurts

Costs money — the destructive default If you must default, default to the reversible option. Defaulting to "overwrite" because it was the faster path means silence caused permanent data loss and you chose it.
Costs money — waiting a week for a reversible decision A cheap decision, blocked on someone who is busy, costing you days. State a default and move. Blocking on something you could have changed later is a self-inflicted delay.
Fails with no error — the buried ask Four paragraphs of context with a question in the middle. It reads as an update, not a request, so it gets no reply and you conclude the client is ignoring you. Ask first, context last.
Fails with no error — two decisions in one message You get an answer to one and assume the other was agreed. It was not read. One decision per message.
No weekly note The client has no idea what is happening, so your first urgent message in three weeks arrives cold and reads as a problem. Regular contact is what makes an urgent request land.
Judgement, not documentation — record blockers on the day, every time A blocker mentioned once in week one and then dropped becomes your fault by week six. The same blocker in every weekly note, dated, is a shared fact nobody can be surprised by. Repetition is the mechanism.

What you say to a client/PM

The four-line decision message Need a call from you before Thursday. Do we send historical orders across, or keep the old system readable as an archive? Sending them is about two weeks and the money won't be exactly right; the archive is a day and keeps the numbers accurate. I'd do the archive. If I don't hear by Thursday I'll plan for the archive and we can revisit. Ask, two costed options, a recommendation, a dated default. Answerable in five seconds with "agreed".
Following the default through Didn't hear back, so as flagged I've gone with the archive. Nothing is lost — if you want the full order history later, it's still possible, it's just the two-week version. Wanted to make sure you knew the choice was made. No reproach, and it restates that the decision is reversible. This is the message that makes a client comfortable with you using defaults again.
Escalating an irreversible one I've asked twice about who owns product pricing and I don't want to guess, because whichever way I build it, the other team's work gets overwritten and there's no undo. I've paused that piece and I'm working on inventory instead, so nothing is idle — but the go-live date moves by however long this takes. Who should I be asking? Names the irreversibility, shows you kept working, states the cost, and asks for a person rather than an answer. That last part is often what actually unblocks it.

Remember this

  • Ask first, context last. A buried question reads as an update and gets no reply.
  • Four lines: ask · two costed options · your recommendation · a dated default.
  • Always recommend. Approving is much cheaper for a busy person than deciding.
  • A stated default with a date turns silence into an answer — but only for reversible things.
  • Default to the recoverable direction. Leaving data alone can be undone; overwriting cannot.
  • One decision per message. Two gets you zero answers.
  • Weekly note: done, next, blocked on you. The blocker line, repeated and dated, is the whole value.

If you remember one thing: give them something to approve, not something to decide — and say what you will do if they say nothing.
