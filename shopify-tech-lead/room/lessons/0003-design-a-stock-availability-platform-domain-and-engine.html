Track 3 · R1 · Lesson 3 · depth

Design a stock-availability platform: domain and engine

A full worked design, so you have one you have actually thought through.

Your win today. One system you can design from scratch out loud — the domain, then the engine that answers the only question that matters.

How it works

The problem, in one sentence

A retailer with shops and a website wants to answer: "can I get this item, here, soon?" — for every item, every shop, and every customer, continuously.

Notice how much that one sentence contains. Not "how many do we have" — that is a number Shopify already holds. It is can I get it, which folds in place, time, and a promise.

The domain: five nouns, and no more

NounIsIs not
ItemThe thing a customer chooses. A variantA product. Nobody buys "a t-shirt"
PlaceSomewhere stock physically isAlways a shop — warehouses and third parties count
CountItem + Place + a number + a timestampA property of the item
PromiseA reservation. Someone is coming for itA sale. It can expire
RuleWhat turns counts into an answerConfiguration. It is the product

The timestamp on Count is the load-bearing decision. A stock number without an age is unusable, because you cannot tell a fresh zero from a stale five. Make freshness part of the data, not a property of the pipeline that carried it.

And Promise as a separate noun is what most designs get wrong. A reservation is not a sale and not a stock adjustment — it is a claim with an expiry. Model it separately and you can expire it, report on it, and explain it. Fold it into the count and you can do none of those.

The engine: turning counts into an answer

The naive engine returns the number. The real engine returns an answer to a question a customer asked, which is a different thing.

counts per place (each with an age) │ ├── subtract PROMISES that have not expired │ ├── apply a SAFETY BUFFER per place │ a shop floor is not accurate to the unit │ ├── apply FRESHNESS rules │ older than N minutes → downgrade the confidence │ └── apply RULES per place: is it sellable from here at all? per item: minimum before we say "available" ▼ an ANSWER, not a number: available · low · unavailable · unknown

Four outcomes, not three. "Unknown" is the one people leave out, and it is the one that keeps you honest — a place whose count is two days old should say unknown, not zero and not five.

Why bands rather than counts

Derive it rather than accepting it. A shop floor miscounts: items are held, damaged, in a fitting room, or mis-scanned. So the true count has an error margin of a few units. Publishing an exact number implies an accuracy the physical world does not provide, and the customer's decision — is it worth the trip? — does not need it. A band absorbs the error and answers the real question.

Exception worth naming: "one left" is the most dangerous number to display, because it is both the most likely to be wrong and the most likely to make someone travel. Many retailers deliberately show nothing below a threshold.

The safety buffer, and who owns it

A buffer of two units per shop means you show "unavailable" while two remain. You lose a few sales and avoid a few wasted journeys. That is a business trade-off, not a technical one — so it is configuration per place, set by the business, not a constant in your code. Making it configurable is part of the design, not a nicety.

Answer before reading on A shop's count says 3. There is one unexpired promise against it, the buffer for that shop is 2, and the count is 40 minutes old. What do you tell the customer?

3 − 1 promise = 2, minus a buffer of 2 = 0, so the honest answer is unavailable — even though a shop staff member looking at the shelf would see three.

That gap is the whole point of the design, and it is the thing you must be able to explain to the client, because they will ask why their website says no while the shelf says yes. The answer: one is promised to someone who is on their way, and two are held back because a shop floor is not accurate to the unit. You are not reporting stock; you are answering "can this customer get it".

The 40-minute age is a separate judgement. If the freshness threshold is 30 minutes, the correct answer becomes unknown rather than unavailable — and "we can't confirm right now" is a better thing to show a customer than a confident wrong no.

Where it hurts

Costs money — a count with no age You cannot distinguish a fresh zero from a stale five, so you either trust old data or discard good data. Both cost sales, and neither is visible. Put the timestamp in the data model.
Costs money — promises folded into counts A reservation modelled as a stock decrement cannot expire, cannot be reported on, and cannot be explained to a merchant asking where the item went. Then abandoned reservations quietly consume real stock forever.
Fails with no error — no "unknown" state Stale data reported as a confident number. Everything looks fine and some answers are simply invented. Four outcomes, not three.
Fails with no error — a buffer hardcoded in the engine The business wants it different per shop, and per season. Hardcoded, it becomes a deploy every time they change their mind, and nobody remembers what the number is or why.
Modelling Place as "shop" Warehouses, third-party fulfilment and popups all hold stock. Naming the concept "shop" bakes in an assumption you will fight later, in every query and every screen.
Judgement, not documentation — the exact-number request will come back A client who accepted bands will ask again for exact counts in six months, usually because one customer complained. Write the reasoning down when you agree it, so the second conversation is a reference rather than a re-litigation.

Say it out loud

Explain this to a beginner

Why must a stock count carry a timestamp as part of the data itself, rather than freshness being a property of the pipeline?

Say it out loud. Then read on.

Now compare with a plain-words answer

Because otherwise you cannot tell the difference between "there are none" and "we have not heard from that shop in three days".

Both arrive as zero. One means do not send the customer. The other means you have no idea, and sending them is a coin flip. If the number does not carry when it was true, those two situations are indistinguishable, and you will treat missing information as certainty.

People try to solve this outside the data — "our pipeline runs every 15 minutes, so numbers are at most 15 minutes old". That holds until one shop's feed breaks, and then the assumption is silently false for that shop only, which is the hardest kind of wrong to notice.

Put the time in the count and the question answers itself everywhere, forever.

The related idea in the same design: the thing a customer is asking is not "how many are there" but "can I get this, here, soon?" So you need a way to say "I have set one aside for you" that is neither a sale nor a stock change — a claim with an expiry. Without that, two customers can both be told yes for the same item.

If you used any of these words, try again staleness · TTL · soft reservation · eventual consistency · domain model. "You cannot tell a fresh zero from a stale zero" is the explanation.

Build it

Task · 60 minutes paper, then code
  1. Write the five nouns down: Item · Place · Count · Promise · Rule. One sentence each.
  2. Give Count a timestamp field. Then write the two zeros side by side: a fresh zero and a three-day-old zero, and what your system does differently for each.
  3. Define Promise with an expiry. Write what happens when it expires unused.
  4. Write the engine as five ordered steps: subtract promises, apply buffer, check freshness, apply rules, answer.
  5. Now implement just the freshness check, and test it with a stale zero.

Proof it worked: your engine returns a different answer for a fresh zero and a stale zero. If both produce "not available", you have thrown away the distinction that the timestamp exists to preserve.

Explain it in five minutes · 20 minutes, recorded a voice recorder

Record the design using the fixed order from R1.1, leading with the five nouns rather than tables.

Proof it worked: you got to Promise and its expiry without being asked. That is the noun that shows you understood the question is about a claim, not a count.

Remember this

  • The question is "can I get this, here, soon?" — not "how many are there".
  • Five nouns: Item, Place, Count, Promise, Rule.
  • A Count carries a timestamp. Freshness is data, not a pipeline property.
  • A Promise is a claim with an expiry, not a sale and not a stock adjustment.
  • The engine subtracts promises, applies a buffer, checks freshness, applies rules.
  • Four outcomes: available · low · unavailable · unknown. "Unknown" keeps you honest.
  • The buffer is a business setting per place, never a constant in code.

If you remember one thing: put the timestamp in the count — without it you cannot tell a fresh zero from a stale five, and every other decision gets worse.
