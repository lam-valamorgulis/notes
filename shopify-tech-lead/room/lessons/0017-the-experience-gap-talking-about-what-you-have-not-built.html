Track 3 · R5 · Lesson 1

The experience gap: talking about what you have not built

A named gap with work attached is a strength. A hidden one is a risk they can smell.

Your win today. A way to answer "have you done this before?" honestly, when the answer is no, that leaves you stronger than a vague yes would.

How it works

Derive it: what is the question actually asking?

"Have you run a live sync in production?" sounds like a check on your history. It is not — nobody cares about your past for its own sake. They are asking "will this go wrong on my watch, and will I find out from you or from a customer?"

That reframing changes everything. A vague yes answers the surface question and fails the real one, because a vague answer is exactly what someone hiding a gap sounds like. And they hear it, even if they cannot name what they heard.

what they ASK "have you done this?" what they MEAN "will this go wrong, and will you tell me?" vague yes → answers the first, fails the second bare no → answers neither named gap + the work → answers the second, which is the one that matters

The three-part answer

PartContent
1 · Name it plainly"I haven't run one in production." No hedging, no softening
2 · Say what you did about itThe specific thing you built or learned, with detail only a doer would have
3 · Say what you know is still missingThe honest remainder. This is the part that makes parts 1 and 2 believable

Part 3 is counter-intuitive and it is the whole technique. Volunteering what your own work does not cover is what proves you are not overselling. Anyone can claim to have built something; only someone who actually did it knows precisely where its edges are.

Why part 2 has to be specific

"I've been learning about it" is worthless — it is what someone says who has read about it. The details are the evidence:

WeakStrong
"I've studied sync patterns.""I built one — a service that syncs a fake ERP with a Shopify dev store. It has switches to make the ERP go stale, duplicate a record, or fail mid-batch, because I wanted to cause those failures rather than wait for them."
"I understand rate limiting.""I found out the hard way that a throttled GraphQL call returns HTTP 200 — the failure is inside the response body. So checking the status code silently misses it."
"I know about data quality issues.""I sampled a real store and found the same option axis spelled both 'Color' and 'Colour', and a tag in two casings. So my test data reproduces those on purpose."

Each strong version contains something you could only know by doing it. That is the point — a specific detail is not showing off, it is the proof.

What not to do

Do notBecause
Claim experience you do not haveOne follow-up question exposes it, and then everything else you said is in doubt
Say "no" and stopYou have confirmed their concern and given them nothing
Blame circumstances — "I never got the chance"Shifts it to something outside your control, which is the opposite of what they want to hear
Overclaim the side project as productionA dev store is not production and they know it. Say what it is
ApologiseThe gap is a fact, not a failing. Treat it as one

The honest framing for a self-built project

Be precise about what it is and is not. "A real service against a real Shopify dev store, with real rate limits and real webhooks — but no real customers and no real money." That sentence costs you nothing and buys all your other claims credibility, because you have shown you know the difference.

Do this now, out loud "Have you handled a production sync between Shopify and an ERP?" Answer in three parts, from memory.

Something close to: "Not in production, no. What I've done is build one — a NestJS service syncing a fake ERP with a Shopify dev store, deliberately with failure switches so I could cause stale reads, duplicate webhooks and mid-batch failures rather than wait for them. It has a reconciliation report, because I wanted to know when it was quietly wrong. What I haven't dealt with is a real merchant's data being wrong at nine in the morning with someone on the phone — that pressure is the part I can't manufacture."

Check yours against the three parts. Did you name it in the first sentence without hedging? Was part 2 specific enough that only someone who built it could say it? And did you volunteer the remainder?

That last sentence is the strongest thing in the answer. It shows you know exactly what your own work does not prove — and that is the quality they were actually testing for.

Where it hurts

Costs money — the vague yes It survives one question and fails the second. And once one answer is doubted, every other answer is re-examined. A hedge costs more than the gap it was hiding.
"No" with nothing after it You have confirmed the concern and offered nothing to weigh against it. The gap becomes the whole answer.
Blaming circumstances "I never got the opportunity" moves it outside your control, which is precisely the opposite of the signal you want. What you did about it is the answer.
Fails with no error — a generic part 2 "I've been reading about it" produces no evidence and quietly confirms the gap. The specificity is the evidence — no detail, no proof.
Overclaiming a dev-store project Calling it production experience is a small lie that discredits a genuinely strong story. Say what it is; the work is impressive enough described accurately.
Judgement, not documentation — the gap closes by working, not by rehearsing No sentence makes an unbuilt thing built. This Lesson makes the honest answer as strong as it can be — the Project in Track 2 is what changes the answer itself. Do the work; the words are the smaller half.

Say it out loud

Explain this to a beginner

"Have you done this before?" — and you have not. Explain what they are actually asking, and why the third part of the answer is what makes the first two believable.

Say it out loud. Then read on.

Now compare with a plain-words answer

They are not really asking about your history. They are asking two things: is this going to go wrong, and will you tell me when it does?

The second one is the one you can answer perfectly regardless of your experience. And it is the one they care about more, because a problem they hear about early is manageable and a problem they discover late is not.

So the answer has three parts: name the gap plainly, say what you did about it, and say what is still missing.

The third part is the one people skip, and it is what makes the other two credible. Anyone can claim they prepared. Volunteering the remaining gap is something only an honest person does — so it retroactively proves you were honest in parts one and two. Skip it and the whole answer reads as a sales pitch.

Part two has to be specific. Not "I studied it" but a detail only somebody who actually did the work would know — a limit you tested, a failure you reproduced, a number you measured with a date on it. That detail is the evidence.

Three things to never do: claim you have done it, blame circumstances for not having, or apologise. All three answer a question nobody asked.

If you used any of these words, try again transferable skills · adjacent experience · growth mindset · imposter syndrome. "They are asking whether it will go wrong and whether you will tell them" is the explanation.

Build it

Task · 45 minutes, written then recorded a voice recorder
  1. Write your real gap in one plain sentence. No softening.
  2. Write what you did about it, with one specific detail only a doer would know — pull it from your Project. A number, a failure you reproduced, a limit you tested with a date.
  3. Write what is still missing. Volunteer it.
  4. Record it in under 45 seconds.
  5. Listen back and delete every apology and every excuse.

Proof it worked: the answer names the gap in the first sentence and ends on what is still missing, with a concrete Project detail in the middle. That combination is what a real practitioner sounds like. If the middle is "I read the documentation", go and finish another Project step first — this Lesson cannot fix a missing middle.

Update it as the Project grows · 10 minutes, monthly your Project

Every time you finish a Project step, rewrite part two with a newer, sharper detail — and shrink part three.

Proof it worked: part three is smaller than it was last month. That shrinking is the actual goal of this whole Course, and this is where you can watch it happen.

Remember this

  • "Have you done this?" means "will it go wrong, and will you tell me?" Answer the second.
  • Three parts: name it plainly · what you did about it · what is still missing.
  • Part 3 is what makes parts 1 and 2 believable. Volunteer the remainder.
  • Part 2 must be specific. A detail only a doer would know is the evidence.
  • Never claim it, never blame circumstances, never apologise.
  • Say what a dev store is and is not. Precision buys credibility everywhere else.
  • The gap closes by working. The words are the smaller half.

If you remember one thing: name the gap in the first sentence and end on what is still missing — that combination is only available to someone who actually did the work.
