Agile delivery as a senior

Standups, sprints, and what a senior is actually expected to do in them.

Your win today. You know what changes about ceremonies when you are senior — and it is not "attend them better".

How it works

Derive it: what is a ceremony for?

Every ritual exists to move information. Ask what information, and to whom, and the right behaviour follows — including when to break the ritual.

CeremonyExists to moveA senior's job in it
StandupBlockers, to whoever can clear themSurface risk early. Notice who is stuck and not saying so
PlanningShared understanding of what and whyPush back on work that is not ready. Split anything too big
Review / demoEvidence to the people who askedShow it working, including what does not
RetroA change, not a feelingTurn one complaint into one owned action

The pattern: a junior participates in ceremonies; a senior makes them produce their output. A retro that ends with everyone feeling heard and nothing changing has failed, and it is the senior person's job to notice.

Standup: two sentences, and one of them matters

WeakStrong
"Yesterday I worked on the sync. Today I'll continue.""The delta load works. Today I'm on inventory write-back. I'm blocked on the ERP credentials — chased Tuesday, still nothing."

The blocker is the only line with information in it. Say it every day it is true, dated. A blocker mentioned once becomes your fault; a blocker repeated daily becomes a shared fact. Repetition is the mechanism, not nagging.

And the senior addition: notice who else is stuck. Someone saying "still working on it" for three days is a person who needs help and cannot ask. Asking them afterwards — not in the standup — is one of the most valuable things you will do that week.

Planning: the two words that make you useful

"Not ready." A ticket with an unanswered load-bearing question is not estimable, and estimating it anyway is how a sprint becomes fiction. Say what is missing and what would make it ready.

The counterpart is splitting. Integration work splits naturally along the flow:

too big: "sync products with the ERP" split: 1. read from the ERP, log what we'd write ← provable, safe 2. write titles and prices, one direction 3. inventory back the other way 4. reconciliation report 5. failure handling and the dead-letter each one is demonstrable on its own.

Step 1 is the move worth learning: a read-only slice that logs its intentions. It proves the hardest part — that you can get the data and understand it — without writing anything to a live store. Cheap, safe, and it de-risks everything after it.

Demo what does not work, too

Show the failure handling working. Break the ERP on purpose and let the audience watch the retry, the dead-letter and the report. That demo builds more confidence than any number of green screens, because it is the only evidence that the system has been thought about past the happy path.

Retro: one action with a name on it

Five complaints and no owner is a worse outcome than one complaint with a name and a date. If you can only get one thing out of a retro, get that. Volunteer to own it yourself when the room goes quiet — a senior taking one action makes the next retro produce two.

Answer before reading on A ticket says "Sync products with the ERP", sized at three days by someone who has not seen the ERP's API. What do you say in planning?

That it is not ready, and exactly why. "I can't size this yet. Two things decide it: whether their API can tell us what changed since a timestamp, and who owns product descriptions. If the API can do it, it's around three days. If it can't, it's more like eight, because we'd compare full lists every night. I can find out in an hour with their docs."

Then split it. Take the read-only slice into this sprint — read from the ERP, log what we would write, change nothing. That is genuinely three days, it is provable at review, and it answers both questions as a side effect.

Notice what you have done: you refused a fictional estimate, gave a real range with its cause, and still put demonstrable work in the sprint. That combination is what "senior" means in a planning meeting.

Where it hurts

Costs money — estimating an unready ticket The number goes in the plan, the unknown resolves the expensive way, and the sprint misses. Saying "not ready" costs one uncomfortable minute.
Costs money — a blocker mentioned once Raised in week one, dropped, and by week six the delay is attributed to you. Repeat it daily, with the date you last chased it.
Fails with no error — "still working on it" for three days Someone is stuck and cannot say so. Standup gives no signal because the words are the same each day. Notice the repetition, and ask them privately.
Fails with no error — the retro that produces feelings Everyone is heard, nothing changes, and the same complaint returns next month. Without one named owner and one date, the meeting was a conversation.
A ticket that cannot be demonstrated "Set up the database schema" produces nothing anyone can see, so progress is invisible and unverifiable. Slice along the flow, so every slice does something end to end.
Judgement, not documentation — do not enforce process on someone else's team If you are the contractor or the new senior, arriving with opinions about their ceremonies spends credibility on the least important thing available. Do the two behaviours that matter — clear blockers, refuse unready work — and let the rituals be theirs.

What you say to a client/PM

Standup, with a blocker Delta load is working end to end. Today I'm on inventory write-back. Still blocked on the ERP credentials — I chased on Tuesday, nothing yet. If they land tomorrow the date holds; after that it starts moving. Progress, plan, blocker, and the consequence of the blocker. Four clauses and the last one is what gets it acted on.
Refusing an unready ticket I don't want to put a number on this one yet — it'd be a guess and it'd end up in the plan. What I can do this sprint is the read-only half: pull their data, log exactly what we'd change, write nothing. That's three days I'm confident about, it's demonstrable, and it answers the two questions that decide the rest. Refuses and immediately offers something valuable of the same size. Almost impossible to argue with, and it keeps the sprint full.
At the demo Before I show you it working, I want to show you it failing. I'm going to turn the ERP off mid-sync. You'll see it retry, then give up on the ones that can't work, and then the report tells us exactly which records need attention. That's the part I'd want to see if I were you. Demonstrating failure handling is the single most confidence-building thing you can do in a review, and almost nobody does it.

Remember this

  • Every ceremony exists to move information. A senior makes it produce its output.
  • Standup is for blockers. Repeat them daily, dated, with the consequence.
  • Notice who is quietly stuck. Three days of "still working on it" is a signal.
  • "Not ready" is the most useful thing you can say in planning — with what is missing.
  • Split along the flow, and lead with a read-only slice that logs its intentions.
  • Demo the failure handling. It builds more confidence than green screens.
  • One retro action with a name and a date beats five complaints. Volunteer to own it.

If you remember one thing: refuse to estimate what is not ready, and offer a read-only slice instead — you keep the sprint honest and full at the same time.
