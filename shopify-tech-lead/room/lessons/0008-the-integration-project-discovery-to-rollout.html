The integration project: discovery to rollout

The whole programme shape — and which stage kills most of them.

Your win today. A seven-stage shape you can put in front of a client on day one, and the knowledge of which stage to protect.

How it works

The seven stages

1. DISCOVERY what exists, who owns what, what they can give us 2. DATA AUDIT four numbers, measured, not asked for 3. DESIGN the ownership table. signed off by a human 4. BUILD catalogue in → inventory back → reconciliation 5. PROVE load real volume. compare. hand-check 6. PILOT one store, one location, or one product family 7. ROLLOUT in cohorts, chosen for variety

Most projects that fail did stage 1 as a meeting and skipped stage 2 entirely. Everything downstream then rests on what the client believes about their data rather than on what is in it.

Stage 2 is the one to protect: measure, do not ask

Four numbers, from the systems themselves:

NumberWhy measured, not asked
Variant count on both sidesClients say products. And productsCount can return a capped floor — verified at exactly 10,000 with AT_LEAST on a real store
Match rate between the two systems"Everything has a product code" is never true. Measure it, and weight it by units sold
Blank rate on the fields you needA field that exists is not a field that is populated
Change rate per dayDecides whether incremental sync is worth building, and sizes every ongoing cost

Each of those has been wrong, in the client's favour, on every project. Measuring takes a day and prevents the discovery arriving in week six as a crisis.

Discovery questions worth asking

Grouped by what they protect:

GroupAsk
OwnershipWho edits each field, and where? Does anyone edit in Shopify directly?
CapabilityCan the other system tell you what changed since a timestamp? Cursor or page numbers? How are deletions signalled?
IdentityWhat code never changes for the life of a product? Do you ever reuse a retired code?
MoneyIs that stock number sellable, or everything on the shelf?
FailureWhat should happen when the other system is unreachable?
PeopleWho signs off the ownership table? Who do I ask when it is unclear?

That last group is the most neglected and the most useful. A project with no named decision-maker stalls at every ambiguity, and you will not find that out until the first one arrives.

Stage 3 needs a signature, not agreement

The ownership table from A3.2 is the design. It must be reviewed field by field by someone with authority, and the review must be recorded — in the repo, not in a chat. Agreement in a meeting evaporates; a file in a diff does not.

Rollout in cohorts, chosen for variety

Cohort byVerdict
Convenience — the easiest stores firstWrong. Proves nothing. Five similar successes then a wall
Size — smallest firstBetter, but still one dimension
Variety — the awkward ones earlyRight. The odd POS, the store that counts stock weekly, the one with a legacy code format

Deliberately include the difficult case in cohort two, while you still have attention and budget for what it teaches. Left to last, its problems arrive when everyone believes the project is finished.

Answer before reading on A client tells you every product has a unique supplier code. What do you do with that statement?

Measure it, today, before it enters the design. Not because they are lying — because "every product has one" is a statement about how the business is supposed to work, and there will be products with blanks, duplicates, and codes in an old format from before a system change.

Then report it two ways: by row and weighted by units sold. "97% of rows match" hides 1,500 unmatched products, and if four of them are bestsellers the number is meaningless. That second framing is what turns a comfortable percentage into an actionable list — and finding it in week one costs a day, while finding it in week six costs the timeline.

Where it hurts

Costs money — skipping the data audit Every downstream estimate rests on what the client believes about their data. The correction arrives in week six, when it is a crisis rather than a task.
Costs money — no named decision-maker The project stalls at every ambiguity while people work out who decides. Ask for the name in discovery, before you need it.
Costs money — cohorts by convenience Five easy stores succeed, everyone declares victory, and the sixth reveals a problem that invalidates the approach. Put the awkward case in cohort two.
Fails with no error — match rate by row count 97% sounds like a pass. Weighted by sales it can be a disaster. Report both, always.
Fails with no error — sign-off in a chat message Six weeks later nobody remembers agreeing, and the chat is unsearchable. The ownership table belongs in the repo, reviewable in a diff.
Treating discovery as one meeting Discovery is a phase with deliverables — the ownership table, the four numbers, the app inventory, the named decision-maker. A meeting produces notes, not deliverables.
Judgement, not documentation — pilot on something that matters, but not the worst A pilot on your client's bestselling range is too risky; a pilot on a range nobody sells proves nothing because nobody would notice if it broke. Pick something real, watched, and survivable. That judgement is yours and it should be explicit.

What you say to a client/PM

Setting up discovery on day one Before I design anything I want two days to look at the actual data in both systems — how many variants, how well they match up, which fields are actually filled in, and how much changes daily. I'm not doubting what you've told me; it's that these numbers decide the whole approach, and I'd rather find a surprise now than in week six. I also need one name: who decides when something's ambiguous? Asks for the audit and the decision-maker together, and frames the audit as protecting the project rather than checking their claims.
Presenting an uncomfortable audit result Here's what I found. 94% of your products match between the two systems — but the 6% that don't include eleven of your top hundred sellers, so it matters more than the number suggests. Most have blank supplier codes. I need either those filled in, or a rule for what we do without one. It's a day or two of someone's time and it saves us a lot later. Leads with the weighted framing, names the cause, and asks for a specific, bounded action rather than presenting a problem.
Arguing for the awkward store early For the second group of stores I'd like to include the one with the older till system, rather than saving it for last. If it's going to cause problems I want to find them while we still have time and attention for it. Leaving it to the end means discovering it when everyone thinks we're finished. Gives the reason in terms of when attention is available, which is a scheduling argument rather than a technical one — and therefore lands with a PM.

Remember this

  • Seven stages: discovery · data audit · design · build · prove · pilot · rollout.
  • The data audit is the stage to protect. Measure four numbers; do not ask for them.
  • Variant count, match rate, blank rate, change rate. All four have been wrong in the client's favour on every project.
  • Report match rate by row and weighted by units sold.
  • Get a named decision-maker in discovery, before you need one.
  • The ownership table needs a recorded sign-off in the repo, not in a chat.
  • Cohort by variety. The awkward store goes in cohort two, not last.

If you remember one thing: measure the data before you design anything — every project that went wrong skipped that day.
