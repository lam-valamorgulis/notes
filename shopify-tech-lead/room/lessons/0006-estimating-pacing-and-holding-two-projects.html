Track 3 · R2 · Lesson 2

Estimating, pacing, and holding two projects

Why your estimate was wrong, and the shape of one that is not.

Your win today. An estimate format that is honest, useful, and does not collapse the first time something surprises you.

How it works

Derive it: what is an estimate actually a statement about?

People treat an estimate as a prediction of duration. It is not — it is a statement about uncertainty. "Three days" claims you know the work to within a day. Usually you do not, and the number is a wish with a decimal point.

So the correct output is not a number. It is a range, plus the thing that decides where in the range you land. That third part is what makes a range useful rather than evasive.

BAD "about three days" ── no uncertainty stated. becomes a promise. breaks. WEAK "three to eight days" ── honest, and useless. why the spread? GOOD "three days if their API can tell me what changed since a timestamp. Eight if it can't and I have to compare full lists. I'll know within an hour of seeing their docs." ── a range, the reason, and when it collapses.

The last version does three things at once: it gives a bound, it explains the spread, and it names when the uncertainty ends. Nobody argues with that estimate, because it is not asking to be believed — it is describing what is not yet known.

Why your estimates were wrong: the four usual causes

CauseFix
You estimated the happy pathFailure handling, retries and reconciliation are usually 30–50% of integration work. Estimate them as line items
You estimated your partAdd the client's part: getting credentials, an API doc, a decision, a test account. This is often the longest wait in the project
You counted products, not variantsThe platform limits variants. A 4× unit error multiplies straight through
You forgot verificationReconciliation, a sample load, a hand-check. Real work with real duration

The second row is the one that surprises people most. On integration work, waiting on the other side is frequently the critical path, and it is entirely absent from a naive estimate. Name it, with a date, and it becomes visible instead of becoming your fault.

Estimate the shape before the number

When you genuinely cannot size something, size its parts and their confidence:

PieceConfidenceNote
Catalogue inHighDone this shape before
Inventory both waysMediumDepends on whose number is sellable stock
Initial load at volumeLowNeeds one sample load to know. Half a day to find out
Reconciliation and monitoringHighStandard, and non-optional

Handing over that table instead of a single number is more useful to a client than any figure, because it tells them which unknown to spend money resolving first.

Pacing, and holding two projects

The instinct is to split days between projects. Do not. Context switching on integration work is expensive because the state you hold in your head is large — whose field is whose, what the last run did, which of six unknowns is still open.

DoInstead of
Whole days, or whole half-days, on one thingAlternating hour by hour
Deliberately leave one thing waiting on someone elseBoth projects active at once
Write down where you stopped, before switchingTrusting you will remember
Tell both clients your rhythmLetting both believe they are the only one

The second row is the real technique: stagger the projects so one is always blocked on the client while you work the other. Two integrations always have waiting in them — for credentials, for a decision, for a vendor to reply. Two projects that are both waiting are a scheduling accident; two where one is always waiting is a plan.

Answer before reading on A PM asks for "a number, not a range" for an ERP sync you have not scoped. What do you say?

Give a number and make it a commitment about something you control. "I'll give you a number on Thursday. To get it I need their API documentation and half a day to load a sample at your real volume. What I can commit to today is that Thursday date."

That is not evasion, and it is the strongest available answer: you have supplied a firm date, named exactly what you need to hit it, and moved the commitment onto ground you can hold. A PM asking for a number usually needs something to put in a plan — and a date for a number is something.

What you must not do is invent a figure to end the conversation. That figure becomes the plan, and you will spend the rest of the project explaining a number you knew was fiction when you said it.

Where it hurts

Costs money — a number invented to end a conversation It goes into a plan and becomes the baseline you are measured against. Every later conversation is now about a variance from a figure you knew was fiction.
Costs money — omitting the client's part Credentials, decisions and vendor replies are often the longest waits in the project. Absent from the estimate, every delay looks like yours.
Costs money — estimating the happy path Failure handling and verification are a third to a half of integration work. Left out, the project is "nearly done" for a very long time.
Fails with no error — a range with no reason "Three to eight days" reads as unwillingness to commit. The spread must have a named cause, or it sounds like padding.
Splitting the day between two projects Integration state is large and switching costs are real. Two half-days produce less than one whole day, and the loss is invisible on a timesheet.
Judgement, not documentation — re-estimate out loud, early When you learn something that moves the estimate, say so the day you learn it. A number revised at week two is information. The same number revised at week six is a broken promise, even though the work was identical.

Say it out loud

Say it out loud. Then read on.

Now compare with a plain-words answer

Because if you actually knew how long it would take, it would be a schedule, not an estimate. An estimate is a statement about how uncertain you are. A wide range means "there is something here I do not know yet"; a narrow range means "I have done exactly this before".

Which is why a bare number is dishonest in both directions — it hides the uncertainty, and it gets remembered as a promise.

So you give three things: the range, the reason it is that wide, and what would collapse it. A range without a reason reads as padding, and people negotiate padding. A range with "it is wide because I do not know if their export includes deleted rows; one hour with their developer collapses this" does not get negotiated — it gets acted on.

What people leave out: handling failure, and proving it works. Not the writing of the happy path. On integration work that is often a third to a half of the whole job, and it is invisible in a demo, so it is the first thing cut and the reason the project overruns.

Two more habits. Include their part — waiting for access, for a decision, for a file is frequently the actual critical path. And count the individually sellable versions, not products: getting that unit wrong multiplies a mistake through the whole estimate.

If you used any of these words, try again confidence interval · story points · velocity · buffer · critical path. "An estimate is a statement about how uncertain you are" is the explanation.

Build it

Task · 60 minutes, written a real project
  1. Take a real piece of work and write the estimate as range + reason + what collapses it.
  2. Add a separate line for failure handling and verification. If it is under a third of the total, justify why in writing.
  3. Add a line for the client's tasks with their own dates.
  4. Check your unit: variants, not products. Note the multiplier explicitly.
  5. Now try to shorten it by removing the verification line, and write down what you would be giving up.

Proof it worked: your estimate has a named collapsing action per range — something someone can actually do this week to narrow it. And the verification line is visible enough that cutting it is a decision somebody makes rather than something that happens.

The refusal, drilled · 10 minutes a voice recorder

Record yourself saying: I don't want to invent a number for you. Give me until Thursday and I'll bring a range with the reason behind it.

Proof it worked: it comes out calmly, without apologising. You commit to a date for the number, which is a real commitment — never invent a number to end a conversation.

Remember this

  • An estimate is a statement about uncertainty, not a prediction of duration.
  • Range + reason + when it collapses. A range without a reason reads as padding.
  • Estimate failure handling and verification — often a third to a half of integration work.
  • Include the client's part. Waiting is frequently the critical path.
  • Variants, not products. A 4× unit error multiplies through everything.
  • Stagger two projects so one is always blocked on someone else. Whole days, never alternating hours.
  • Re-estimate the day you learn something. Week two it is information; week six it is a broken promise.

If you remember one thing: never invent a number to end a conversation — commit to a date for the number instead.
