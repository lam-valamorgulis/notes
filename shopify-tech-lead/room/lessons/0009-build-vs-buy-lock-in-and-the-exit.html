Build vs buy, lock-in, and the exit

The question a client should ask you and usually does not: how do we get out?

Your win today. A three-question test for build versus buy, and the habit of designing the exit at the same time as the entrance.

How it works

Derive it: what are you actually buying?

Not features. You are buying someone else's ongoing maintenance — their handling of API version changes, their edge cases, their on-call. And you are paying for it with money and with a dependency.

So the question is never "could we build this?" — you almost always could. It is "do we want to own this forever?"

AskIf yes
1. Is this a source of competitive advantage — something customers choose you for?Build. Do not outsource your differentiator
2. Will we have someone maintaining it in two years?Building is sustainable. If no, buy
3. Is the vendor's version genuinely close to what we need?Buy. If it is 60% right, buying costs more than building

Question 1 is the strategic one and question 2 is the honest one. Question 2 kills most build decisions, and it is the one nobody asks aloud — because the answer is usually "no, we'd build it and then nobody would own it".

The three kinds of lock-in, in order of severity

1. DATA lock-in they hold data you cannot export ── worst. you cannot leave at any price. 2. PROCESS lock-in your team's workflows are shaped around them ── expensive. retraining and rebuilding. 3. INTEGRATION lock-in you wrote code against their API ── cheapest. it is just work.

Everyone worries about the third and it is the least important. Data lock-in is the one to negotiate away before signing, because no amount of engineering fixes it later. And this Course has seen the concrete version: a vendor platform holding a customer credit ledger — a balance that is real money, living in a system you cannot export from.

Design the exit at the same time as the entrance

Three questions to answer before you commit, in writing:

QuestionAcceptable answer
Can we get all our data out, in a usable format, on demand?Yes, self-serve, without asking them
How much of our code touches them directly?One module. Never sprinkled through
What would replacing them cost, in weeks?A number you have actually thought about

The middle row is entirely within your control, and it is the cheapest insurance in software. Put the vendor behind one module — a façade with your own vocabulary. Then swapping them is rewriting one module rather than auditing the whole codebase. It costs almost nothing to do up front and is expensive to retrofit.

This is the same boundary as the shopify module in A2.1. Same reason, different vendor.

The honest recommendation is usually "buy, and stay portable"

Not a compromise — a genuinely better position. You get the vendor's maintenance today and keep the ability to leave. State it as one recommendation rather than as hedging: "buy it, put it behind one module, and make sure we can export our data."

Answer before reading on A client asks whether to build their own click-and-collect system instead of buying one. Run the three questions.

1 · Competitive advantage? No. Customers do not choose a retailer because its collection flow is bespoke. They choose it for stock, price and convenience.

2 · Maintained in two years? Almost certainly not. It would be built by an agency and then owned by nobody — and it touches stock and customer promises, so unowned is dangerous rather than merely untidy.

3 · Is the vendor's version close? Usually yes; this is a mature category.

So: buy. And then the valuable part of your answer — the part the client did not ask for — is the exit: put it behind one module, confirm the data is exportable, and know roughly what replacing it would cost. That turns "buy" from a surrender into a managed position.

Where it hurts

Costs money — data lock-in discovered after signing A vendor holding a balance, a history or a customer relationship you cannot export. You cannot leave at any price. Negotiate exportability before you sign, never after.
Costs money — building something nobody will own Question 2 answered optimistically. The build lands, the team disperses, and in eighteen months it is unmaintained code touching stock and money.
Costs money — buying something 60% right The gap gets filled with custom work around the vendor, which is the worst of both: you pay the licence and own the complexity. If it is not close, building is cheaper.
Fails with no error — the vendor sprinkled through the codebase Their client library called from forty files. Nothing is wrong until you need to leave, and then the migration is an audit of everything. One module, always.
Two systems holding the same money The concrete case from Track 2: a vendor with a credit ledger, plus store credit issued in Shopify. Both real, neither aware of the other, and a customer can spend the same balance twice.
Judgement, not documentation — the vendor's plan gates are theirs, not Shopify's A vendor's own product tiers can require a particular Shopify plan for some features and not others. So "does this need Plus?" can have a different answer per feature, set by the vendor rather than by Shopify. Confirm per feature, in writing, before scoping.

What you say to a client/PM

The recommendation I'd buy it. You could build it — the inventory part isn't the hard bit — but you'd be building the collection flow, the store locator and the staff screens, then maintaining them forever, and customers don't choose you because those are bespoke. Two conditions though: I want it behind one piece of our code so we could swap it, and I want written confirmation you can export your own data whenever you like. A clear recommendation with two conditions that cost nothing and preserve every option. This is what a lead sounds like on a commercial question.
The question they did not ask One thing worth asking them before you sign: if you left in three years, could you take your data with you, and in what format? It's a much easier conversation now than later. Vendors answer it happily pre-sale and much less happily afterwards. Offers a piece of commercial advice a developer is not expected to give, which is exactly why it lands.
When the client wants to build for the wrong reason I'll build it if you want it. The question I'd want answered first is who maintains it in two years — because this touches stock and customer promises, so it can't be left unowned. If there's a name, building is fine. If there isn't, buying is genuinely the safer call even though it feels like less control. Does not refuse. Names the one condition and is honest that buying feels worse and usually is not.

Remember this

  • You are buying someone's ongoing maintenance, not features.
  • Three questions: competitive advantage · maintained in two years · is theirs close enough.
  • Question 2 kills most build decisions, and nobody asks it aloud.
  • Data lock-in is the severe one. Negotiate exportability before signing; engineering cannot fix it later.
  • Integration lock-in is the cheapest. One module, a façade in your vocabulary.
  • 60% right is the worst case — you pay the licence and own the complexity.
  • The usual right answer is "buy, and stay portable". State it as one recommendation.

If you remember one thing: design the exit while you are choosing the entrance — ask how the data comes out before anyone signs.
