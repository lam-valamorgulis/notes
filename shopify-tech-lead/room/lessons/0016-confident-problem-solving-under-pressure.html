Confident problem-solving under pressure

Debugging while someone watches. The method is the confidence.

Your win today. A fixed debugging sequence you narrate out loud — so being watched makes you look methodical instead of making you rush.

How it works

Derive it: what does the watcher actually judge?

Not whether you find it quickly. They cannot tell how hard the bug is. What they can see is whether you have a method or are guessing — and guessing is visible immediately, because a guesser jumps between unrelated hypotheses.

So confidence under pressure is not a personality trait to acquire. It is a visible sequence, and the sequence is what you practise.

1. REPRODUCE "let me make it happen again" 2. NARROW halve the search space. say which half. 3. HYPOTHESIS one, stated out loud, with a test 4. TEST the cheapest test that could disprove it 5. FIX and say what class of bug it was

Narrate every step

Silence while you think looks like being stuck. Narrating turns the same thinking into a demonstration:

StepSay
Reproduce"First I want to see it happen. Can you tell me the exact product and time?"
Narrow"Two possibilities: we sent the wrong value, or we sent the right one and something changed it after. Our log tells us which — so that's the first thing to check."
Hypothesis"My guess is the watermark. If that's right, we'd expect a gap in the records around that timestamp — let's look."
Test"That would rule it out cheaply, so let's do that before the harder check."
Fix"This is the same class as the one last month — a value we trusted that we should have re-read."

Step 2 is the one that reads as senior. Splitting the problem in half out loud shows you are reducing a search space rather than trying things. That single sentence changes how the rest of the session is perceived.

The three sentences that buy you room

Drill these Before I guess, let me check one thing.
I don't know yet. Here's how I'd narrow it down.
Two possibilities. Let me rule out the cheap one first. All three replace a silence or a guess with a visible method. The third is the most useful and the most reusable.

What to do when you are genuinely stuck

DoNot
Say what you have ruled outKeep trying things silently
Ask for the thing you are missing — a log, an example, accessWork around the gap and hope
Say "I'd like twenty minutes on my own with this"Debug badly with an audience
Offer a mitigation while you investigateLeave it broken while you look for the cause

That last row is the tech-lead move. Separating "stop the bleeding" from "find the cause" is a senior instinct, and saying it out loud is worth more than the fix — because it shows you know the client's priority is not your curiosity.

Under real pressure: what you refuse

  • No untested change to production, however small and however urgent it feels.
  • No fix you cannot explain. If you do not know why it works, you do not know what else it did.
  • No skipping the reproduction. A fix for a bug you never reproduced is a guess with a deployment.
  • No debugging at 2am if it can wait until 8am. Mitigate, sleep, fix properly.
Answer before reading on A client is on a call. Stock is wrong on about two hundred products and they want it fixed now. What are your first two sentences?

Something close to: "First, let's stop it getting worse — I'm going to pause the sync so nothing else gets overwritten while we look. Then: can you give me two product codes that are wrong, so I can see it happen rather than guess?"

Two sentences, two moves. Mitigate, then reproduce. Pausing the sync is the "stop the bleeding" step, and it buys you the thing you most need — time to work properly. Asking for two specific examples starts the method and also gently converts the client from a spectator into a participant.

What you did not do: start theorising about the cause on the call. You have no evidence yet, and a wrong theory said out loud to a worried client is expensive to retract.

Where it hurts

Costs money — an untested change under pressure The urgency is real and the change is still untested. This is how a wrong stock number becomes a wrong stock number plus an outage.
Costs money — investigating while it keeps breaking Every minute spent on root cause is a minute of continued damage. Mitigate first — pause the job, roll back, hide the feature — then investigate.
Theorising out loud with no evidence A confident wrong theory told to an anxious client is repeated to their team and then has to be retracted. Say "two possibilities" instead of naming one.
Fails with no error — a fix you cannot explain It stops the symptom and you do not know why. So you do not know what else changed, and the same cause resurfaces somewhere else later, looking unrelated.
Silent thinking with an audience Identical thinking, opposite impression. Narrate, or you look stuck while doing good work.
Judgement, not documentation — you are allowed to ask for the room "Give me twenty minutes on my own and I'll come back with something" is a completely acceptable thing to say, and it produces a better outcome than an hour of watched flailing. Very few people say it.

Say it out loud

Explain this to a beginner

Something is broken in production and people are watching. Explain why "let's stop it getting worse first" is the sentence that marks you as senior.

Say it out loud. Then read on.

Now compare with a plain-words answer

Because everybody's instinct in that moment is to find the cause, and finding the cause takes time during which the damage keeps accumulating.

Turning it off, pausing the sync, switching to a backup — none of that fixes anything, and all of it stops the bleeding. You can then investigate calmly instead of investigating while orders are being lost. Saying it out loud also visibly separates containing from diagnosing, which is exactly the distinction juniors have not made yet.

The other half is that confidence under pressure is not a feeling, it is a visible sequence: reproduce it, narrow it down, state a theory, test the theory, then name what class of problem it was. Doing that in order looks like competence because it is competence.

And you narrate every step. Thinking silently looks identical to being stuck, and in a room of anxious people, looking stuck makes everything worse. "Two possibilities — let me rule out the cheap one first" costs you nothing and tells everyone you are in control.

One discipline: never name a cause before you have evidence. A confident wrong theory sends other people down the wrong path, and taking it back costs far more than the seconds you saved.

If you used any of these words, try again mitigation · incident command · root cause analysis · bisect · blast radius. "Stopping the bleeding is not the same as finding the cause, and it comes first" is the explanation.

Build it

Task · 45 minutes, recorded your Project + a person

Have someone break your Project without telling you what they did.

  1. Ask them to turn on one failure switch in the fake ERP, silently.
  2. Debug it out loud, recorded, in the sequence: mitigate · reproduce · narrow · hypothesis · test · name the class.
  3. Say "let's stop it getting worse first" and actually do something containing before diagnosing.
  4. Narrate every step, including dead ends.
  5. Do not name a cause until you have evidence for it.

Proof it worked: listen back. You contained before you diagnosed, and you never stated a cause you could not yet prove. Any silence longer than about ten seconds is a place to add narration next time.

The sentence bank · 10 minutes a voice recorder

Record four sentences until they are automatic: "let's stop it getting worse first" · "two possibilities, let me rule out the cheap one" · "I can reproduce it" · "I don't have evidence for a cause yet".

Proof it worked: they come out without construction. Under pressure you will not be composing English, so it has to already be there.

Remember this

  • Confidence is a visible sequence: reproduce · narrow · hypothesis · test · name the class.
  • Narrate every step. Silent thinking looks like being stuck.
  • "Two possibilities — let me rule out the cheap one first" is the sentence that reads as senior.
  • Mitigate before you investigate. Stop the bleeding, then find the cause.
  • Never name a cause without evidence. A retracted theory is expensive.
  • No untested production change, no fix you cannot explain, no skipping the reproduction.
  • You are allowed to ask for twenty minutes alone. Almost nobody does.

If you remember one thing: say "let's stop it getting worse first" — that sentence alone marks you out as someone who has done this before.
