Track 3 · R3 · Lesson 1

Buying thinking time, and saying "I don't know yet"

Fixed sentences for the ten seconds when your English deserts you.

Your win today. Six sentences you have said out loud enough times that they arrive without effort — so a hard question buys you thinking time instead of costing you composure.

How it works

Derive it: what actually goes wrong under pressure?

You know the answer. You have read the Lesson. And in the moment, three things happen at once: you translate, you construct a sentence, and you manage the silence. Three tasks, one of which — the sentence — you can remove entirely by knowing it in advance.

That is the whole method. Not "improve your English". Pre-build the sentences for the four moments that recur, drill them out loud until they are automatic, and spend your working memory on the answer instead of on the grammar.

under pressure you are doing THREE things: 1. thinking of the answer ← keep this 2. building the sentence ← REMOVE THIS. pre-built. 3. managing the silence ← the sentence handles it

Moment 1 · You need ten seconds

Silence feels much longer to you than to them. But an unexplained pause reads as "he doesn't know", while a narrated pause reads as care. So say what you are doing:

Drill these out loud, five times each Let me think about that for a second.
Good question. Let me get this right rather than fast.
Can I take that in two parts? All three buy the same ten seconds. The third also gives your answer a structure, which makes the rest easier to say.

Moment 2 · You need the question again

Never guess at a question you did not fully hear. Asking is normal — native speakers do it constantly — and mishearing costs far more.

Drill these Sorry, could you say that again?
Just to make sure I understood — you're asking about X, is that right?
When you say "sync", do you mean both directions or just one? The second one is the strongest: repeating the question in your own words both confirms it and buys you the time to think while you say it.

Moment 3 · You do not know

This is the one people fear and it is the biggest opportunity in the whole Chapter. "I don't know" alone sounds weak. "I don't know yet, and here is how I'd find out" sounds senior. The difference is one clause, and it converts a gap into a demonstration of method.

Drill these — the highest-value sentences here I don't know yet. What I'd do is load a sample at your real volume and measure it — about half a day.
I'd have to check that. I wouldn't want to guess at a number that ends up in a plan.
I don't know that one. I do know where to look — Shopify publishes the limits and I'd check the date on them. Each one refuses to guess and then supplies a method with a cost. Nobody penalises that. Many people are relieved by it.

Moment 4 · You lost the thread mid-sentence

It happens to everyone in a second language and it feels catastrophic. It is not, if you have a way out:

Drill these Let me start that again more simply.
Sorry — the short version is…
I'm overcomplicating it. The point is… All three reset without apologising twice. And "the short version is" almost always produces a better answer than the one you abandoned.

How to drill this

DoNot
Say each sentence out loud, five timesRead them
Practise while doing something else — walking, washing upOnly at a desk with full attention
Record yourself once and listen backAssume it sounds how you think it does
Pick one sentence per moment and own itTry to remember all twelve

That last row matters most. You need one sentence per moment, not twelve. Four sentences, automatic, is worth more than twelve you have to choose between — because choosing is the thing that fails under pressure.

Do this now, out loud Say your one chosen sentence for each of the four moments, from memory, without looking. Which one did you have to think about?

Whichever one you hesitated on is not yet yours. Say that one ten more times today, out loud, while doing something else. The test is not whether you can recall it when you are calm and thinking about it — it is whether it arrives when you are not.

And notice which moment you found hardest to even imagine needing. For most people it is moment 3, because admitting not knowing feels like the risk. It is the opposite: the person who says "I don't know yet, here's how I'd find out" is the person everyone trusts with the unknown parts.

Where it hurts

Costs money — guessing a number to fill a silence It ends the awkward pause and goes straight into a plan. Every later conversation is then about a variance from a figure you invented in a moment of discomfort.
The unexplained pause Four seconds of silence reads as "he doesn't know". The same four seconds after "let me get this right rather than fast" reads as care. Same pause, opposite meaning.
Answering a question you did not hear You answer a different question well. The listener concludes you were not listening, which is worse than the misunderstanding itself. Ask.
Apologising for your English It draws attention to the one thing you want in the background, and it invites the listener to start evaluating your language instead of your answer. Never apologise for it. Just use short sentences.
Fails with no error — twelve sentences instead of one Under pressure, choosing between options is itself the thing that fails. One per moment, automatic.
Judgement, not documentation — short sentences are a strength here A non-native speaker using short, clear sentences often communicates better than a native speaker using long ones — especially to a client. Do not try to sound complex. Simple and definite is the register you want anyway.

What you say to a client/PM

A hard technical question you can answer, but need a moment for Let me take that in two parts. First, whether it's possible — yes. Second, what it costs — that depends on one thing, which is whether their system can tell us what changed since a timestamp. "In two parts" buys the pause and structures the answer. You now have a scaffold instead of an open field.
A number you genuinely do not have I don't know yet, and I don't want to guess — a number I invent today becomes the plan. Give me half a day to load a real sample and I'll have a proper answer. What I can commit to now is that timing. Refuses, gives a method, gives a cost, and commits to something concrete. This is the strongest available answer and it should be automatic.
When you have over-explained and lost the room I'm overcomplicating this. The short version: their system decides prices, Shopify decides stock, and each one only travels one direction. That's it. Resets cleanly and usually lands better than the original attempt. Self-correcting in public reads as confidence, not as weakness.

Remember this

  • Pre-build the sentence so you can spend your attention on the answer.
  • Narrate the pause. "Let me get this right rather than fast" turns silence into care.
  • Repeat the question back — it confirms it and buys thinking time at the same time.
  • "I don't know yet" plus a method and a cost is the strongest answer in this Lesson.
  • "The short version is…" when you lose the thread. It usually improves the answer.
  • Never apologise for your English. Use short sentences instead.
  • One sentence per moment, drilled out loud. Choosing is what fails under pressure.

If you remember one thing: "I don't know yet — here's how I'd find out" is a senior answer, and it needs to arrive without you thinking about it.
