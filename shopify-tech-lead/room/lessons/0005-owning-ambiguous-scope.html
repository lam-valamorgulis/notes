Owning ambiguous scope

The brief is three sentences. What you ask, and what you decide without asking.

Your win today. A rule for sorting unknowns into "ask" and "decide" — so you stop either blocking on questions or guessing on things that mattered.

How it works

Derive it: what makes an unknown worth a question?

Every ambiguity has two properties: how expensive it is to get wrong, and how cheap it is to change later. That gives four situations, and only one of them deserves a question.

cheap to change expensive to change ┌────────────────────────┬────────────────────────┐ low cost │ DECIDE │ DECIDE, and say so │ if wrong │ do not mention it │ one line in a note │ ├────────────────────────┼────────────────────────┤ high cost │ DECIDE, flag it │ ★ ASK ★ │ if wrong │ "assumed X, easy to │ this is the only │ │ change if wrong" │ blocking question │ └────────────────────────┴────────────────────────┘

So the discipline is: find the top-right and bottom-right, ask about the bottom-right, state assumptions for everything else, and get on with it.

Feynman version: only stop and ask when being wrong would be both costly and hard to undo. Everything else is a decision you are being paid to make.

The two failure modes, and both are common

FailureLooks likeWhy it happens
BlockingTwelve questions before starting. Nothing moves for a weekTreating every unknown as equal, and fear of being wrong
GuessingTwo weeks of work in the wrong direction, discovered at reviewNot wanting to look uncertain, or not noticing the unknown was load-bearing

Blocking feels responsible and reads as helpless. Guessing feels decisive and reads as careless. The middle — decide, state the assumption, keep moving — is what a lead does, and it is a habit rather than a talent.

The three questions that resolve most Shopify ambiguity

For integration work specifically, these are almost always in the bottom-right box:

QuestionWhy it is load-bearing
Who decides this field when both systems disagree?Wrong answer means overwriting real data. Expensive and hard to undo
Is this number sellable stock or everything on the shelf?Wrong answer means overselling. Directly costs money
What happens when the other system is down?Undecided means the code decides by accident, usually by failing

Three questions, all answerable by a non-engineer, all cheap to ask. If you only ever ask these on a new integration, you will be right more often than someone who asks twenty.

How to state an assumption so it actually gets read

WeakStrong
"Assumed the ERP owns pricing.""I've assumed your ERP decides prices and Shopify follows. If your team ever edits a price directly in Shopify, tell me — it'd be overwritten overnight."
"Assumed daily sync.""I've assumed once a day is fine for product details. Stock will be within minutes. Say if either needs to be different."

The difference is that the strong version names the consequence if the assumption is wrong. Nobody reads a list of assumptions. Everybody reads "it'd be overwritten overnight".

Answer before reading on A ticket says: "Sync products from the ERP to Shopify." Name one thing you would ask and two you would decide.

Ask: which fields does each side own — specifically, does anyone edit product details directly in Shopify? That is expensive if wrong (you overwrite their work) and hard to undo (the old values are gone). It is the one genuinely blocking question.

Decide: the sync interval — start with something sensible and say so, because it is trivial to change. And the identifier — use their product code, note it, move on. Both are cheap to revise, so asking would slow the work down for no benefit.

The thing to notice: "sync products" is four words that contains one real question and about fifteen decisions. Finding which is the skill.

Where it hurts

Costs money — guessing on ownership You assume the ERP owns everything, build it, and the first run overwrites the marketing team's hand-written descriptions. Their work is gone and there is no undo. This is the classic instance of a bottom-right question treated as a decision.
Costs money — blocking for a week Twelve questions sent, nothing built, and the client concludes you cannot work independently. Every question you did not need to ask spent trust you will want later.
Fails with no error — an assumption stated with no consequence Buried in a list, nobody reads it, and it was wrong. You did technically flag it, which will not help anyone. Name what happens if it is wrong, or you did not really flag it.
Asking in a channel nobody reads A blocking question in a group chat at 5pm on Friday is not a question, it is a hope. If it genuinely blocks you, it needs a named person and a stated deadline.
Judgement, not documentation — some ambiguity is a business disagreement Sometimes nobody has decided who owns a field because two teams want different things, and your question exposes that. Do not resolve it in code. Say plainly that it is a decision between two people and that you will hold until they make it — that is escalating, not blocking.

What you say to a client/PM

Asking the one blocking question One thing I need before I start, because it's expensive to get wrong. Does anyone on your team edit product titles or descriptions directly in Shopify? If they do, and I set this up with the ERP as the source, their edits would get overwritten every night. Everything else I can make a sensible call on and tell you what I chose. One question, the consequence spelled out, and an explicit promise to decide the rest yourself. That last clause is what makes the question welcome rather than annoying.
Stating decisions, at the start Three calls I've made so I could get moving. Product details sync once a day, stock every few minutes. I'm matching products on your ERP product code. And if your ERP is unreachable we hold the last known values rather than blanking anything. All three are easy to change — flag any that don't match how you work. Shows initiative and stays correctable. Three specific decisions with an open door reads as ownership; asking about all three reads as needing supervision.
When the ambiguity is really a disagreement between two people I've hit something I shouldn't decide. Your buying team wants prices controlled in the ERP; the marketing team is currently setting promotional prices in Shopify. Both are reasonable, and whichever we pick means the other loses something. I'll keep working on the parts that don't depend on it — can the two of them agree by Thursday? Refuses to arbitrate a business decision in code, keeps working, and asks for a dated resolution. This is the highest-value sentence in the Lesson.

Remember this

  • Sort unknowns by cost-if-wrong and cost-to-change. Only expensive-and-irreversible earns a question.
  • Blocking reads as helpless; guessing reads as careless. Decide, state, move.
  • Three load-bearing questions: who owns each field · sellable or shelf stock · what happens when they are down.
  • State assumptions with their consequence. Nobody reads a list; everybody reads "overwritten overnight".
  • A blocking question needs a named person and a date. A group chat is not a question.
  • Do not arbitrate a business disagreement in code. Name it and hold that part.
  • Announce the decisions you made, briefly, so ownership is visible and still correctable.

If you remember one thing: ask only when being wrong is both costly and permanent — everything else is what you are paid to decide.

Ask questions. Then "next" for estimates.
