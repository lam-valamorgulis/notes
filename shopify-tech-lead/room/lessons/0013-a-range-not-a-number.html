Track 3 · R3 · Lesson 3

A range, not a number

The sentence that gives an honest estimate without giving a promise.

Your win today. One sentence pattern you can produce under pressure that satisfies someone who wants a number, without you inventing one.

How it works

Derive it: why does a range annoy people?

Because a bare range gives them nothing to do. "Three to eight days" cannot go in a plan, cannot be argued with, and cannot be shortened. So it reads as evasion even when it is honest.

The fix is not to narrow the range. It is to add the two things that make a range useful: why it is wide, and when it stops being wide.

the four-part sentence 1. the LOW end, with its condition "three days if X" 2. the HIGH end, with its condition "eight if not X" 3. what X is one specific, checkable thing 4. when you will KNOW "within an hour of seeing their docs" ── now they can act: they can go and get X.

Part 4 is what turns it from a hedge into a plan. A range that collapses on a named date is something a PM can put in a schedule.

The pattern, and three fillings

Drill this shape until it is automatic [LOW] if [CONDITION]. [HIGH] if not, because [CONSEQUENCE]. I'll know [WHEN]. One shape, four slots. Learn the shape and you can fill it for anything, which is far easier than remembering example sentences.
SituationThe sentence
An unknown API"Three days if their API can tell me what changed since a timestamp. Eight if it can't, because then I compare full lists every night. I'll know within an hour of seeing their docs."
An unknown volume"A week if the catalogue is under about fifty thousand variants. Three if it's much bigger, because the load itself becomes the project. I'll know today — I just need read access to count."
An unknown decision"Two days if you're happy for the ERP to own descriptions. A week if your team needs to keep editing them in Shopify, because then we need a field-level merge. I'll know as soon as you've decided."

Notice the third one. The condition is a decision the client owns, so the sentence quietly makes their own delay visible without accusing anyone of anything.

When they insist on one number

Some people cannot use a range, and that is a legitimate constraint rather than stubbornness. Give them a number about something you control:

Drill this I'll give you a number on Thursday. To get it I need their API docs and half a day to test. What I can commit to today is the Thursday date. A firm commitment, just not the one they asked for. A PM needs something for the plan, and a date for a number is something.

If they still insist, give the high end as the number and say so explicitly: "then I'd have to say eight days, because I have to quote the case where the unknown goes badly." That is honest, and it usually makes them want the range back.

Two habits that protect the range afterwards

HabitWhy
Say the unit. "Three working days, not calendar days"Half of all estimate arguments are this
Re-state the range when it collapses. "It's the eight-day path — the unknown resolved the expensive way"Ties the bad news to a risk you already named, which makes it information rather than a failure
Do this now, out loud A client asks how long it takes to migrate their 60,000 products from another platform. You know almost nothing yet. Say the four-part sentence.

Something close to: "Around three weeks if the product data is clean and everything has a stable product code. Six or more if a lot of the codes are missing or duplicated, because then matching becomes the bulk of the work rather than the loading. I'll know in two days — I need read access to both systems to measure the match rate."

Check your version against the four slots. Did you give both ends? Did you name one specific condition rather than "it depends on the data"? Did you say when you would know, and what you need to know it?

The most common miss is slot 4. Without it the sentence is still a hedge — with it, you have given the client a two-day task that shortens their own project, which is a much better meeting.

Where it hurts

Costs money — a single number given under pressure It becomes the baseline you are measured against for the rest of the project. Every conversation after it is about a variance from a figure you knew was a guess.
A range with no reason "Three to eight days" reads as padding and invites negotiation on the number rather than on the unknown. The spread must have one named cause.
A range that never collapses If you cannot say when you will know, you have not identified the unknown precisely enough. Go back and find the specific thing.
"It depends on the data" Technically true and useless. Depends on what about the data? Name the measurable property — match rate, blank rate, variant count.
Fails with no error — working days versus calendar days You meant one, they heard the other, and nobody discovers it until the deadline. Say the unit every time.
Judgement, not documentation — the range is not a negotiation opener Do not inflate the high end expecting to be pushed down. Clients learn that quickly, and then they discount everything you say. The range should be your actual belief, which is what makes it defensible when it turns out to be right.

Say it out loud

Explain this to a beginner

A bare range like "three to eight weeks" sounds evasive. Explain the two things that turn it into something useful.

Say it out loud. Then read on.

Now compare with a plain-words answer

The reason it is that wide, and when it stops being that wide.

"Three to eight weeks" tells the listener nothing except that you are unsure, so they treat the spread as padding and start negotiating it down. "Three weeks if their export includes deleted rows, eight if it does not, and one hour with their developer tells us which — I can have that by Thursday" is not negotiable, because there is nothing to argue with. There is only something to do.

The collapse date is the part almost everybody leaves out, and it is what turns a hedge into a plan. Without it, a range is a statement about your feelings. With it, it is a schedule with one open question on it.

If they insist on a single number: do not invent one. Commit to a date for the number instead — that is a real commitment and it is honest. If you are genuinely forced, quote the high end and say why. Most people ask for the range back at that point, which is the outcome you wanted anyway.

One thing to never say: "it depends on the data". Everyone says it and it means nothing. Name the specific measurable property it depends on.

If you used any of these words, try again confidence interval · padding · buffer · unknown unknowns · T-shirt sizing. "The reason it is wide and when it stops being wide" is the explanation.

Build it

Task · 30 minutes, written then recorded a real estimate
  1. Take a real estimate and write the four slots: low with its condition · high with its condition · what the condition actually is · when you will know.
  2. Replace any "it depends on the data" with a named measurable property — match rate, blank rate, whether deletions appear.
  3. Record yourself delivering it in under 30 seconds.
  4. Then record the version for when they insist on one number: commit to a date for the number.

Proof it worked: your range has a date on which it collapses, and someone could act on it this week. If your reason is a feeling rather than a measurable property, rewrite it.

The forced-number drill · 10 minutes a voice recorder

Have someone press you three times for a single number. Hold the range twice, then give the high end with its reason.

Proof it worked: you never invented a middle number to end the conversation. That invented middle number is the one that gets remembered as a promise.

Remember this

  • Four slots: low with its condition · high with its condition · what the condition is · when you will know.
  • A bare range reads as evasion. The reason and the collapse date are what make it useful.
  • When they insist on a number, commit to a date for the number.
  • If truly forced, quote the high end and say why. They usually want the range back.
  • Never say "it depends on the data". Name the measurable property.
  • Say the unit — working days, not calendar days.
  • Do not inflate the high end. The range must be your real belief.

If you remember one thing: always say when the range collapses — that is what turns a hedge into a plan.
