The design conversation: whiteboard for a client, not a grader

A grader wants to see what you know. A client wants to make a decision. Those need different answers.

Your win today. You can tell within thirty seconds whether you are being assessed or being consulted — and change what you say accordingly.

How it works

Derive it: what does the listener need from this?

The same design, described to two people, should not sound the same. Ask what each of them will do with what you say.

A grader (interviewer, senior engineer)A client or PM
WantsEvidence you know the trade-offsEnough to decide and to trust you
ValuesDepth, rigour, edge casesClarity, cost, risk
Reaction to detailMore is betterMore is worse
Reaction to "it depends"Good, if you name what it depends onFrustrating, unless you also give a default
Wants to hear about failureYes, in mechanismYes, in consequence and mitigation

The row that changes your behaviour most is the third. With a grader, adding detail scores points. With a client, every unnecessary detail is a small withdrawal from their attention and their confidence. Same knowledge, opposite instinct.

Tell which one you are in

signals you are being GRADED signals you are being CONSULTED "how would you handle…" "what do you recommend?" "what if it were 100× bigger?" "how long, and how much?" follow-ups go deeper follow-ups go wider they already know the answer they are deciding something

And the practical mixed case: a client meeting with their technical lead present contains both. Aim your answer at the decision-maker and offer the depth as an aside — "happy to go deeper on the retry logic if that's useful". That sentence serves both listeners in nine words.

The client version: three things, then stop

SayExample
What it does, in their words"Your ERP decides prices; Shopify decides stock"
What it costs — time, money, or a constraint"Stock will be a minute or two behind, not instant"
What could go wrong, and what we do"If their system is down, prices hold at the last known good ones"

Then stop. Silence after three clear points is not awkward — it is the space in which they ask the question they actually care about, which is more useful than anything you would have said next.

What to leave out on purpose

Naming these makes the omission a choice rather than a gap:

  • Technology names. "A queue" not "BullMQ on Redis". The name conveys nothing and invites a question you do not want to spend the meeting on.
  • Your table structure. Nobody is deciding anything based on it.
  • How clever the hard part was. This is the hardest one to resist and the least useful thing you can say.
  • Failure modes with no consequence for them. Mention the ones that change what they might decide.

Drawing for a client

Boxes with their nouns in them. Arrows with verbs, not protocols — "sends prices", not "POST /products". And one arrow at a time, narrating as you draw, so the picture assembles at the speed they can follow rather than appearing complete and unreadable.

Answer before reading on Mid-explanation, the client asks: "why can't it just be instant?" What is the wrong answer, and what is the right one?

The wrong answer is the true one delivered as a mechanism — webhook delivery guarantees, retry windows, eventual consistency. All correct, all irrelevant to their decision, and it converts a question into a lecture.

The right answer is a consequence plus a choice: "It'll usually be a few seconds. I won't promise instant because Shopify's own guidance is that a notification can arrive late, and I'd rather build something that's definitely right than something that's usually instant. If genuinely-instant matters for a specific thing, tell me which, and I'll design that part differently."

That answer gives them the number, the reason, and a way to push back if their need is real. The mechanism is available if they ask — and now it is their choice to go there.

Where it hurts

Answering a client like a grader Technically excellent and unusable. They cannot decide anything from it, so they defer, and the meeting produces nothing. Depth is generous with a grader and costly with a client.
"It depends" with no default Honest, and it hands your job back to them. Always follow it with what you would do and what would change your mind.
Fails with no error — the polite nod You went too deep, they stopped following, and they will not say so. The meeting ends warmly and no decision was made. Only checkpoints catch this.
Naming your technology choices Every product name is either meaningless to them or triggers a question about something they read once. Say what it does.
Describing how hard it was It reads as either complaining or fishing, and it never helps a decision. If effort is relevant, it is relevant as a number in an estimate.
Judgement, not documentation — do not win the meeting You can be right and lose the client's confidence. If someone proposes something worse, the goal is the better outcome, not the visible correction. "That could work — the thing I'd worry about is X" gets there and leaves them intact.

What you say to a client/PM

Serving both audiences at once At a high level: your ERP owns prices, Shopify owns stock, and we move each one direction only so they can't fight. That's the whole design. Happy to go deeper on how we handle their system being temporarily down, if that's useful. Complete for the decision-maker, with an explicit door for the technical person. Nobody is bored and nobody is short-changed.
When you genuinely do not know I don't know yet, and I don't want to guess at it. What I'd do is load a sample at your real volume and measure — that's about half a day and it turns a guess into a number. Can I do that before we commit to a date? "I don't know" plus a concrete plan and a cost reads as rigour. "I don't know" alone reads as unpreparedness. The difference is the next sentence.
When someone proposes a worse design That could work. The thing I'd want to check is what happens when two people edit the same product in both systems on the same day — with that approach I think we'd get a flicker. Want me to try it and see, or shall we design around it now? Does not reject, names one specific consequence, and offers two ways forward. You get the better outcome without anyone being corrected.

Remember this

  • A grader wants evidence. A client wants a decision. Same knowledge, opposite instinct on detail.
  • Tell which room you are in from whether follow-ups go deeper or wider.
  • For a client: what it does, what it costs, what could go wrong — then stop.
  • Silence after three points is where their real question arrives.
  • Leave out technology names, table structures, and how hard it was.
  • Never say "it depends" without a default and what would change it.
  • Do not win the meeting. Aim for the better outcome, not the visible correction.

If you remember one thing: with a client, every extra detail costs you something — say three things and stop talking.
