Disagreeing upward, and the senior narrative

How to push back on someone above you — and how to tell your own six years in a way that lands.

Your win today. A shape for disagreeing that leaves the other person's authority intact, and a way to describe your own experience that is specific instead of a list of technologies.

How it works

Part 1 · Disagreeing upward

Derive it from what the other person needs. A PM or a senior who is wrong does not need to be corrected — they need the information they were missing, delivered in a way that lets them change their mind without losing face. Those are different goals, and only the second one works.

1. AGREE with the goal "I want that too" 2. ONE specific concern not three. one, and concrete 3. HAND BACK the decision "your call — here's what I'd do" ── you have not corrected anyone. you have added information.
Drill this shape I want that too. The one thing I'd check first is [specific concern], because [specific consequence]. Your call — I'd [recommendation]. Three clauses. The first removes the conflict, the second is the actual content, the third returns the authority. Almost nobody digs in against this.

Why one concern, not three

Three concerns reads as resistance and invites a defence of the whole idea. One specific concern reads as diligence and invites a fix. So pick your strongest single point — usually the one with money or an irreversible consequence attached — and hold the rest.

If the one concern is answered well, you were wrong about that one and you should say so. That is what makes the technique credible the next time.

When you are overruled

Drill this — it matters more than the disagreement Understood — I'll build it that way. I've noted the concern about overselling so we know where to look if it comes up. Accepts the decision, records the position once, and stops. Repeating an objection after a decision turns a reasonable engineer into an obstruction.

The rule: say it once, record it once, then commit fully. Half-hearted execution of a decision you lost is the worst available outcome — it damages the work and your credibility at the same time.

The one exception

If the decision is genuinely unsafe — money, customer data, something irreversible — you escalate rather than accept. And you say exactly that:

Rare, and it needs to be automatic I'm not comfortable making this call at my level. It affects customer data and it can't be undone, so I'd like it confirmed by whoever owns that risk. I'll keep working on everything else meanwhile. Not a refusal and not insubordination — a request for the decision to be made at the right level. And it keeps you working, which is what stops it looking like a protest.

Part 2 · The senior narrative

Now the other half. Asked "tell me about your experience", most people list technologies. That answers nothing, because everyone lists the same technologies.

WeakStrong
"Six years, mostly Shopify. Liquid, React, some Node.""Six years on Shopify. Three of those deep in themes — I know where the storefront hurts. Then front end, then increasingly the integration side, which is what I'm doing now."

The strong version has a direction. It is a story with a trajectory, and it makes the next role the obvious next step rather than a jump.

The three-sentence shape

Build yours, then drill it [Where I started, and what it taught me.] [What I moved toward, and why.] [What I'm doing now, and the gap I'm closing.] Three sentences, thirty seconds, and it ends on growth rather than on a claim. Ending on what you are still learning is stronger than ending on mastery.

Talking about a gap honestly

This is the part that matters most, and it has its own Lesson in R5. The short version: a named gap with a plan attached is a strength; a hidden gap is a risk.

Drill this What I haven't done is run a live sync in production. So I've been building one — a real service against a real store, with the failure handling and a reconciliation report — because I'd rather have hit those problems on my own time than on yours. Names the gap without flinching, then shows the response. That sentence is only available to someone who actually did the work, which is why it lands.
Do this now, out loud A PM says "let's skip the reconciliation report for now, we're tight on time." Disagree upward in three clauses.

Something close to: "I want to hit the date too. The one thing I'd worry about is that without the report we'd have no way to know if the sync is quietly wrong — a sync doing nothing looks exactly like a sync working. Your call, but I'd keep a cut-down version, maybe half a day, that just compares prices and stock."

Check it against the three clauses. Did you agree with the goal first? Was there exactly one concern? Did you hand the decision back with a recommendation?

And notice the recommendation is not "keep it as scoped" — it is a smaller version. Offering a cheaper form of your own position is what makes disagreeing upward work, because it gives the other person a way to agree with you that also respects their constraint.

Where it hurts

Three concerns instead of one Reads as resistance to the whole idea and triggers a defence. One specific concern, with money or irreversibility attached, invites a fix.
Costs money — repeating the objection after a decision A reasonable position becomes obstruction, and people stop bringing you into decisions. Say it once, record it once, commit fully.
Costs money — half-hearted execution of a decision you lost The work suffers and it is visibly your work. You lose twice. Commit properly or escalate; there is no third option.
Fails with no error — silent disagreement You thought it was wrong, said nothing, and it went wrong. Nobody knows you knew, so you get none of the credit for the judgement and all of the delivery. This is the most common failure here.
A narrative that is a technology list Everyone lists the same technologies. Without a direction it says nothing about you, and it makes your next step look like a jump rather than a progression.
Judgement, not documentation — do not stockpile "I told you so" If your recorded concern turns out to be right, the useful move is to fix it without mentioning that you called it. The record exists so the fix is fast, not so you can be vindicated. People remember which one you used it for.

Say it out loud

Explain this to a beginner

You think your manager's plan is wrong. Explain the three-clause shape that lets you say so — and why you raise one concern rather than three.

Say it out loud. Then read on.

Now compare with a plain-words answer

The shape: agree with the goal, then one specific concern, then hand the decision back.

Agreeing with the goal first matters because otherwise the disagreement sounds like you want a different outcome. You do not — you want the same outcome and you think this route will not get there. Saying that explicitly changes it from a conflict into help.

Handing the decision back matters because it is theirs. You are giving them information they did not have, not taking the decision from them.

Why one concern: three concerns is not three times as persuasive, it is a list. Lists get skimmed, and the reader picks the weakest item to argue with. One concern has to be dealt with. So pick the one with money or irreversibility attached, and leave the others.

Then a move that makes it much easier for them to agree: offer a cheaper version of your own position. Not "do it my way" but "could we spend one day checking the thing I am worried about?" That gives them a way to take your concern seriously without abandoning their plan.

And if you are overruled: say it once, write it down once, then commit fully. Half-executing a decision you disagreed with is the worst available outcome — it fails, and it fails in a way that looks like you were right for the wrong reason.

If you used any of these words, try again disagree and commit · escalation · risk register · stakeholder buy-in. "Three concerns is a list, and lists get skimmed" is the explanation.

Build it

Task · 30 minutes, written then recorded a real disagreement
  1. Take a real disagreement. Write down all your concerns.
  2. Pick the one with money or irreversibility attached. Delete the rest.
  3. Write it in three clauses: agree with the goal · the one concern · hand the decision back.
  4. Add the cheaper version of your position — the one-day check rather than the full change.
  5. Record it. It should be under 40 seconds.

Proof it worked: it is one concern, not a list, and it ends with a decision for them rather than a demand. And your cheap version is something they could say yes to without losing face.

The overruled drill · 15 minutes, written no store needed

Write the record you would keep if overruled: what you said, what was decided, the date. One paragraph. Then write the sentence in which you commit fully.

Proof it worked: the record is factual, with no "I told you so" in it, and your commitment sentence has no reservations attached. Escalate only for unsafe and irreversible — and keep working while you do.

Remember this

  • Three clauses: agree with the goal · one specific concern · hand back the decision.
  • One concern, not three. Pick the one with money or irreversibility attached.
  • Offer a cheaper version of your own position. It gives them a way to agree.
  • Overruled: say it once, record it once, commit fully. Never half-execute.
  • Escalate only for unsafe and irreversible — and keep working while you do.
  • A narrative needs a direction, not a technology list. Three sentences, ending on growth.
  • A named gap with a plan is a strength. A hidden one is a risk.

If you remember one thing: agree with the goal before you disagree with the plan — and offer a smaller version of what you wanted.

Ask questions. Chapter R3 done — these are the sentences to drill out loud, not read.
