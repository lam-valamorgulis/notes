The territory map

Everything Shopify work contains — what is core, what hurts, and what this Course does not yet teach.

What this page is for. The Scars pages collect the traps in what the Course does teach. This page is the opposite: the whole territory, including the parts still missing. It is deliberately uncomfortable to read.

First: why does Shopify work hurt at all?

Not because the platform is bad. Because five structural forces act on every piece of it, and every pain point in this trade traces back to one of them. Learn the five and you can predict where a new feature will hurt before you have touched it.

1. YOU DO NOT OWN THE PLATFORM it changes every quarter, and it can simply say no 2. MONEY MOVES THROUGH IT mistakes are financial, not cosmetic. and often silent 3. THE CUSTOMER IS PRESENT some of your code runs while a person waits to buy 4. THERE IS ALWAYS A SECOND SYSTEM and a third. each one adds a copy of a fact 5. THE MERCHANT IS A USER TOO they edit things you did not expect, in the admin, tonight
ForceWhat it producesThe reflex it should give you
1 · Not yoursQuarterly versions · deprecations · plan gates · invitation-only platforms · limits that moveDate every fact. Pin every version. Never quote a limit from memory
2 · MoneyOverselling · wrong prices · double ledgers · misstated historyCompare values, not counts. Never recompute what the platform calculated
3 · Customer presentLatency budgets · no retries · fail-open decisions · abandoned carts you never seeDecide fail-open or fail-closed before you build. Never retry inside checkout
4 · Second systemOwnership conflicts · staleness · identity mapping · reconciliationAsk "who decides this field?" for every field
5 · Merchant editsOverwritten work · flickering values · invisible Flow automations · handles that changeRecord what you sent. Never match on anything a human edits

The Feynman version: you are writing code inside somebody else's shop, about somebody else's money, while a customer waits, against a system you do not control, and a person may change it behind you tonight. Every hard thing follows from that sentence.

Not covered — the honest list

This page has now been rebuilt twice. The first version named fourteen gaps; all fourteen were filled. The second named nine; all nine were filled on 2026-08-23 and have moved into the table above. So the list below is the third pass, and it was built the same way: by searching every page in the Course for each candidate area and keeping only the ones with no real coverage.

One of these is a deliberate refusal, not an oversight Managed Markets was scheduled to be written and was not, because the research did not confirm enough to teach it honestly. It stays on this list rather than becoming a Lesson built on assumptions. The slot it would have taken went to P7.4 instead, which was verifiable and turned out to be a live change to what the Course already taught.

The pattern held a third time Round one's top gap was payments. Round two's were fulfilment orders and bundles. Round three's are the remaining Function APIs and discount allocation. Look at what all five have in common: every one is money being recomputed while a customer waits. That is force 2 meeting force 3, and it has now been the answer three times running. When you meet a Shopify feature you have never used, ask whether money moves and whether somebody is waiting. If both, that is where it will hurt.

How to read the pain column

Pain is not difficulty. Plenty of hard things are low pain because they fail loudly. Pain is frequency × cost × invisibility, and the third term is the one people underrate.

PainSignatureExamples from this Course
Very highFrequent, expensive, and silentOverselling from on_hand vs available · the omitted line price on an imported order · a watermark set to now()
HighFrequent and expensive, but detectableRate limits · a deleted webhook subscription · a Plus gate discovered mid-scope
MediumAnnoying, recoverableA quarterly version bump · a missing metafield definition
LowTedious, obvious when wrongContent migration volume · CLI ergonomics

Every "very high" row in this Course is silent. That is the pattern worth internalising: the failures that cost the most are the ones that produce no error, which is exactly why reconciliation is the only thing that measures correctness.

Being honest about the gaps is the point A course claiming to cover everything would be lying, and you would find out in a client meeting. Seven named gaps you can see are worth more than a comfortable sense of completeness — and R5.1 is the Lesson on saying exactly that out loud.
Want a gap filled? Say "teach me the validation Functions", "teach me discount allocation", "teach me POS hardware" — or work down the list in order. Each one becomes a Chapter, researched and dated the same way as the rest.
