Scars — The Room

The conversation traps. Read before a client call or an interview.

Explaining a system

Starting with the database Concrete, so it feels safe. But the schema is a consequence of decisions the listener has not heard, so you are giving answers before questions. → R1.1
The uninterrupted monologue Four minutes, no checkpoint. They lost you at minute one and are too polite to say. The meeting ends warmly and nothing was communicated. Check in every ninety seconds and offer to go back.
Only the happy path Sounds complete, reads as inexperienced. The listener's unease has no name, so it becomes general lack of confidence in you rather than a question you could answer.
Renaming their domain They say "styles", you say "product variants". Every sentence now needs translation and some will be wrong. Use their nouns.
Answering a client like a grader Detail is generous with an interviewer and costly with a client. Same knowledge, opposite instinct. → R1.2
Naming your technology choices "A queue", not "BullMQ on Redis". The product name is either meaningless or triggers a question you did not want to spend the meeting on.
Winning the meeting You can be right and lose the client's confidence. Aim for the better outcome, not the visible correction: "that could work — the thing I'd worry about is X".

Scope and estimates

Guessing on field ownership You assume the ERP owns everything and the first run deletes the marketing team's descriptions. No undo. This is the one genuinely blocking question. → R2.1
Blocking for a week on twelve questions Nothing built, and the client concludes you cannot work independently. Every unnecessary question spends trust you will want later.
An assumption stated with no consequence Buried in a list, unread, and wrong. Name what happens if it is wrong or you did not really flag it.
A number invented to end a conversation It becomes the baseline you are measured against forever. Every later conversation is a variance from a figure you knew was fiction. → R2.2
Omitting the client's part of the timeline Credentials, decisions and vendor replies are often the longest waits. Absent from the estimate, every delay looks like yours.
Estimating only the happy path Failure handling and verification are a third to a half of integration work. Left out, the project is "nearly done" for a very long time.
Working days versus calendar days You meant one, they heard the other, and nobody finds out until the deadline. Say the unit every time.
Splitting the day between two projects Integration state is large; switching costs are real. Two half-days produce less than one whole day, and the loss is invisible on a timesheet.
Re-estimating late A number revised at week two is information. The same number at week six is a broken promise, for identical work.

Getting a reply

The buried ask Four paragraphs of context with a question in the middle. It reads as an update and gets no reply. Ask first, context last. → R2.3
Two decisions in one message You get one answer and assume the other was agreed. It was not read. One decision per message.
The destructive default If you must default, default to the reversible direction. Leaving data alone can be undone; overwriting cannot.
No weekly note The client has no idea what is happening, so your first urgent message in three weeks arrives cold. Regular contact is what makes an urgent request land.
A blocker mentioned once Raised in week one, dropped, and by week six the delay is yours. Repeat it every week, dated. Repetition is the mechanism.

Saying no, and disagreeing

Complying silently with something unwise You build the thing you know will oversell because saying so felt confrontational. The consequence is now partly yours, because you knew. → R3.2
"It's not best practice" An appeal to an authority the client does not recognise. Sounds like preference. Name the specific failure.
A bare no with nothing after it The conversation ends with them wanting something and you blocking it. Always follow no with "what I can do is".
Saying yes to everything Every yes is a silent no to something else. "Where would you like those three days to come from?" makes the trade visible.
Three concerns instead of one Reads as resistance and triggers a defence of the whole idea. One specific concern, with money or irreversibility attached. → R3.4
Repeating an objection after a decision A reasonable position becomes obstruction and people stop including you. Say it once, record it once, commit fully.
Half-hearted execution of a decision you lost The work suffers and it is visibly your work. Commit properly or escalate — there is no third option.
Silent disagreement You thought it was wrong, said nothing, it went wrong. Nobody knows you knew, so you get the delivery and none of the credit for the judgement.
Stockpiling "I told you so" If your recorded concern proves right, fix it without mentioning you called it. The record exists so the fix is fast. People remember which one you used it for.

Speaking under pressure

Guessing a number to fill a silence It ends the awkward pause and goes straight into the plan. → R3.1
The unexplained pause Four seconds of silence reads as "he doesn't know". The same four seconds after "let me get this right rather than fast" reads as care.
Apologising for your English It moves attention to the one thing you want in the background. Never apologise — use short sentences instead.
Twelve sentences instead of one Under pressure, choosing is what fails. One sentence per moment, drilled out loud.
A bare range "Three to eight days" reads as padding. It needs one named cause and a date when it collapses. → R3.3
"It depends on the data" True and useless. Depends on what about the data — match rate, blank rate, variant count?
Inflating the high end Clients learn it quickly and then discount everything you say. The range must be your actual belief.

Review, mentoring, incidents

No severity on a blocking comment A real bug worded as a suggestion gets merged. The author read your tone correctly. → R4.1
Citing a principle instead of a consequence "This isn't idempotent" requires them to already know why it matters. "A retry would double the stock" does not.
The same comment three times You are patching a systemic gap by hand forever. It belongs in a rule, a lint check or a document.
Explaining why you did it wrong When receiving review, justification reads as defensiveness. "Good catch, I'll fix it" is stronger than a correct explanation.
Investigating while it keeps breaking Every minute on root cause is a minute of continued damage. Mitigate first — pause the job, roll back, hide the feature. → R4.2
An untested change under pressure This is how a wrong stock number becomes a wrong stock number plus an outage.
Theorising out loud with no evidence A confident wrong theory gets repeated to their team and then retracted. Say "two possibilities" instead.
Silent thinking with an audience Identical thinking, opposite impression. Narrate the method or you look stuck while doing good work.

Interviews

The vague yes about experience you lack It survives one question and fails the second — and then every other answer is re-examined. A hedge costs more than the gap it hid. → R5.1
"No" with nothing after it You confirmed the concern and offered nothing to weigh against it.
Blaming circumstances "I never got the opportunity" moves it outside your control — the opposite of the signal you want.
A generic "what I did about it" "I've been reading about it" produces no evidence. The specificity is the proof.
Leading with platform knowledge Table stakes. Everyone read the same documentation. It wins a screen and nothing after it. → R5.2
A confident undated number Platform limits move. A limit with no date, said confidently, quietly shows you do not know they move.
Answering the words, not the probe "How long would it take?" answered as a duration, when it was testing estimating honesty. You were accurate and failed, and nobody tells you.
A perfect mock score with nothing built The mock scores what you can say. It cannot score what you have run. That combination is exactly the position that lost an interview once.
